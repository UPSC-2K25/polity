Fundamental Rights

Fundamental Rights are constitutionally guaranteed protections that secure individual liberty, equality, dignity and freedom against arbitrary State action. They establish essential conditions for democratic government and the rule of law.

They are contained in Part III of the Constitution, covering Articles 12 to 35. Most are enforceable through constitutional courts. Article 32 guarantees the right to approach the Supreme Court for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights, while Article 226 gives High Courts a wider writ jurisdiction.

Fundamental Rights are not absolute. Their exercise is subject to constitutionally specified restrictions and limitations. Their interpretation has evolved through judicial decisions, legislation and constitutional amendments.

1. Constitutional Philosophy and Importance

Fundamental Rights embody the constitutional commitment to limiting governmental power and protecting the conditions necessary for individual development. They reflect the influence of liberal constitutionalism, the freedom movement and the experience of colonial restrictions.

Why are Fundamental Rights necessary?

  • Limited government: They restrict arbitrary legislative and executive action.
  • Individual liberty: They protect freedoms essential to personal autonomy and human dignity.
  • Political democracy: They protect speech, association, assembly and participation in public life.
  • Substantive equality: They prohibit discrimination and permit constitutionally authorised affirmative action.
  • Minority protection: They protect religious, linguistic and cultural interests.
  • Social transformation: They prohibit practices such as untouchability, forced labour and human trafficking.
  • Constitutional remedies: They provide legal mechanisms to challenge violations.

Historical foundations

The demand for civil liberties and equality developed through the Indian national movement. The Nehru Report of 1928, the Karachi Resolution of 1931 and debates within the Constituent Assembly contributed to the constitutional understanding of rights.

The Constitution combined enforceable rights with broader social and economic objectives. This distinction is reflected in the relationship between Part III and the Directive Principles in Part IV.

The Right to Property was originally a Fundamental Right under Articles 19(1)(f) and 31. The 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978 removed it from Part III. It is now a constitutional right under Article 300A.

3. Article 12: Meaning of the State

Article 12 defines "the State" for the purposes of Part III. It includes:

  • The Government and Parliament of India.
  • The Government and Legislature of each State.
  • All local authorities within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India.
  • Other authorities within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India.

Why is Article 12 important?

Most Fundamental Rights operate as limitations on State action. Article 12 therefore helps determine which bodies are subject to the constitutional obligations contained in Part III.

Meaning of "other authorities"

The expression has been interpreted broadly by the Supreme Court. A body may qualify as an instrumentality or agency of the State depending on the nature and degree of governmental control and other relevant indicators.

Relevant considerations developed in judicial decisions include:

  • Whether the government owns the entire share capital.
  • Whether financial assistance from the State meets substantially the entire expenditure.
  • Whether the State exercises deep and pervasive control.
  • Whether the body's functions are of public importance and closely related to governmental functions.
  • Whether a government department has been transferred to the body.
  • Whether the body enjoys a government-conferred or protected monopoly.

These are indicators rather than a rigid checklist. No single factor is necessarily conclusive.

Important judgments

  • Rajasthan Electricity Board v. Mohan Lal (1967): Interpreted "other authorities" broadly.
  • Sukhdev Singh v. Bhagatram (1975): Recognised statutory corporations performing public functions as authorities under Article 12.
  • R.D. Shetty v. International Airport Authority of India (1979): Developed indicators for identifying governmental instrumentalities.
  • Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib (1981): Explained that the legal form of an entity is not decisive in determining whether it is an instrumentality of the State.
  • Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. Indian Institute of Chemical Biology (2002): Emphasised whether the body is financially, functionally and administratively dominated by or under the control of government.
  • Zee Telefilms Ltd. v. Union of India (2005): Held that the BCCI was not State under Article 12 on the facts before the Court, while leaving open the possibility of public law remedies in appropriate cases.

4. Article 13: Laws Inconsistent with Fundamental Rights

Article 13 establishes the constitutional supremacy of Fundamental Rights over inconsistent ordinary laws.

Article 13(1): Pre-Constitution laws

Laws in force immediately before the commencement of the Constitution become void to the extent that they are inconsistent with Fundamental Rights.

Article 13(2): Post-Constitution laws

The State shall not make any law that takes away or abridges the rights conferred by Part III. A law made in contravention is void to the extent of the contravention.

Doctrine of severability

If only part of a law is unconstitutional, the valid portion may survive where it can be separated from the invalid portion and the legislative scheme can continue coherently.

Doctrine of eclipse

A pre-Constitution law inconsistent with a Fundamental Right is rendered unenforceable to the extent of the inconsistency. It is often described as remaining eclipsed rather than disappearing entirely.

Constitutional amendments and Article 13

The 24th Amendment inserted Article 13(4), stating that Article 13 does not apply to constitutional amendments made under Article 368. However, constitutional amendments remain subject to the basic structure limitation established in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973).

5. Right to Equality: Articles 14–18

Article 14: Equality before Law and Equal Protection of Laws

Article 14 guarantees equality before the law and equal protection of the laws to every person within the territory of India.

  • Equality before law: A negative concept associated with the absence of special privileges and equal subjection to ordinary law.
  • Equal protection of laws: A positive concept requiring equal treatment of persons similarly situated and permitting constitutionally justified differentiation.

Reasonable classification

A classification ordinarily satisfies the traditional test when:

  1. It is based on an intelligible differentia that distinguishes those grouped together from those excluded; and
  2. The differentia has a rational nexus with the object sought to be achieved by the law.

Article 14 also prohibits arbitrary State action. Its interpretation has developed beyond the narrow classification test to include broader concerns of fairness, non-arbitrariness and substantive equality.

Article 15: Prohibition of Discrimination

Article 15 prohibits the State from discriminating against any citizen only on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth.

  • Article 15(1): Prohibits discrimination by the State on the specified grounds.
  • Article 15(2): Prohibits specified forms of discrimination in access to shops, public restaurants, hotels, places of public entertainment and certain public facilities. It applies to citizens and addresses discrimination by both the State and private persons within its scope.
  • Article 15(3): Permits special provisions for women and children.
  • Article 15(4): Permits special provisions for the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes and for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
  • Article 15(5): Enables special provisions concerning admission to educational institutions, including private institutions whether aided or unaided, subject to the exception for minority educational institutions under Article 30(1).
  • Article 15(6): Permits special provisions for economically weaker sections, including reservation in educational institutions subject to constitutional conditions and the exclusion provided for minority educational institutions under Article 30(1).

Article 16: Equality of Opportunity in Public Employment

Article 16 guarantees equality of opportunity for all citizens in matters relating to employment or appointment to offices under the State.

  • Article 16(1): Equality of opportunity in public employment.
  • Article 16(2): Prohibits discrimination on specified grounds.
  • Article 16(3): Allows Parliament to prescribe residence requirements for certain classes of employment under a State or Union Territory.
  • Article 16(4): Permits reservation for a backward class of citizens that, in the opinion of the State, is not adequately represented in State services.
  • Article 16(4A): Enables reservation in promotion, with consequential seniority where constitutionally permitted, for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes inadequately represented in services.
  • Article 16(4B): Allows unfilled reserved vacancies to be treated as a separate class for determining the ceiling on reservations in subsequent years, subject to the provision.
  • Article 16(5): Preserves specified laws requiring the holder of an office connected with a religious or denominational institution to belong to a particular religion or denomination.
  • Article 16(6): Permits reservation for economically weaker sections in public employment, subject to the constitutional ceiling and other conditions.

Article 17: Abolition of Untouchability

Article 17 abolishes untouchability and forbids its practice in any form. Enforcement of any disability arising out of untouchability is an offence punishable according to law.

Important legislation includes:

  • Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955.
  • Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989.

Article 17 is an important example of a Fundamental Right with significant horizontal operation: it addresses discriminatory practices in society and is not limited to State action.

Article 18: Abolition of Titles

  • The State shall not confer titles, except military and academic distinctions.
  • Indian citizens shall not accept titles from foreign States.
  • Foreigners holding an office of profit or trust under the State cannot accept titles from foreign States without the President's consent.
  • Persons holding an office of profit or trust under the State cannot accept presents, emoluments or offices from or under a foreign State without the President's consent, as provided in Article 18(4).

National awards such as the Bharat Ratna and Padma awards are not prohibited titles when they are not used as prefixes or suffixes to the recipients' names.

Important equality judgments

  • State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar (1952): Examined reasonable classification under Article 14.
  • E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu (1974): Connected equality with non-arbitrariness.
  • Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978): Reinforced the relationship between fairness, non-arbitrariness and Articles 14, 19 and 21.
  • Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992): Considered reservation for backward classes, the creamy layer and the general 50 per cent ceiling, subject to exceptional circumstances recognised by the Court.
  • Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022): Upheld the 103rd Amendment providing for EWS reservation by majority.

6. Right to Freedom: Articles 19–22

Article 19: Six Freedoms

Article 19 guarantees six freedoms to citizens of India:

  1. Freedom of speech and expression — Article 19(1)(a).
  2. Freedom to assemble peacefully and without arms — Article 19(1)(b).
  3. Freedom to form associations, unions or cooperative societies — Article 19(1)(c).
  4. Freedom to move freely throughout the territory of India — Article 19(1)(d).
  5. Freedom to reside and settle in any part of the territory of India — Article 19(1)(e).
  6. Freedom to practise any profession or to carry on any occupation, trade or business — Article 19(1)(g).

Reasonable restrictions

Article 19 does not make these freedoms absolute. The Constitution specifies the permissible grounds on which restrictions may be imposed.

Freedom Restriction provision Grounds
Speech and expression Article 19(2) Sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation and incitement to an offence.
Peaceful assembly Article 19(3) Sovereignty and integrity of India and public order.
Associations and unions Article 19(4) Sovereignty and integrity of India, public order and morality.
Movement and residence Article 19(5) Interests of the general public and protection of the interests of any Scheduled Tribe.
Profession, occupation, trade or business Article 19(6) Interests of the general public, professional or technical qualifications, and State monopoly in specified activities.

Article 20: Protection in Respect of Conviction for Offences

Article 20 provides three protections to persons in relation to criminal conviction and prosecution.

  • Article 20(1) — Ex post facto criminal laws: A person cannot be convicted for an act that was not an offence when committed or subjected to a greater penalty than the law prescribed at that time. The protection concerns criminal conviction and punishment; it does not generally prohibit retrospective civil legislation.
  • Article 20(2) — Double jeopardy: No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once.
  • Article 20(3) — Self-incrimination: No person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself.

Article 20 protections are available to persons, not only citizens. They also cannot be suspended through an order under Article 359 during a National Emergency.

Article 21: Protection of Life and Personal Liberty

Article 21 states that no person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.

In Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), the Supreme Court held that a procedure depriving a person of life or liberty must be just, fair and reasonable and not arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive.

Judicial interpretation has recognised several dimensions of life and personal liberty, including:

  • Right to live with human dignity.
  • Right to privacy.
  • Right to livelihood.
  • Right to speedy trial.
  • Right to legal aid in appropriate cases.
  • Right to a clean and healthy environment.
  • Protection against arbitrary deprivation of liberty.
  • Rights relating to bodily integrity and personal autonomy.
  • Protection against custodial abuse and unlawful detention.

Article 21 is available to every person, including foreign nationals. The precise scope of each recognised dimension depends on judicial interpretation and the facts of the case.

Article 21A: Right to Education

Article 21A provides that the State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children aged six to fourteen years in such manner as the State may, by law, determine.

  • Inserted by the 86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002.
  • Implemented through the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009.
  • Read with Article 45, which directs the State to provide early childhood care and education until children complete six years of age.
  • Read with Article 51A(k), which places a duty on parents or guardians to provide educational opportunities to children between six and fourteen years.

Article 22: Protection against Arrest and Detention

Article 22 provides safeguards against arbitrary arrest and detention. It distinguishes ordinary arrests from preventive detention.

Safeguards in ordinary arrests

  • The arrested person must be informed, as soon as may be, of the grounds of arrest.
  • The person has the right to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner of choice.
  • The arrested person must be produced before the nearest magistrate within 24 hours of arrest, excluding the time necessary for the journey.
  • The person cannot be detained beyond that period without the authority of a magistrate.

Constitutional exceptions

The safeguards in Article 22(1) and Article 22(2) do not apply to enemy aliens or persons arrested or detained under preventive detention laws, subject to the separate safeguards provided for preventive detention.

Preventive detention safeguards

  • Detention must be authorised under a law providing for preventive detention.
  • The detenu must ordinarily be informed of the grounds of detention as soon as may be, subject to the constitutional exception for facts considered against the public interest to disclose.
  • The detenu must be given the earliest opportunity of making a representation against the detention order.
  • Detention beyond three months is subject to the conditions in Article 22(4), including the role of an Advisory Board unless Parliament provides otherwise within the constitutional limits.
  • Parliament may prescribe the circumstances and maximum period of preventive detention under Article 22(7), subject to the Constitution.

Preventive detention is intended to prevent anticipated conduct, not to punish a person for a completed offence. Its exceptional nature requires strict compliance with constitutional and statutory safeguards.

7. Right against Exploitation: Articles 23–24

Article 23: Prohibition of Human Trafficking and Forced Labour

Article 23 prohibits traffic in human beings, begar and other similar forms of forced labour. Contravention is punishable in accordance with law.

  • Human trafficking: Prohibits the trade and exploitation of human beings.
  • Begar: Prohibits forced service without payment.
  • Forced labour: Covers labour extracted through coercion, including situations where economic compulsion makes a person work for less than the legally prescribed minimum wage, as explained in judicial interpretation.

Article 23 applies against both the State and private individuals. It permits the State to impose compulsory service for public purposes, provided that it does not discriminate only on grounds of religion, race, caste or class.

Article 24: Prohibition of Child Labour in Specified Employment

Article 24 prohibits the employment of children below fourteen years in factories, mines or any other hazardous employment.

The provision must be read with child-protection legislation, including the Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986, as amended.

Important judgments

  • People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982): Explained the scope of forced labour under Article 23.
  • Sanjit Roy v. State of Rajasthan (1983): Held that payment below the minimum wage in famine relief work could violate Article 23.
  • M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu (1996): Addressed child labour and measures for rehabilitation and protection of children.

8. Right to Freedom of Religion: Articles 25–28

Article 25: Freedom of Conscience and Religion

All persons are equally entitled to freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and propagate religion.

This right is subject to public order, morality, health and the other provisions of Part III.

Article 25 also permits the State to regulate economic, financial, political or other secular activities associated with religious practice and to enact laws for social welfare and reform.

The constitutional explanation to Article 25 treats the wearing and carrying of kirpans as included in the profession of the Sikh religion. The provision also explains that references to Hindus for the purposes of Article 25(2)(b) include Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists.

Article 26: Freedom to Manage Religious Affairs

Every religious denomination or any section thereof has the right, subject to public order, morality and health:

  • To establish and maintain institutions for religious and charitable purposes.
  • To manage its own affairs in matters of religion.
  • To own and acquire movable and immovable property.
  • To administer such property in accordance with law.

Article 27: Freedom from Taxation for Promotion of a Particular Religion

No person shall be compelled to pay taxes, the proceeds of which are specifically appropriated in payment of expenses for the promotion or maintenance of any particular religion or religious denomination.

This provision does not prohibit every form of public expenditure that may have an incidental connection with religion. Its application depends on the purpose and appropriation of the tax.

Article 28: Religious Instruction in Educational Institutions

  • No religious instruction shall be provided in an educational institution wholly maintained out of State funds.
  • This prohibition does not apply to an institution administered by the State but established under an endowment or trust that requires religious instruction.
  • No person attending an educational institution recognised by the State or receiving State aid shall be required to take part in religious instruction or worship without consent; for a minor, the guardian's consent is required.

Secularism and religious freedom

Indian secularism does not require an absolute separation between the State and religion in every context. The Constitution permits regulation of secular activities associated with religion and measures for social reform, while protecting constitutionally guaranteed religious freedoms.

Important judgments

  • Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Shirur Mutt (1954): Examined the scope of religious freedom and the distinction between religious matters and secular administration.
  • Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala (1986): Protected students who respectfully stood during the National Anthem but did not sing due to their sincerely held religious beliefs.
  • Ismail Faruqui v. Union of India (1994): Considered aspects of religious practice and property in the context of the Ayodhya acquisition legislation.
  • Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (2018): Examined constitutional questions concerning women's entry into the Sabarimala temple; subsequent review proceedings and related litigation have to be considered separately when assessing the current legal position.

9. Cultural and Educational Rights: Articles 29–30

Article 29: Protection of Interests of Minorities

Article 29(1) gives any section of citizens residing in India or any part of it, having a distinct language, script or culture of its own, the right to conserve the same.

Article 29(2) prohibits denial of admission to any citizen into an educational institution maintained by the State or receiving State aid on grounds only of religion, race, caste or language.

Article 29 is not confined exclusively to religious and linguistic minorities. Its wording protects any section of citizens with the specified distinct cultural characteristics.

Article 30: Right of Minorities to Establish and Administer Educational Institutions

All religious and linguistic minorities have the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.

  • Article 30(1): Protects the right of religious and linguistic minorities to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.
  • Article 30(1A): Provides protection concerning compulsory acquisition of property of minority educational institutions, ensuring that the amount fixed does not restrict or abrogate the right guaranteed by Article 30(1).
  • Article 30(2): Prohibits discrimination in granting aid to educational institutions on the ground that they are under minority management.

Minority educational institutions remain subject to constitutionally permissible regulation concerning academic standards, educational excellence, public order and other legitimate requirements. Regulation must not destroy the protected right to administer.

Important judgments

  • State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951): Addressed the relationship between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles and led to the First Amendment's insertion of Article 15(4).
  • St. Xavier's College v. State of Gujarat (1974): Considered the scope of minority institutions' rights to administer educational institutions.
  • T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka (2002): Examined the rights of minorities and non-minorities to establish and administer educational institutions.
  • P.A. Inamdar v. State of Maharashtra (2005): Considered State regulation and admissions in unaided professional educational institutions.

10. Right to Constitutional Remedies: Article 32

Article 32 guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights. The Court may issue directions, orders or writs for this purpose.

Dr. B. R. Ambedkar described Article 32 as the heart and soul of the Constitution. The right to constitutional remedies makes the guarantees in Part III practically enforceable.

Five constitutional writs

Writ Meaning and purpose
Habeas Corpus Requires the authority detaining a person to produce that person before the court so that the legality of detention can be examined.
Mandamus Commands a public authority to perform a public or legal duty it has failed to perform.
Prohibition Prevents a lower court or tribunal from continuing proceedings beyond its jurisdiction.
Certiorari Quashes an order or decision of a lower court, tribunal or authority where legally justified, including for jurisdictional error.
Quo Warranto Questions the legal authority by which a person holds a substantive public office.

Article 32 and Article 226 compared

Basis Article 32 Article 226
Issuing court Supreme Court. High Courts.
Purpose Enforcement of Fundamental Rights. Enforcement of Fundamental Rights and for other purposes.
Nature of right It is itself a Fundamental Right. It is a constitutional power of High Courts.
Territorial scope Supreme Court exercises jurisdiction under its constitutional framework. High Court jurisdiction is governed by Article 226, including the territorial connection requirements.
Scope Limited to enforcement of Fundamental Rights. Wider subject matter, including other legal rights.

The wider subject-matter jurisdiction of Article 226 does not mean that High Courts must entertain every petition. They may consider alternative remedies, delay, disputed facts and other recognised principles governing writ jurisdiction.

11. Articles 31A–31C: Saving of Certain Laws

Article 31A

Article 31A protects specified categories of laws, particularly those relating to acquisition of estates and related rights, from invalidation on the basis of certain Fundamental Rights challenges. Its scope is governed by the text of the provision and its constitutional conditions.

Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule

Article 31B protects laws included in the Ninth Schedule from being treated as void merely because they are inconsistent with or take away rights conferred by Part III, subject to the basic structure doctrine.

In I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007), the Supreme Court clarified that post-24 April 1973 Ninth Schedule insertions are open to judicial review where their impact damages the basic structure.

Article 31C

Article 31C protects laws genuinely giving effect to the policy of the State towards securing the principles specified in Article 39(b) and Article 39(c) from challenge under Articles 14 and 19, subject to the limits established by constitutional interpretation.

The 42nd Amendment attempted to extend this protection to all Directive Principles. In Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980), the Supreme Court invalidated that extension. The provision's present scope remains confined to the principles in Article 39(b) and Article 39(c).

In Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra (2024), the Supreme Court considered the interpretation of Article 39(b) and the scope of Article 31C. The decision clarified the constitutional approach to identifying material resources of the community and reaffirmed the limited operation of Article 31C.

12. Articles 33–35: Special Provisions

Article 33: Modification of Rights for Certain Forces

Parliament may determine the extent to which Fundamental Rights apply to members of:

  • The Armed Forces.
  • Forces charged with the maintenance of public order.
  • Persons employed in intelligence or counter-intelligence organisations established by the State.
  • Specified telecommunication systems established for the purposes of such forces or organisations.

The purpose is to ensure the proper discharge of duties and maintenance of discipline.

Article 34: Restriction of Rights during Martial Law

Article 34 permits Parliament to indemnify persons for acts done in connection with the maintenance or restoration of order in an area where martial law was in force, subject to the constitutional provision.

Article 35: Legislation to Give Effect to Certain Provisions

Article 35 gives Parliament exclusive legislative power over specified matters relating to the implementation of certain provisions in Part III. It also provides for parliamentary legislation concerning punishment for acts declared offences under specified Fundamental Rights.

13. Fundamental Rights and the Right to Property

The Right to Property was originally guaranteed as a Fundamental Right under Articles 19(1)(f) and 31.

The 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978 removed it from Part III and introduced Article 300A in Part XII.

Article 300A provides that no person shall be deprived of property save by authority of law. The right is therefore a constitutional right, not a Fundamental Right.

Deprivation of property must have legal authority. Executive action without authority of law cannot validly deprive a person of property.

15. Fundamental Rights during National Emergency

The Constitution provides special rules governing the operation and enforcement of certain Fundamental Rights during a National Emergency.

Article 358: Suspension of Article 19 protections

Article 358 concerns the effect of a National Emergency declared on the grounds of war or external aggression on the protections under Article 19.

  • It does not apply to an Emergency declared solely on the ground of armed rebellion.
  • Its operation is subject to the constitutional conditions introduced by the 44th Amendment.
  • It concerns the restrictions on State legislative and executive power under Article 19 during the relevant Emergency, subject to the constitutional requirements.

Article 359: Suspension of the right to move courts

Article 359 empowers the President to suspend the right to move any court for enforcement of specified Fundamental Rights during a National Emergency, subject to the constitutional procedure and limitations.

  • The Presidential order must specify the rights whose enforcement is suspended.
  • The suspension operates for the period specified in the order, subject to the constitutional framework.
  • Articles 20 and 21 cannot be included in such an order.

Effect of the 44th Amendment

The 44th Amendment strengthened safeguards relating to Fundamental Rights during Emergency. In particular, it ensured that the protections of Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended through Article 359.

17. Relationship between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles

Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles are complementary parts of the constitutional framework. Fundamental Rights protect individual liberty and equality, while Directive Principles guide the State towards social and economic welfare.

Basis Fundamental Rights Directive Principles
Location Part III. Part IV.
Articles 12–35. 36–51.
Nature Constitutional rights and protections. Guiding principles for State policy.
Enforceability Generally enforceable by courts. Not directly enforceable by courts under Article 37.
Primary purpose Protect liberty, equality and individual rights. Promote social and economic welfare.
Implementation Constitutional remedies and legal safeguards. Legislation, public policy and governance.

The constitutional relationship has evolved through judicial decisions. The present approach emphasises harmony and balance rather than treating either Part as universally superior to the other.

The relationship is examined further in the chapter on Directive Principles of State Policy.

18. Contemporary Constitutional Issues

Freedom of speech and digital communication

Digital platforms have expanded the practical exercise of speech and expression. They have also raised questions about online restrictions, intermediary regulation, misinformation, privacy and the proportionality of State action. Restrictions must be assessed against the grounds specified in Article 19(2) and applicable constitutional standards.

Privacy and State surveillance

Recognition of privacy as a Fundamental Right has implications for personal data, surveillance, informational autonomy and bodily integrity. State measures affecting privacy must satisfy applicable constitutional requirements, including legality, legitimate purpose and proportionality.

Preventive detention and personal liberty

Preventive detention remains a significant constitutional issue because it permits deprivation of liberty without an ordinary criminal conviction. Effective procedural safeguards, timely consideration of representations and judicial review are important to prevent arbitrary detention.

Affirmative action and substantive equality

Reservation policies continue to raise questions concerning representation, backwardness, economic disadvantage, equality of opportunity and the constitutional limits of classification. Judicial interpretation distinguishes formal equality from measures designed to address structural disadvantage.

Freedom of religion and individual rights

Constitutional disputes may involve competing claims concerning religious autonomy, gender equality, dignity, public order and social reform. Such questions require careful interpretation of Articles 25 and 26 alongside the other provisions of Part III.

Access to constitutional remedies

Legal costs, delays, unequal access to representation and procedural complexity can weaken the practical enjoyment of rights. Legal aid, effective judicial administration and accessible remedies remain important to constitutional governance.

19. Significance and Limitations

Constitutional significance

  • Establishes enforceable limits on governmental power.
  • Protects individual liberty, dignity and autonomy.
  • Provides constitutional safeguards against discrimination.
  • Strengthens democratic participation and public accountability.
  • Protects cultural, educational and religious pluralism.
  • Enables constitutional courts to review State action.
  • Supports social transformation through equality and anti-exploitation guarantees.

Practical limitations and challenges

  • Rights may be restricted on constitutionally permitted grounds.
  • Judicial remedies can be affected by delay, cost and access barriers.
  • Some rights require legislation and institutional capacity for effective implementation.
  • Conflicts between individual liberty and collective interests require careful constitutional balancing.
  • Private power can affect the practical enjoyment of rights even where the constitutional provision primarily restrains the State.
  • Formal equality does not by itself eliminate structural and social inequalities.

20. Measures to Strengthen the Realisation of Rights

  • Improve access to legal aid and strengthen institutions that assist disadvantaged groups.
  • Reduce judicial delays and improve the effectiveness of constitutional remedies.
  • Ensure that restrictions on freedoms are lawful, necessary and proportionate.
  • Strengthen accountability mechanisms for custodial abuse and unlawful detention.
  • Improve implementation of anti-discrimination and anti-exploitation legislation.
  • Promote awareness of constitutional rights through education and public institutions.
  • Protect institutional independence and the effectiveness of judicial review.
  • Ensure that affirmative action policies are evidence-based and consistent with constitutional requirements.
  • Strengthen privacy and data-protection safeguards in public administration.
  • Promote a constitutional culture that respects pluralism, dissent, dignity and equality.

Quick Revision

  • Fundamental Rights are contained in Part III, Articles 12–35.
  • There are six broad categories of Fundamental Rights.
  • Article 12 defines the State for Part III.
  • Article 13 provides for invalidity of laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights to the extent of inconsistency.
  • Article 14 guarantees equality before law and equal protection of laws to all persons.
  • Articles 15 and 16 provide equality protections and permit specified forms of affirmative action.
  • Article 17 abolishes untouchability.
  • Article 18 abolishes titles, subject to military and academic distinctions.
  • Article 19 guarantees six freedoms to citizens, subject to specified reasonable restrictions.
  • Article 20 provides protection against ex post facto criminal laws, double jeopardy and compelled self-incrimination.
  • Article 21 protects life and personal liberty.
  • Article 21A guarantees education for children aged six to fourteen years.
  • Article 22 provides safeguards concerning arrest and preventive detention.
  • Articles 23–24 prohibit trafficking, forced labour and specified forms of child labour.
  • Articles 25–28 protect freedom of religion.
  • Articles 29–30 protect cultural and educational interests.
  • Article 32 guarantees the right to approach the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights.
  • Article 226 gives High Courts wider writ jurisdiction than Article 32 in terms of subject matter.
  • Article 300A protects property as a constitutional right, not a Fundamental Right.
  • Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended through an order under Article 359.
  • The basic structure doctrine limits Parliament's power to amend the Constitution.
