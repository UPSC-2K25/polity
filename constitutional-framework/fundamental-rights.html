Fundamental Rights

Fundamental Rights are constitutional guarantees that protect individual liberty, equality and dignity. They limit arbitrary State action and provide legal safeguards essential to democratic government.

They are contained in Part III of the Constitution, Articles 12 to 35. They are generally enforceable through courts, and the Constitution provides remedies when these rights are violated.

1. Meaning and Importance

Fundamental Rights establish minimum conditions necessary for the development of individuals and the functioning of a democratic society. They protect citizens and, in several cases, all persons within India.

  • Protect individual liberty and human dignity.
  • Ensure equality before the law.
  • Prevent arbitrary exercise of State power.
  • Protect religious, cultural and educational freedoms.
  • Provide constitutional remedies against rights violations.
  • Support democratic participation and the rule of law.

3. Classification of Fundamental Rights

The Constitution currently provides six broad categories of Fundamental Rights:

  1. Right to Equality — Articles 14–18.
  2. Right to Freedom — Articles 19–22.
  3. Right against Exploitation — Articles 23–24.
  4. Right to Freedom of Religion — Articles 25–28.
  5. Cultural and Educational Rights — Articles 29–30.
  6. Right to Constitutional Remedies — Article 32.

The Right to Property was originally a Fundamental Right under Articles 19(1)(f) and 31. It was removed from Part III by the 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978. It is now protected as a constitutional right under Article 300A.

4. Right to Equality (Articles 14–18)

Article 14: Equality before Law and Equal Protection of Laws

Article 14 guarantees equality before the law and equal protection of the laws to every person within the territory of India.

  • Equality before law: A negative concept associated with the absence of special privileges and equal subjection to ordinary law.
  • Equal protection of laws: A positive concept requiring equal treatment of persons similarly situated. It permits reasonable classification when constitutionally justified.

Reasonable classification must be based on an intelligible differentia and that differentia must have a rational connection with the object sought to be achieved. Article 14 also prohibits arbitrary State action.

Article 15: Prohibition of Discrimination

Article 15 prohibits the State from discriminating against citizens only on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth.

It also provides for specified forms of affirmative action:

  • Article 15(3): Special provisions for women and children.
  • Article 15(4): Advancement of socially and educationally backward classes, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
  • Article 15(5): Special provisions concerning admission to educational institutions, including private institutions, subject to the constitutional exception for minority educational institutions under Article 30(1).
  • Article 15(6): Special provisions for economically weaker sections, including the constitutionally permitted reservation in educational institutions.

Article 16: Equality of Opportunity in Public Employment

Article 16 guarantees equality of opportunity for all citizens in matters relating to employment or appointment to offices under the State.

  • Article 16(2) prohibits discrimination on specified grounds.
  • Article 16(3) permits Parliament to prescribe residence requirements in certain cases.
  • Article 16(4) permits reservation for backward classes inadequately represented in State services.
  • Article 16(4A) provides for reservation in promotion for specified categories of employees belonging to Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, subject to constitutional conditions.
  • Article 16(4B) deals with unfilled reserved vacancies.
  • Article 16(6) provides for reservation for economically weaker sections in public employment.

Article 17: Abolition of Untouchability

Article 17 abolishes untouchability and forbids its practice in any form. Enforcement of any disability arising out of untouchability is an offence punishable according to law.

Important legislation includes the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 and the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989.

Article 18: Abolition of Titles

Article 18 prohibits the State from conferring titles other than military and academic distinctions. It also restricts citizens and persons holding offices of profit or trust under the State from accepting certain titles or honours from foreign States.

National awards such as the Bharat Ratna and Padma awards are not prohibited titles, provided they are not used as prefixes or suffixes to the names of recipients.

5. Right to Freedom (Articles 19–22)

Article 19: Six Freedoms

Article 19 guarantees six freedoms to citizens of India:

  1. Freedom of speech and expression — Article 19(1)(a).
  2. Freedom to assemble peacefully and without arms — Article 19(1)(b).
  3. Freedom to form associations, unions or co-operative societies — Article 19(1)(c).
  4. Freedom to move freely throughout the territory of India — Article 19(1)(d).
  5. Freedom to reside and settle in any part of the territory of India — Article 19(1)(e).
  6. Freedom to practise any profession or to carry on any occupation, trade or business — Article 19(1)(g).

The Constitution permits reasonable restrictions on these freedoms on specified grounds. The grounds differ according to the particular freedom and are set out in Articles 19(2) to 19(6).

Article 20: Protection in Respect of Conviction for Offences

Article 20 provides three important protections to persons accused of offences:

  • Article 20(1): No ex post facto criminal law. A person cannot be convicted for an act that was not an offence when committed or subjected to a greater penalty than the law prescribed at that time.
  • Article 20(2): Protection against double jeopardy. No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once.
  • Article 20(3): Protection against self-incrimination. No person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself.

Article 21: Protection of Life and Personal Liberty

Article 21 states that no person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.

Judicial interpretation has expanded the meaning of life and personal liberty to include several rights essential to a dignified existence.

Rights recognised through judicial interpretation include:

  • Right to live with human dignity.
  • Right to privacy.
  • Right to livelihood.
  • Right to a speedy trial.
  • Right to legal aid in appropriate cases.
  • Right to a clean and healthy environment.
  • Protection against arbitrary deprivation of liberty.

Article 21 is available to every person, not only citizens.

Article 21A: Right to Education

Article 21A provides free and compulsory education to all children between six and fourteen years of age in the manner determined by law.

It was inserted by the 86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002. The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 provides the legislative framework for its implementation.

Article 22: Protection against Arrest and Detention

Article 22 provides safeguards to persons who are arrested and detained.

In ordinary arrests, the safeguards include:

  • Being informed of the grounds of arrest.
  • Consulting and being defended by a legal practitioner of choice.
  • Being produced before the nearest magistrate within 24 hours of arrest, excluding necessary journey time.
  • Not being detained beyond the period authorised by a magistrate without further legal authority.

These protections have constitutional exceptions for enemy aliens and persons detained under preventive detention laws, subject to the safeguards prescribed in Article 22.

6. Right against Exploitation (Articles 23–24)

Article 23: Prohibition of Human Trafficking and Forced Labour

Article 23 prohibits human trafficking, begar and other similar forms of forced labour. Contravention is punishable in accordance with law.

The prohibition applies against both the State and private individuals. The State may impose compulsory service for public purposes, but it cannot discriminate solely on specified grounds.

Article 24: Prohibition of Child Labour in Hazardous Employment

Article 24 prohibits the employment of children below fourteen years of age in factories, mines or other hazardous employment.

The Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986, as amended, provides a statutory framework concerning child and adolescent labour.

7. Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28)

Article 25: Freedom of Conscience and Religion

Article 25 guarantees to all persons freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and propagate religion.

This freedom is subject to public order, morality, health and the other provisions of Part III. The State may regulate secular activities associated with religious practice and undertake social welfare and reform.

Article 26: Freedom to Manage Religious Affairs

Every religious denomination or section thereof has the right, subject to public order, morality and health, to:

  • Establish and maintain institutions for religious and charitable purposes.
  • Manage its own affairs in matters of religion.
  • Own and acquire movable and immovable property.
  • Administer such property in accordance with law.

Article 27: Freedom from Taxation for Promotion of a Religion

Article 27 protects persons from being compelled to pay taxes specifically appropriated for the promotion or maintenance of a particular religion or religious denomination.

Article 28: Religious Instruction in Educational Institutions

Article 28 regulates religious instruction and worship in educational institutions:

  • No religious instruction shall be provided in an educational institution wholly maintained out of State funds.
  • This prohibition does not apply to institutions administered by the State but established under an endowment or trust requiring religious instruction.
  • No person attending a State-recognised or State-aided educational institution shall be required to participate in religious instruction or worship without consent, or the consent of a guardian where applicable.

8. Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29–30)

Article 29: Protection of Interests of Minorities

Article 29(1) gives any section of citizens having a distinct language, script or culture the right to conserve it.

Article 29(2) prohibits denial of admission to any citizen into an educational institution maintained by the State or receiving State aid on grounds only of religion, race, caste or language.

Article 29 is not limited exclusively to religious or linguistic minorities.

Article 30: Right of Minorities to Establish and Administer Educational Institutions

Religious and linguistic minorities have the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.

  • Article 30(1) protects the right to establish and administer such institutions.
  • Article 30(1A) deals with compulsory acquisition of property of minority educational institutions.
  • Article 30(2) prohibits discrimination in granting aid to educational institutions on the ground that they are under minority management.

9. Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32)

Article 32 guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights. The Supreme Court may issue directions, orders or writs for this purpose.

Dr. B. R. Ambedkar described Article 32 as the heart and soul of the Constitution.

Five Constitutional Writs

Writ Meaning and Purpose
Habeas Corpus Protects against unlawful detention by requiring the detained person to be produced before the court.
Mandamus Commands a public authority to perform a public or legal duty it has failed to perform.
Prohibition Prevents a lower court or tribunal from continuing proceedings beyond its jurisdiction.
Certiorari Quashes an order or decision of a lower court or tribunal where legally justified, including jurisdictional error.
Quo Warranto Questions the legal authority by which a person holds a public office.

High Courts may issue writs under Article 226 not only for enforcement of Fundamental Rights but also for other purposes. The jurisdiction under Article 226 is therefore wider in subject matter than Article 32.

10. Articles 33–35: Special Provisions

Article 33: Modification of Rights for Certain Forces

Parliament may determine the extent to which Fundamental Rights apply to members of the Armed Forces, forces charged with maintaining public order, intelligence organisations and related services, in order to ensure proper discharge of duties and maintenance of discipline.

Article 34: Restriction of Rights during Martial Law

Article 34 permits Parliament to indemnify persons for acts done in connection with the maintenance or restoration of order in an area where martial law was in force, subject to the constitutional provision.

Article 35: Legislation to Give Effect to Certain Provisions

Article 35 gives Parliament exclusive power to make laws on specified matters concerning the implementation of certain Fundamental Rights provisions.

11. Fundamental Rights and the Right to Property

The Right to Property was originally guaranteed as a Fundamental Right under Articles 19(1)(f) and 31.

The 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978 removed it from Part III. Article 300A was introduced in Part XII, providing that no person shall be deprived of property save by authority of law.

The Right to Property is therefore a constitutional right, not a Fundamental Right. It remains subject to constitutional and statutory safeguards.

13. Fundamental Rights during Emergency

The Constitution contains special provisions concerning Fundamental Rights during a National Emergency.

Article 358

Article 358 concerns the suspension of the protections under Article 19 during a National Emergency declared on grounds of war or external aggression. It does not apply to an Emergency declared solely on the ground of armed rebellion. Its operation is subject to the constitutional conditions and safeguards.

Article 359

Article 359 empowers the President to suspend the right to move courts for enforcement of specified Fundamental Rights during an Emergency, subject to the constitutional procedure.

The rights guaranteed by Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended through an order under Article 359.

14. Important Supreme Court Judgments

Case Year Constitutional Significance
Champakam Dorairajan v. State of Madras 1951 Addressed the relationship between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles; led to the First Amendment's insertion of Article 15(4).
Shankari Prasad v. Union of India 1951 Upheld Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights under the constitutional amendment procedure as then understood.
Golaknath v. State of Punjab 1967 Held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights; this position was later overruled.
Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala 1973 Established that Parliament may amend the Constitution but cannot damage or destroy its basic structure.
Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India 1978 Expanded the interpretation of Article 21 and required procedure depriving personal liberty to be just, fair and reasonable.
Minerva Mills v. Union of India 1980 Emphasised the harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.
Indra Sawhney v. Union of India 1992 Considered reservation for backward classes and the creamy layer principle.
K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India 2017 Recognised the right to privacy as a Fundamental Right protected by the Constitution.
Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India 2018 Emphasised equality, dignity, liberty and constitutional morality.
Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India 2022 Upheld the constitutional validity of the 103rd Amendment providing for EWS reservation.

15. Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles

Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles serve complementary purposes. Fundamental Rights protect individual freedoms and constitutional guarantees, while Directive Principles guide the State towards social and economic welfare.
