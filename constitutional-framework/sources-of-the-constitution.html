Sources of the Indian Constitution

The Constitution of India reflects the experience of India's own constitutional development and the selective study of constitutional systems elsewhere. The Constituent Assembly examined different institutional arrangements and adapted them to the political, social and administrative conditions of an independent, diverse and democratic country.

The Government of India Act, 1935 was a major source of the administrative framework. Foreign constitutions contributed ideas concerning parliamentary government, federalism, rights, judicial review, social policy and constitutional procedures. These influences did not make the Indian Constitution a mechanical compilation of foreign provisions.

1. Government of India Act, 1935

The Government of India Act, 1935 was the most substantial immediate source of many administrative and structural provisions incorporated into the Constitution. The Constitution-makers retained or adapted several institutional arrangements while replacing colonial authority with democratic constitutional government.

Important features derived or adapted

  • Federal scheme and distribution of legislative powers through three legislative lists.
  • Office of the Governor and aspects of provincial administration.
  • Public Service Commission framework.
  • Emergency-related administrative provisions.
  • Federal Court as an institutional predecessor of the Supreme Court.
  • Administrative and procedural details of government.

Important differences

  • The 1935 Act operated under British sovereignty; the Constitution established a sovereign democratic republic.
  • The Act provided for provincial autonomy but did not establish the same democratic federal structure and constitutional supremacy as the Constitution.
  • The Constitution introduced universal adult franchise, enforceable Fundamental Rights and a more comprehensive system of constitutional accountability.

Analytical point: Institutional continuity did not mean political continuity. The Constitution transformed inherited administrative arrangements by placing them within a framework of popular sovereignty, fundamental rights and responsible government.

2. United Kingdom

The British constitutional tradition influenced India's parliamentary institutions, cabinet government and several legislative practices. These ideas were adapted to a written Constitution that is supreme over all organs of government.

  • Parliamentary form of government.
  • Cabinet system and collective responsibility.
  • Rule of law.
  • Legislative procedure and parliamentary privileges.
  • Single citizenship.
  • Writ jurisdiction and aspects of the common-law tradition.
  • Bicameral parliamentary structure.
  • Legislative procedures and conventions associated with parliamentary government.

India's system differs from the British model: India has a written and supreme Constitution, an elected republican head of State, judicial review and a constitutionally entrenched amendment procedure. Parliament is powerful but does not possess the same doctrine of unlimited parliamentary sovereignty.

3. United States of America

The United States Constitution influenced the Indian framework for rights, judicial institutions and constitutional limitations on public power.

  • Fundamental Rights.
  • Independence of the judiciary.
  • Judicial review.
  • Impeachment of the President.
  • Removal process for judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts.
  • Written Constitution and constitutional supremacy.
  • Vice-President as ex-officio Chairman of the Council of States.

The Indian system is not a replica of the American presidential federation. India adopted parliamentary government and an integrated judiciary, and its federal arrangements give the Union substantial constitutional powers.

4. Ireland

The Irish Constitution of 1937 influenced the Directive Principles of State Policy and aspects of the method of nomination to the Council of States.

  • Directive Principles of State Policy.
  • Nomination of members to the Rajya Sabha.

The Directive Principles guide the State in promoting social and economic welfare. They are not enforceable by courts as independent claims, but Article 37 declares them fundamental in the governance of the country.

Clarification: The election of elected Rajya Sabha members by elected members of State Legislative Assemblies through proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote should not be confused with the Irish influence on nomination.

5. Canada

The Canadian constitutional framework influenced India's federal arrangement, especially the position of the Union and the distribution of residual legislative powers.

  • Federation with a strong Centre.
  • Residuary legislative powers vested in the Union.
  • Appointment of State Governors by the Union.
  • Advisory jurisdiction of the Supreme Court.

India's federal structure combines constitutionally recognised States with a strong Union. The distribution of powers is subject to the provisions of the Constitution and judicial interpretation.

6. Australia

The Australian Constitution influenced India's legislative distribution and certain parliamentary and economic arrangements.

  • Concurrent List.
  • Freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse.
  • Joint sitting of the two Houses of Parliament to resolve specified legislative deadlocks.

The Concurrent List enables both Parliament and State Legislatures to legislate on specified subjects. In cases of repugnancy, Article 254 provides the constitutional rules governing the relationship between parliamentary and State legislation.

India's provisions on trade and commerce are contained in Part XIII. Their operation must be understood in the context of the Constitution's rules on permissible restrictions and exceptions.

7. Germany — Weimar Constitution

The Weimar Constitution of Germany is commonly identified as an influence on India's emergency framework, particularly provisions concerning the suspension of certain rights during exceptional situations.

  • Emergency-related provisions concerning the operation or suspension of certain rights.

India's emergency provisions are contained in Part XVIII. Their operation is governed by specific constitutional grounds, procedures and safeguards. The Indian framework has its own constitutional design and should not be equated with the Weimar arrangements.

8. Former Union of Soviet Socialist Republics

Soviet constitutional thought is commonly associated with the Constitution's emphasis on social and economic justice and the inclusion of Fundamental Duties.

  • Fundamental Duties.
  • Emphasis on social, economic and political justice in the Preamble.

Fundamental Duties were inserted by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976. The Preamble's commitment to justice, however, must be understood within India's own freedom movement and the Objectives Resolution adopted by the Constituent Assembly.

9. France

The French Revolution and French republican tradition influenced the ideals expressed in the Preamble.

  • Republican form of government.
  • Ideals of liberty, equality and fraternity.

These ideals express the constitutional commitment to individual freedom, equal civic status and fraternity among citizens. Their meaning in India is shaped by the Constitution's wider commitment to dignity, social justice and democratic citizenship.

10. South Africa

The South African constitutional framework influenced aspects of India's constitutional amendment procedure and the electoral method for elected Rajya Sabha members.

  • Elements of the constitutional amendment procedure.
  • Proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote for election of Rajya Sabha members by elected members of State Legislative Assemblies.

India's amendment procedure is a composite arrangement. Different categories of amendments follow different requirements, including special majorities and, for specified federal matters, ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures.

11. Japan

The Japanese Constitution is associated with the expression "procedure established by law" used in Article 21 of the Indian Constitution.

The expression originally reflected a narrower approach than the American doctrine of due process of law. However, in Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), the Supreme Court held that the procedure contemplated by Article 21 must be fair, just and reasonable and not arbitrary.

This judicial interpretation substantially expanded the protection of life and personal liberty. The present understanding of Article 21 therefore cannot be reduced to the original comparative source of the phrase.

12. Other Influences and Constitutional Context

Constitutional conventions and common-law traditions

Several features of Indian constitutional practice developed through conventions, legislative traditions and common-law principles. Their operation is shaped by the written Constitution and judicial interpretation.

Indian national movement

The Constitution was also shaped by ideas and demands developed within India's freedom struggle, including:

  • Representative and responsible government.
  • Universal adult franchise and political equality.
  • Protection of civil liberties.
  • Social and economic justice.
  • National unity alongside cultural and linguistic diversity.
  • Protection of disadvantaged communities.

Objectives Resolution

The Objectives Resolution moved by Jawaharlal Nehru in December 1946 and adopted in January 1947 provided an important indigenous statement of the Assembly's constitutional aspirations. Its principles are reflected in the Preamble.

These associations are useful for understanding comparative influences, but they are not exhaustive explanations of the origin of each constitutional provision. Some provisions have multiple influences or evolved through Indian constitutional experience.

14. Borrowing, Adaptation and Constitutional Originality

The use of foreign constitutional models does not by itself determine the character or legitimacy of a Constitution. The relevant questions are how ideas were selected, adapted, reconciled and placed within the country's own constitutional structure.

Why comparative borrowing was useful

  • It enabled constitution-makers to draw on tested institutional experiences.
  • It provided alternatives for designing federal, parliamentary and judicial institutions.
  • It helped identify safeguards against arbitrary power and institutional instability.

Why adaptation was necessary

  • India had a distinctive history of colonial rule and a mass freedom movement.
  • Its social and linguistic diversity required specific constitutional safeguards.
  • The Constitution had to combine democratic government with social transformation.
  • The scale and developmental challenges of the country required a particular institutional balance.

Conclusion: The Indian Constitution is best understood as a distinct constitutional synthesis. It draws on comparative experience while deriving its authority and objectives from the people of India and the deliberations of the Constituent Assembly.
