Salient Features of the Indian Constitution

The Constitution of India establishes the framework of governance for a sovereign democratic republic. It combines features drawn from different constitutional systems with principles shaped by India's freedom struggle, social diversity and historical experience.

Its distinctive character lies in the combination of parliamentary government, federal arrangements, fundamental rights, Directive Principles, an independent judiciary, universal adult franchise and constitutional mechanisms for social transformation.

1. Lengthiest Written Constitution

The Indian Constitution is one of the most detailed written national constitutions in the world. Its extensive provisions reflect the need to govern a large and diverse country and to establish detailed arrangements for its institutions.

  • It contains provisions on the Union, States, local government, citizenship, rights, duties, public services, elections and emergency administration.
  • It incorporates detailed administrative and institutional provisions that might be left to ordinary legislation in some other countries.
  • Its length has increased through constitutional amendments and the insertion of new provisions.

The original Constitution adopted in 1949 contained 395 Articles, 22 Parts and 8 Schedules. These figures describe the original text, not its present amended form.

2. Drawn from Various Sources

The Constitution-makers studied constitutional systems across the world and adapted suitable features to Indian conditions. The Constitution also drew extensively on the Government of India Act, 1935.

The borrowed provisions were not adopted mechanically. They were modified and integrated into a constitutional framework based on popular sovereignty and the needs of independent India.

The sources of individual provisions are examined in detail in the chapter on Sources of the Constitution.

3. Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic

The Preamble describes India as a sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic republic. These terms express the fundamental character and constitutional aspirations of the Indian State.

Sovereign

India is internally supreme and externally independent. It is not subject to the authority of any foreign power in its domestic or external affairs. Its constitutional authority derives from the people.

Socialist

The Constitution seeks social and economic justice and aims to reduce inequalities. The word "Socialist" was added to the Preamble by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976. Indian constitutional socialism operates within a democratic framework and does not prescribe a single economic model.

Secular

India has no state religion. The State must treat religions in accordance with constitutional principles and guarantee freedom of conscience and religion, subject to constitutional limitations. The word "Secular" was added to the Preamble by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976.

Democratic

Political authority ultimately rests with the people. Citizens participate in government through elections and representative institutions. Democracy also operates through constitutional rights, accountability and the rule of law.

Republic

The head of the Indian State is elected, directly or indirectly, for a specified term rather than holding office by hereditary succession. The President of India is the constitutional head of the Union.

4. Parliamentary Form of Government

India follows the parliamentary system of government at both the Union and State levels. The executive is drawn from the legislature and remains collectively responsible to the elected lower house.

  • The President is the constitutional head of the Union, while the Prime Minister heads the Council of Ministers.
  • The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha under Article 75(3).
  • At the State level, the Governor is the constitutional head and the Chief Minister heads the Council of Ministers.
  • The State Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly under Article 164(2).
  • The executive remains in office so long as it enjoys the confidence of the elected lower house.

The system promotes accountability of the political executive to the legislature. It also distinguishes the nominal executive from the real political executive.

5. Federal System with Unitary Features

The Constitution establishes a Union of States and distributes legislative and executive powers between the Union and the States. At the same time, it gives the Union significant powers to preserve national unity and respond to exceptional circumstances.

Federal features

  • Two levels of government: Union and States.
  • Constitutional distribution of legislative powers.
  • Written and supreme Constitution.
  • Independent judiciary to interpret the Constitution.
  • A partly rigid amendment procedure, requiring State ratification for specified federal provisions.

Unitary or centralising features

  • Single Constitution for most of the country.
  • Single citizenship.
  • Strong legislative and administrative powers of the Union.
  • Emergency provisions affecting the federal distribution of powers.
  • Parliament's power to alter the boundaries, areas and names of States under the Constitution.
  • Integrated judicial system and All India Services.

India is often described as having a federal structure with a strong Centre. The Supreme Court has recognised federalism as part of the Constitution's basic structure.

6. Blend of Rigidity and Flexibility

The Indian Constitution is neither wholly rigid nor wholly flexible. Different provisions can be amended through different procedures.

  • Certain provisions can be changed by Parliament through a simple majority under the ordinary legislative procedure.
  • Most constitutional provisions require a special majority in each House of Parliament under Article 368.
  • Amendments affecting specified federal provisions also require ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures.

This arrangement seeks to balance constitutional stability with the ability to respond to changing social and political circumstances.

7. Fundamental Rights

Part III of the Constitution guarantees Fundamental Rights. These rights protect individuals and groups against certain forms of arbitrary State action and provide essential conditions for democratic life.

  • Right to Equality (Articles 14–18).
  • Right to Freedom (Articles 19–22).
  • Right against Exploitation (Articles 23–24).
  • Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28).
  • Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29–30).
  • Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32).

Fundamental Rights are enforceable through courts. The right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights is guaranteed under Article 32.

Detailed provisions are covered in Fundamental Rights.

8. Directive Principles of State Policy

Part IV, Articles 36–51, contains the Directive Principles of State Policy. They provide guidelines for the State in making laws and policies to promote social and economic justice.

  • They aim to establish conditions for social and economic welfare.
  • They guide legislation and public policy.
  • They are not enforceable by courts, but Article 37 declares them fundamental in the governance of the country.

Their purpose is to advance a welfare-oriented constitutional order.

See Directive Principles of State Policy for detailed notes.

9. Fundamental Duties

Part IVA, Article 51A, sets out the Fundamental Duties of citizens. They express civic responsibilities and encourage respect for constitutional values, national institutions and the environment.

  • Fundamental Duties were introduced by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976.
  • They were initially ten in number.
  • The 86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002 added the eleventh duty relating to educational opportunities for children between six and fourteen years of age.
  • They are generally non-justiciable, though legislation may give effect to particular duties.

See Fundamental Duties for the complete list and constitutional background.

10. Independent and Integrated Judiciary

The Constitution establishes an independent judiciary to interpret the Constitution, protect rights and resolve disputes. India has an integrated judicial system with the Supreme Court at its apex.

  • The Supreme Court is the highest judicial authority.
  • High Courts function at the State level and have constitutional jurisdiction.
  • Subordinate courts operate under the supervision and control of High Courts.
  • Judicial independence is supported by safeguards relating to appointment, tenure, removal, remuneration and judicial functioning.
  • The judiciary exercises judicial review to examine the constitutional validity of laws and executive actions.

11. Judicial Review and Rule of Law

Judicial review enables constitutional courts to examine whether legislative and executive actions comply with the Constitution. Laws and actions that violate constitutional provisions may be declared invalid to the extent of the inconsistency.

The rule of law means that public power must be exercised according to law and constitutional limits. Government authorities are not above the law, and legal rights must be protected through fair procedures.

12. Universal Adult Franchise

Article 326 provides for elections to the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies on the basis of adult suffrage, subject to constitutional and statutory qualifications.

  • Every citizen who is at least 18 years old and is not otherwise disqualified under the law is entitled to be registered as a voter.
  • The voting age was reduced from 21 to 18 years by the 61st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1988.
  • Universal adult franchise promotes political equality and broad-based democratic participation.

13. Single Citizenship

India provides for single citizenship for the entire country. Unlike some federations where citizens may hold both national and state citizenship, an Indian citizen is a citizen of India and not separately a citizen of a particular State.

Single citizenship supports national unity and equality of citizenship across the country. Citizenship provisions are contained in Part II of the Constitution, while Parliament has power to regulate citizenship by law.

14. Emergency Provisions

The Constitution provides for exceptional arrangements to address threats to national security, constitutional governance and financial stability.

  • National Emergency: Article 352, relating to war, external aggression or armed rebellion.
  • President's Rule: Article 356, relating to failure of constitutional machinery in a State.
  • Financial Emergency: Article 360, relating to a threat to the financial stability or credit of India or any part of its territory.

These provisions can substantially affect the distribution of powers and must be exercised within constitutional limits.

15. Three-Tier System of Government

The Constitution recognises local self-government institutions as an important part of democratic decentralisation.

  • The 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992 gave constitutional status to Panchayati Raj Institutions.
  • The 74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992 gave constitutional status to urban local bodies, including Municipalities.
  • These provisions seek to promote democratic participation and decentralised governance at the grassroots level.

16. Special Provisions for Social Justice and Diversity

The Constitution recognises India's social and cultural diversity and provides safeguards and special arrangements for disadvantaged groups and particular regions.

  • Provisions for the advancement of Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and socially and educationally backward classes.
  • Reservation arrangements in legislatures, public employment and educational institutions, subject to constitutional provisions and laws.
  • Protection of linguistic and cultural rights.
  • Special administrative provisions for specified tribal areas and regions.
  • Constitutional recognition of multiple languages and safeguards for linguistic minorities.

17. Independent Constitutional Bodies

The Constitution establishes independent institutions to support accountability, impartial administration and democratic functioning.

  • Election Commission of India (Article 324).
  • Union and State Public Service Commissions (Articles 315–323).
  • Comptroller and Auditor General of India (Articles 148–151).
  • Finance Commission (Article 280).

These institutions perform specialised constitutional functions and help maintain checks and balances within the system of government.

18. Cooperative Federalism

The Constitution provides mechanisms through which the Union and States can coordinate policy and administration. Federal institutions and intergovernmental arrangements enable cooperation on matters of common concern.

Examples include the Inter-State Council under Article 263, the GST Council under Article 279A and the distribution of financial resources through the Finance Commission.

Points to Remember

  • India has a federal system with a strong Centre, rather than a purely unitary system.
  • The parliamentary executive is collectively responsible to the elected lower house.
  • Fundamental Rights are enforceable, while Directive Principles are non-justiciable but fundamental in governance.
  • The Constitution combines rigidity and flexibility through different amendment procedures.
  • The 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 added the words Socialist and Secular to the Preamble.
  • The 61st Amendment Act, 1988 reduced the voting age from 21 to 18 years.
  • The 73rd and 74th Amendment Acts, 1992 constitutionalised rural and urban local self-government institutions.
