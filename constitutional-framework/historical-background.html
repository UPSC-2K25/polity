Historical Background of the Indian Constitution

The Constitution of India emerged from a long process of constitutional, political and administrative change. Its institutional foundations developed during British rule, when the East India Company gradually transformed from a commercial organisation into a territorial power and eventually gave way to direct administration by the British Crown.

Between 1773 and 1947, successive British parliamentary enactments introduced centralised administration, legislative councils, limited representation, public services, provincial autonomy and federal arrangements. Indian political movements and demands for self-government also shaped this evolution. The Constitution-makers inherited some institutional arrangements but transformed them through popular sovereignty, universal adult franchise, Fundamental Rights and constitutional democracy.

1. Regulating Act, 1773

The Regulating Act, 1773 was the first major attempt by the British Parliament to regulate the administration of the East India Company in India. It followed concerns about the Company's financial crisis, administrative failures and misuse of political power, particularly in Bengal.

  • The Governor of Bengal was designated the Governor-General of Bengal. Warren Hastings became the first holder of this office under the new arrangement.
  • An Executive Council of four members was created to assist the Governor-General.
  • The Presidencies of Bombay and Madras were made subordinate to Bengal in specified matters, particularly war and peace, subject to exceptions.
  • A Supreme Court of Judicature was provided for at Fort William, Calcutta. It began functioning in 1774 with a Chief Justice and three other judges.
  • The Company was required to report its civil, military and revenue affairs to the British Government.

Significance: The Act established parliamentary oversight over Company administration and initiated the process of centralising British authority in India.

Limitation: Ambiguities in the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and conflicts between the Court, the Governor-General's Council and Company officials created administrative difficulties.

2. Act of Settlement, 1781

The Act of Settlement, 1781, also known as the Amending Act, was passed to address difficulties arising from the operation of the Regulating Act, particularly conflicts involving the Supreme Court at Calcutta.

  • It restricted the Supreme Court's jurisdiction over acts performed by the Governor-General and Council in their official capacities.
  • Revenue matters and specified official acts of the Company's administration were placed outside the Court's ordinary jurisdiction.
  • The Court was directed to respect the personal laws applicable to Hindus and Muslims in relevant matters.
  • It clarified aspects of the relationship between the Supreme Court and the Company's administrative authorities.

Significance: It attempted to reduce institutional conflict and clarify the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court in British India.

3. Pitt's India Act, 1784

Pitt's India Act was enacted to correct weaknesses in the Regulating Act and strengthen British Government supervision over the Company's political administration.

  • It distinguished the Company's commercial activities from its political and territorial functions.
  • A Board of Control was created to supervise the Company's civil, military and revenue affairs.
  • The Court of Directors continued to manage the Company's commercial affairs.
  • The Governor-General's authority over the Presidencies was strengthened.
  • The British Government obtained greater control over the Company's political decisions.

Significance: It introduced a system commonly described as dual control: the Company retained its administrative and commercial machinery, while the British Government exercised control over political affairs through the Board of Control.

4. Charter Act, 1793

The Charter Act of 1793 renewed the Company's charter for another twenty years and continued the existing framework of Company administration.

  • The Company's trade monopoly in India was continued.
  • The powers of the Governor-General and Governors were maintained and, in specified respects, strengthened.
  • The expenses of the Board of Control were placed on Indian revenues.
  • The Company's administrative arrangements under the system of dual control continued.

Significance: The Act largely continued the existing system rather than introducing a fundamental constitutional change. It is important as part of the sequence of Charter Acts governing the Company's authority.

5. Charter Act, 1813

The Charter Act of 1813 renewed the Company's charter for twenty years but significantly reduced its commercial monopoly.

  • The Company's monopoly over trade with India was ended, except for the trade in tea and trade with China.
  • The Company retained its political and administrative authority in India.
  • Missionaries were permitted to enter India under the arrangements established by the Act.
  • It provided for an annual allocation of one lakh rupees for the revival and improvement of literature and the encouragement of learned Indians, as well as the promotion of scientific knowledge.

Significance: It weakened the Company's commercial monopoly and introduced a formal provision for expenditure on education and knowledge.

6. Charter Act, 1833

The Charter Act of 1833, also known as the Saint Helena Act, marked a major stage in the centralisation of British Indian administration. It completed the transformation of the East India Company from a commercial organisation into an administrative agency.

  • The Governor-General of Bengal became the Governor-General of India. Lord William Bentinck was the first to hold this office.
  • The legislative powers of the Bombay and Madras Presidencies were withdrawn and centralised in the Governor-General-in-Council.
  • The Company was deprived of its remaining commercial functions.
  • A Law Member was added to the Governor-General's Council for legislative purposes. Thomas Babington Macaulay was the first Law Member.
  • The Act provided for the establishment of a Law Commission to examine and consolidate Indian laws. The first Law Commission was constituted in 1834 under Macaulay.
  • It declared that no person in India should be disqualified from holding a Company office on grounds of religion, place of birth, descent or colour, subject to qualifications prescribed for the office.

Significance: It strengthened central legislative authority, advanced the development of an all-India administrative system and introduced an important principle of non-discrimination in eligibility for public employment.

7. Charter Act, 1853

The Charter Act of 1853 was the last of the Charter Acts. Unlike earlier Charter Acts, it did not renew the Company's rule for a fixed twenty-year period.

  • It separated the legislative and executive functions of the Governor-General's Council by adding legislative members for law-making purposes.
  • It introduced the principle of open competition for recruitment to the civil services, although the competitive system was developed through subsequent arrangements.
  • It expanded legislative representation by including members from additional provinces and presidencies.
  • It provided for a separate Lieutenant-Governor for Bengal.

Significance: It advanced the separation of legislative work from executive administration and laid an important foundation for merit-based civil-service recruitment.

8. Government of India Act, 1858

The Revolt of 1857 exposed serious weaknesses in Company administration. The British Parliament responded by ending Company rule and transferring the governance of India directly to the British Crown.

  • The administration of India was transferred from the East India Company to the British Crown.
  • The office of Secretary of State for India was created. The Secretary of State was a member of the British Cabinet and was assisted by a Council of India.
  • The Board of Control and the Court of Directors were abolished, ending the earlier system of dual control.
  • The Governor-General also came to be known as the Viceroy, representing the British Crown. Lord Canning became the first Viceroy of India.
  • The Company's remaining governing authority was brought to an end.

Significance: The Act ended Company rule and established direct Crown administration, beginning a new phase in India's colonial constitutional history.

9. Indian Councils Act, 1861

The Indian Councils Act of 1861 introduced changes in the legislative and executive arrangements following the transfer of power to the Crown.

  • It enlarged the Governor-General's legislative council by adding members, including non-official members.
  • It recognised the portfolio system, under which different members of the Executive Council were assigned responsibility for particular departments.
  • It restored legislative powers to the Bombay and Madras Presidencies, beginning a process of legislative decentralisation.
  • It empowered the Governor-General to establish legislative councils in other provinces.
  • It enabled the nomination of Indians to legislative councils, introducing limited Indian participation in law-making.
  • It recognised the Governor-General's power to issue ordinances during emergencies, subject to the conditions prescribed by law.

Significance: It encouraged limited Indian participation in legislation, recognised departmental administration through the portfolio system and began reversing excessive legislative centralisation.

10. Indian Councils Act, 1892

The Indian Councils Act of 1892 expanded legislative councils and provided limited opportunities for discussion of public finances and government policies.

  • It increased the number of additional members in the central and provincial legislative councils.
  • Members were permitted to discuss the annual financial statement, subject to restrictions.
  • Members could ask questions on matters of public interest, subject to prescribed conditions.
  • It introduced an indirect electoral element through recommendations made by specified local bodies, universities, chambers of commerce and other organisations for nomination to legislative councils.

Significance: It marked a limited expansion of representative participation and legislative discussion, although the councils remained largely under official control.

11. Indian Councils Act, 1909

Also known as the Morley-Minto Reforms, the Indian Councils Act of 1909 expanded legislative councils and introduced separate electorates for Muslims.

  • The size of the central and provincial legislative councils was increased.
  • Separate electorates were introduced for Muslims, allowing Muslim voters to elect representatives to designated seats.
  • Indians were included in the Executive Councils. Satyendra Prasanna Sinha became the first Indian member of the Viceroy's Executive Council.
  • Legislative councils received greater opportunities to discuss budgets and resolutions, although their powers remained limited.

Significance: It expanded legislative participation but institutionalised communal representation through separate electorates, contributing to the development of communal divisions in electoral politics.

12. Government of India Act, 1919

Also known as the Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms, the Government of India Act, 1919 introduced dyarchy in the provinces and expanded representative institutions at the central level.

  • Provincial subjects were divided into reserved and transferred subjects. Reserved subjects were administered by the Governor and his Executive Council, while transferred subjects were administered by ministers responsible to the provincial legislative council.
  • A bicameral legislature was established at the Centre, consisting of the Council of State and the Legislative Assembly.
  • Direct elections were introduced on a wider scale, although the franchise remained restricted by qualifications.
  • Separate electorates were extended to additional communities and interests.
  • The Act provided for the establishment of a Public Service Commission. The first Public Service Commission in India was established in 1926.
  • Provincial budgets were separated from the central budget.
  • It provided for a statutory commission to review the working of the constitutional reforms after ten years. This led to the appointment of the Simon Commission.

Significance: It introduced dyarchy at the provincial level, established bicameralism at the Centre and widened the limited electoral framework.

Limitation: The division of provincial subjects created administrative difficulties. Governors retained significant powers, and the system did not establish responsible government at the provincial level.

13. Government of India Act, 1935

The Government of India Act, 1935 was the most extensive constitutional legislation enacted by the British Parliament for India. Several of its institutional arrangements influenced the Constitution of independent India.

  • It proposed an All-India Federation consisting of British Indian provinces and princely states. The federation did not come into operation because the required participation of princely states was not secured.
  • It abolished dyarchy in the provinces and introduced provincial autonomy.
  • It proposed dyarchy at the Centre, but this provision was never brought into operation.
  • Legislative powers were divided into three lists: the Federal List, Provincial List and Concurrent List.
  • It provided for bicameral legislatures in six provinces.
  • It provided for the establishment of a Federal Court, which began functioning in 1937.
  • It provided for the establishment of a Reserve Bank of India. The RBI began operations in 1935 under the separate Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934.
  • It provided for Federal, Provincial and Joint Public Service Commissions.
  • It extended the franchise, although voting rights remained restricted by property, tax, educational and other qualifications.
  • It introduced safeguards and special responsibilities for Governors and the Governor-General, limiting the practical scope of responsible government.

Significance: It provided a detailed framework for federal administration, provincial autonomy, legislative distribution of powers, public services and judicial institutions. Several structural features of the present Constitution have historical links with this Act.

Limitation: The proposed federation never came into operation, and the Governor-General and Governors retained extensive discretionary and special powers. The Act did not establish full responsible government at the Centre.

14. Indian Independence Act, 1947

The Indian Independence Act, 1947 provided the legal framework for the end of British rule and the creation of two independent Dominions, India and Pakistan.

  • British rule in India ended on 15 August 1947.
  • Two independent Dominions, India and Pakistan, were created.
  • The office of the Secretary of State for India was abolished.
  • The Constituent Assemblies of the two Dominions were empowered to frame their respective Constitutions and exercise legislative authority.
  • The British Crown's suzerainty over princely states lapsed, ending the earlier paramountcy arrangements.
  • The Constituent Assembly of India became a sovereign body and exercised legislative authority for the Dominion.

Significance: It ended British political authority in India and enabled the Constituent Assembly to frame and adopt an independent Constitution.

Evolution of Constitutional Institutions

The historical Acts can also be understood through the gradual evolution of major constitutional institutions and principles.

Centralisation and decentralisation

  • The Regulating Act, 1773 initiated parliamentary supervision and a stronger central administrative structure.
  • The Charter Act, 1833 centralised legislative authority in the Governor-General-in-Council.
  • The Indian Councils Act, 1861 restored legislative powers to the Bombay and Madras Presidencies.
  • The Government of India Act, 1935 established a detailed distribution of legislative powers between different levels of government.

Growth of representative institutions

  • The Indian Councils Act, 1861 enabled limited nomination of Indians to legislative councils.
  • The Indian Councils Act, 1892 expanded legislative discussion and introduced an indirect electoral element.
  • The Indian Councils Act, 1909 expanded councils and introduced separate electorates for Muslims.
  • The Government of India Act, 1919 introduced bicameralism at the Centre and widened the electoral framework.
  • The Government of India Act, 1935 introduced provincial autonomy, though under significant colonial safeguards.

Evolution of public services

  • The Charter Act, 1853 introduced the principle of open competition for civil-service recruitment.
  • The Government of India Act, 1919 provided for a Public Service Commission.
  • The first Public Service Commission in India was established in 1926.
  • The Government of India Act, 1935 provided for Federal, Provincial and Joint Public Service Commissions.

Evolution of judicial institutions

  • The Regulating Act, 1773 provided for the Supreme Court at Calcutta, which began functioning in 1774.
  • The Act of Settlement, 1781 clarified aspects of the Supreme Court's jurisdiction.
  • The Government of India Act, 1935 provided for a Federal Court, which began functioning in 1937.
  • The Federal Court formed an important institutional link in the historical development of India's higher judiciary.

Influence on the Constitution of India

The Constitution-makers drew upon India's administrative and constitutional experience while adapting inherited institutions to the requirements of an independent, democratic republic.

These continuities should not be mistaken for simple copying. The Constitution-makers transformed the inherited framework through popular sovereignty, universal adult franchise, Fundamental Rights, parliamentary responsibility, an independent judiciary, constitutional supremacy and a commitment to social and economic justice.

Critical Assessment

The constitutional development of British India was gradual and largely controlled by colonial interests. Reforms expanded administrative efficiency and limited representative participation, but they did not establish full democratic self-government.

  • Centralisation: Colonial administration increasingly concentrated authority in the hands of British institutions.
  • Limited representation: Legislative councils expanded over time, but franchise and participation remained restricted.
  • Communal electorates: Separate electorates institutionalised religious divisions in electoral representation.
  • Administrative continuity: Several institutions and procedures developed during colonial rule influenced the post-independence administrative framework.
  • Nationalist influence: Indian political movements, constitutional demands and debates increasingly shaped the direction of constitutional change.

The central constitutional transformation in 1947–1950 was the shift from colonial authority to a sovereign political order founded on the people of India.
