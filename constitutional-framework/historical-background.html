Historical Background of the Indian Constitution

The Indian Constitution did not emerge in isolation. Its institutional framework developed through a long process of constitutional and administrative changes during British rule. These changes gradually transformed the East India Company from a commercial organisation into a territorial power and later brought India under direct British Crown administration.

The constitutional development of British India can be understood through the major Acts passed by the British Parliament between 1773 and 1947. These Acts introduced institutions and principles that influenced the making of independent India's Constitution.

1. Regulating Act, 1773

The Regulating Act of 1773 was the first major step taken by the British Parliament to regulate the affairs of the East India Company in India. It followed concerns about the Company's administration, financial difficulties and misuse of political power in Bengal.

  • The Governor of Bengal was designated the Governor-General of Bengal. Warren Hastings became the first Governor-General under this arrangement.
  • An Executive Council of four members was created to assist the Governor-General.
  • The Governors of Bombay and Madras were made subordinate to the Governor-General of Bengal in matters of war and peace, subject to specified exceptions.
  • A Supreme Court was established at Calcutta in 1774, consisting of a Chief Justice and three other judges.
  • The Company was required to submit reports on its civil, military and revenue affairs to the British Government.

Significance: It established parliamentary oversight over the Company's administration and introduced the beginnings of a centralised administrative structure.

2. Pitt's India Act, 1784

Pitt's India Act was passed to address weaknesses in the Regulating Act and establish more effective control over the Company's political activities.

  • It distinguished the Company's commercial functions from its political functions.
  • A Board of Control was established to supervise the Company's civil, military and revenue affairs.
  • The Court of Directors continued to manage the Company's commercial affairs.
  • The Governor-General's control over the Presidencies was strengthened.

Significance: It created a system of dual control in which the Company retained its administrative machinery while the British Government exercised greater control over political matters.

3. Charter Act, 1813

The Charter Act of 1813 renewed the Company's charter for another twenty years but significantly reduced its commercial monopoly.

  • The Company's monopoly over trade with India was ended, except for trade in tea and trade with China.
  • The Company continued to retain its political and administrative authority in India.
  • Christian missionaries were permitted to come to India for missionary activities, subject to the prescribed arrangements.
  • The Act provided for an annual allocation of one lakh rupees for the promotion of education in India.

Significance: It marked an important stage in the gradual ending of the Company's commercial monopoly and introduced a formal provision for educational expenditure.

4. Charter Act, 1833

The Charter Act of 1833 brought about a major change in the administrative structure of British India. It centralised legislative authority and completed the transformation of the East India Company into an administrative agency.

  • The Governor-General of Bengal became the Governor-General of India. Lord William Bentinck was the first to hold this office.
  • The legislative powers of the Bombay and Madras Presidencies were withdrawn and centralised in the Governor-General-in-Council.
  • The East India Company was deprived of its remaining commercial functions and became an administrative body.
  • A Law Member was added to the Governor-General's Executive Council for legislative purposes. Thomas Babington Macaulay was the first Law Member.
  • The Act declared that no Indian subject of the Company should be disqualified from holding office on grounds of religion, place of birth, descent or colour, subject to the qualifications prescribed for the office.

Significance: It established a stronger central legislative authority and laid the foundation for an all-India administrative structure.

5. Charter Act, 1853

The Charter Act of 1853 was the last of the Charter Acts. Unlike earlier Charter Acts, it did not fix a specific twenty-year period for the continuation of the Company's rule.

  • It separated, for legislative purposes, the Governor-General's Executive Council from its legislative functions by adding legislative members.
  • It introduced an open competition system for recruitment to the civil services, laying the foundation for a merit-based system.
  • It allowed the legislative council to include representatives from additional provinces and presidencies.
  • It provided for the appointment of a separate Lieutenant-Governor for Bengal.

Significance: It introduced important legislative and civil-service changes and marked a further stage in the development of representative legislative institutions.

6. Government of India Act, 1858

The Revolt of 1857 exposed serious weaknesses in the Company's administration. The British Parliament responded by ending Company rule and transferring the governance of India directly to the British Crown.

  • The administration of India was transferred from the East India Company to the British Crown.
  • The office of Secretary of State for India was created. The Secretary of State was a member of the British Cabinet and was assisted by a Council of India.
  • The designation of Governor-General of India was supplemented by the title Viceroy, representing the British Crown. Lord Canning became the first Viceroy of India.
  • The Board of Control and Court of Directors were abolished, ending the earlier system of dual control.

Significance: It ended the rule of the East India Company and established direct Crown rule in India.

7. Indian Councils Act, 1861

The Indian Councils Act of 1861 introduced changes in the legislative and executive arrangements following the transfer of power to the Crown.

  • It enlarged the Governor-General's legislative council by including additional members, some of whom could be non-officials.
  • It recognised the portfolio system, under which different members of the Executive Council were assigned responsibility for particular departments.
  • It restored legislative powers to the Bombay and Madras Presidencies, beginning a process of decentralisation.
  • It empowered the Governor-General to establish legislative councils in other provinces.
  • It provided for the establishment of legislative councils in new provinces and for the nomination of Indians to legislative councils.

Significance: It encouraged limited Indian participation in legislation and began reversing the excessive centralisation introduced earlier.

8. Indian Councils Act, 1892

The Indian Councils Act of 1892 expanded legislative councils and provided limited opportunities for discussion of public finances and government policies.

  • It increased the number of additional members in the central and provincial legislative councils.
  • Members were permitted to discuss the annual financial statement, subject to restrictions.
  • Members could ask questions on matters of public interest, subject to prescribed conditions.
  • It introduced an indirect electoral element through recommendations made by specified bodies and organisations for nomination to legislative councils.

Significance: It marked a limited expansion of representative participation, although the councils remained largely under official control.

9. Indian Councils Act, 1909

Also known as the Morley-Minto Reforms, the Indian Councils Act of 1909 expanded legislative councils and introduced separate electorates for Muslims.

  • The size of the central and provincial legislative councils was increased.
  • Separate electorates were introduced for Muslims, allowing Muslim voters to elect their representatives to designated seats.
  • Indians were included in the Executive Councils. Satyendra Prasanna Sinha became the first Indian member of the Viceroy's Executive Council.
  • Legislative councils received greater opportunities to discuss budgets and resolutions, though their powers remained limited.

Significance: It expanded legislative participation but institutionalised communal representation through separate electorates.

10. Government of India Act, 1919

Also known as the Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms, the Government of India Act, 1919 introduced a system of dyarchy in the provinces and expanded representative institutions at the central level.

  • Provincial subjects were divided into reserved and transferred subjects. Reserved subjects were administered by the Governor and his Executive Council, while transferred subjects were administered by ministers responsible to the provincial legislative council.
  • A bicameral legislature was established at the Centre, consisting of the Council of State and the Legislative Assembly.
  • The system of direct elections was introduced on a wider scale, although the franchise remained limited.
  • Separate electorates were extended to additional communities and interests.
  • The Act provided for the establishment of a Public Service Commission. The first Public Service Commission in India was established in 1926.
  • It provided for the separation of provincial budgets from the central budget.
  • A statutory commission was to review the working of the reforms after ten years. This led to the appointment of the Simon Commission.

Significance: It introduced dyarchy at the provincial level and established a bicameral legislature at the Centre, while expanding the limited electoral framework.

11. Government of India Act, 1935

The Government of India Act, 1935 was the most extensive constitutional legislation enacted by the British Parliament for India. Several of its institutional provisions influenced the Constitution of independent India.

  • It proposed an All-India Federation consisting of British Indian provinces and princely states. The federation did not come into operation because the required participation of princely states was not secured.
  • It abolished dyarchy in the provinces and introduced provincial autonomy.
  • It proposed dyarchy at the Centre, but this provision was never brought into operation.
  • Legislative powers were divided into three lists: Federal, Provincial and Concurrent.
  • It provided for bicameral legislatures in six provinces.
  • It provided for the establishment of a Federal Court, which began functioning in 1937.
  • It provided for the establishment of the Reserve Bank of India, which commenced operations in 1935 under separate legislation.
  • It provided for the establishment of Federal, Provincial and Joint Public Service Commissions.
  • It extended the franchise, although voting rights continued to be restricted by property, tax, educational and other qualifications.

Significance: It provided a detailed framework for federal administration, provincial autonomy, legislative distribution of powers and public services. Many administrative and federal features of the present Constitution have historical links with this Act.

12. Indian Independence Act, 1947

The Indian Independence Act, 1947 provided the legal framework for the end of British rule and the creation of two independent Dominions, India and Pakistan.

  • British rule in India ended on 15 August 1947.
  • Two independent Dominions, India and Pakistan, were created.
  • The office of the Secretary of State for India was abolished.
  • The Constituent Assemblies of the two Dominions were empowered to act as their legislative bodies and to frame their respective Constitutions.
  • The British Crown's suzerainty over princely states lapsed, subject to the arrangements accompanying independence.
  • The Constituent Assembly of India became a sovereign body and exercised legislative authority for the Dominion.

Significance: It ended British political authority in India and enabled the Constituent Assembly to frame and adopt an independent Constitution.

Influence on the Constitution of India

The Constitution of India drew upon the institutional experience of British India while adapting it to the requirements of a sovereign, democratic republic. Important continuities include:

  • The federal scheme and distribution of legislative powers were influenced substantially by the Government of India Act, 1935.
  • The office of Governor and several administrative arrangements have historical links with the colonial provincial system.
  • Public Service Commission arrangements developed through earlier constitutional legislation.
  • The parliamentary system evolved through the gradual development of legislative councils and responsible government institutions.
  • Judicial institutions developed through the colonial court system, including the Federal Court established under the Government of India Act, 1935.

The Constitution-makers did not simply reproduce colonial institutions. They adapted and transformed them within a framework of popular sovereignty, fundamental rights, universal adult franchise, parliamentary democracy and constitutional supremacy.

Important Points for Revision

  • 1773: First major parliamentary intervention in the Company's Indian administration.
  • 1784: Board of Control established under Pitt's India Act.
  • 1833: Governor-General of Bengal became Governor-General of India.
  • 1858: Company rule ended and Crown rule began.
  • 1909: Separate electorates for Muslims introduced.
  • 1919: Dyarchy introduced in provinces.
  • 1935: Provincial autonomy and three legislative lists provided.
  • 1947: Constituent Assembly became sovereign in its constitution-making role.
