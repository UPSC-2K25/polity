Schedules of the Indian Constitution

Schedules are supplementary components of the Constitution. They contain detailed lists, allocations, forms, territorial descriptions and institutional arrangements that support the substantive Articles.

The original Constitution contained eight Schedules. Four additional Schedules were subsequently added, taking the total to twelve. The Schedules cover subjects ranging from the territorial organisation of India and distribution of legislative powers to tribal administration, recognised languages, anti-defection and local government.

A Schedule is not an independent code. Its provisions must be read with the Articles that refer to it and with relevant constitutional amendments, legislation and judicial decisions.

2. First Schedule: States and Union Territories

The First Schedule contains the names of the States and Union Territories of India and describes their territorial extent. It gives effect to the territorial organisation contemplated by Article 1.

Constitutional connection

  • Article 1: Declares India, that is Bharat, to be a Union of States and refers to the territories of India.
  • Article 2: Empowers Parliament to admit into the Union, or establish, new States.
  • Article 3: Empowers Parliament to form new States and alter the areas, boundaries or names of existing States.
  • Article 4: Permits supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions in laws made under Articles 2 and 3.

Changes to the First Schedule may follow the admission or establishment of States and the reorganisation of existing territorial units.

A law under Article 3 is not treated as a constitutional amendment under Article 368. However, the prescribed procedure, including reference of the proposal to the affected State legislature for its views, must be followed.

See Federalism and Decentralisation for the Union's territorial and federal structure.

3. Second Schedule: Emoluments and Related Provisions

The Second Schedule contains provisions relating to the emoluments, allowances, privileges and other service-related matters of specified constitutional authorities.

It includes provisions concerning:

  • The President and Governors.
  • The Speaker and Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha.
  • The Chairman and Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha.
  • Presiding officers of State Legislatures.
  • Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts.
  • The Comptroller and Auditor-General of India.

The relevant constitutional provisions and laws govern the actual amounts, conditions and entitlements. The Schedule should therefore be read with the applicable Articles and legislation rather than treated as a complete current pay-scale list.

4. Third Schedule: Oaths and Affirmations

The Third Schedule prescribes the forms of oaths and affirmations for specified constitutional offices and elected representatives.

It includes forms for:

  • Union Ministers.
  • Candidates for election to Parliament.
  • Members of Parliament.
  • Judges of the Supreme Court.
  • The Comptroller and Auditor-General of India.
  • State Ministers.
  • Candidates for election to State Legislatures.
  • Members of State Legislatures.
  • Judges of High Courts.

The oaths of the President and Governors are prescribed separately in Articles 60 and 159 respectively, and are not included in the Third Schedule.

Oaths are constitutionally significant because they formally affirm allegiance to the Constitution and commitment to discharge the responsibilities of the relevant office.

5. Fourth Schedule: Allocation of Rajya Sabha Seats

The Fourth Schedule provides for the allocation of seats to States and eligible Union Territories in the Council of States, or Rajya Sabha.

  • It is connected with Article 80, which deals with the composition of the Council of States.
  • The allocation is broadly based on population, subject to constitutional provisions.
  • The Schedule reflects the representation of States and eligible Union Territories in the federal second chamber.

The Fourth Schedule should be distinguished from the procedure for electing Rajya Sabha members. That procedure is governed by Article 80 and relevant election legislation, including the system of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote for elected members.

6. Fifth Schedule: Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes

The Fifth Schedule deals with the administration and control of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes in States other than the tribal areas governed under the Sixth Schedule.

It is linked to Article 244(1) and provides a special framework for the administration of Scheduled Areas.

Important features

  • The President may declare an area to be a Scheduled Area and may alter its boundaries or discontinue its designation in accordance with the constitutional framework.
  • The Governor has special responsibilities concerning the administration of Scheduled Areas.
  • A Tribes Advisory Council is provided for in States having Scheduled Areas. It may also be established in certain other States having Scheduled Tribes, as provided by the Schedule.
  • The Governor may make regulations for the peace and good government of Scheduled Areas, subject to the prescribed constitutional requirements.
  • The Governor submits reports to the President concerning the administration of Scheduled Areas.
  • The Union has an executive responsibility to give directions to States regarding their administration.

Tribes Advisory Council

The Tribes Advisory Council advises on matters concerning the welfare and advancement of Scheduled Tribes in the State, as referred to it by the Governor.

The Council ordinarily consists of not more than twenty members, with as nearly as possible three-fourths of its members being representatives of Scheduled Tribes in the State Legislative Assembly, subject to the provisions of the Schedule.

Relationship with PESA

The Fifth Schedule should be studied alongside the Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA). PESA extends the Panchayati Raj framework to Fifth Schedule areas with special safeguards for Gram Sabhas, customary practices and community resources.

The constitutional objective is to combine democratic decentralisation with protection of tribal identity, autonomy and community interests.

See PESA and Tribal Self-Governance when that chapter is available in the Federalism module.

7. Sixth Schedule: Tribal Areas in Four States

The Sixth Schedule provides a special administrative framework for specified tribal areas in:

  • Assam
  • Meghalaya
  • Tripura
  • Mizoram

It provides for Autonomous District Councils and, where applicable, Regional Councils. These institutions enable a degree of local self-government within the constitutional framework.

Autonomous District Councils

The Sixth Schedule provides for Autonomous District Councils in specified tribal areas. Regional Councils may also be constituted for specified areas within an autonomous district.

Legislative powers

Councils may make laws on specified subjects, including matters relating to land, non-reserved forests, shifting cultivation, village administration, inheritance, marriage and social customs, subject to the constitutional limitations and approval requirements applicable to the particular provision.

Judicial powers

The Schedule provides for the establishment of village and other local courts for specified disputes involving members of Scheduled Tribes, subject to constitutional and statutory conditions.

Financial and executive powers

  • Councils may levy specified taxes and collect certain revenues.
  • They may receive grants and other financial assistance under the constitutional framework.
  • They may manage specified local institutions and resources.

The Sixth Schedule seeks to protect tribal autonomy, customary practices and local self-government. Its institutional design is distinct from the Fifth Schedule framework.

8. Seventh Schedule: Distribution of Legislative Powers

The Seventh Schedule distributes legislative subjects between Parliament and State Legislatures through three lists. It is principally connected with Article 246 and must be read with other provisions governing legislative competence.

A. Union List

The Union List contains subjects on which Parliament ordinarily has exclusive legislative competence, subject to the Constitution.

Examples include:

  • Defence and armed forces.
  • Foreign affairs.
  • Atomic energy.
  • Railways.
  • Currency and coinage.
  • Banking.
  • Citizenship and naturalisation.
  • Inter-State trade and commerce.

B. State List

The State List contains subjects on which State Legislatures ordinarily have exclusive legislative competence, subject to constitutional exceptions.

Examples include:

  • Public order.
  • Police.
  • Public health and sanitation.
  • Agriculture.
  • Land.
  • Local government.
  • Markets and fairs.

C. Concurrent List

Both Parliament and State Legislatures can ordinarily legislate on Concurrent List subjects. In case of repugnancy between a parliamentary law and a State law on a Concurrent List matter, Article 254 generally gives precedence to the parliamentary law, subject to its constitutional exceptions.

Examples include:

  • Criminal law and criminal procedure.
  • Marriage and divorce.
  • Education.
  • Forests.
  • Trade unions and labour welfare.
  • Electricity.
  • Economic and social planning.

Important constitutional qualifications

The distribution of legislative powers is not absolute. Parliament may legislate on State List subjects in specified circumstances, including:

  • A resolution of the Rajya Sabha under Article 249 in the national interest.
  • During a Proclamation of Emergency, under Article 250.
  • When two or more States consent under Article 252.
  • To implement international treaties, agreements and conventions under Article 253.
  • During President's Rule, under Article 356, subject to the constitutional framework.

Changes made by constitutional amendments

The Seventh Schedule has been modified through constitutional amendments. The 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 transferred certain subjects from the State List to the Concurrent List, including:

  • Education.
  • Forests.
  • Protection of wild animals and birds.
  • Weights and measures, except establishment of standards.
  • Administration of justice and organisation of courts, subject to the constitutional entry.

The distribution of powers is central to Indian federalism. It should be understood alongside financial relations, administrative relations and the residuary legislative power of Parliament.

See Federalism and Decentralisation.

Constitutional amendments relating to the Eighth Schedule

  • The original Constitution included 14 languages.
  • Sindhi was added by the 21st Amendment Act, 1967.
  • Konkani, Manipuri and Nepali were added by the 71st Amendment Act, 1992.
  • Bodo, Dogri, Maithili and Santali were added by the 92nd Amendment Act, 2003.
  • The 96th Amendment Act, 2011 changed the name "Oriya" to "Odia".

Inclusion in the Eighth Schedule does not by itself make a language the national language or the official language of every State. The Constitution does not declare any language to be the national language of India.

The Eighth Schedule should be distinguished from the official language provisions in Part XVII and from the powers of States to adopt official languages under Article 345.

10. Ninth Schedule: Protection of Specified Laws

The Ninth Schedule was added by the First Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951. It is associated with Article 31B.

It was originally intended to protect specified laws, particularly land reform legislation, from being invalidated on certain Fundamental Rights grounds.

Article 31B

Article 31B provides special protection to Acts and Regulations included in the Ninth Schedule, subject to the constitutional limitations recognised by the Supreme Court.

Judicial limitation: I.R. Coelho

In I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007), the Supreme Court held that laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 are open to judicial review if their impact damages or destroys the basic structure of the Constitution.

The relevant inquiry concerns the effect of the law on constitutional rights and basic structure principles, rather than merely its formal inclusion in the Ninth Schedule.

Therefore, Ninth Schedule inclusion does not create absolute immunity from judicial review.

11. Tenth Schedule: Anti-Defection Law

The Tenth Schedule was inserted by the 52nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1985. It deals with disqualification of members of Parliament and State Legislatures on grounds of defection.

It is linked to Articles 102(2) and 191(2).

Grounds of disqualification

  • A member belonging to a political party voluntarily gives up membership of that party.
  • A member votes or abstains from voting in the House contrary to a direction issued by the political party or an authorised person, without prior permission, and the action is not condoned within fifteen days.
  • An independently elected member joins a political party after the election.
  • A nominated member joins a political party after the expiry of six months from the date on which the member takes the seat.

Merger exception

Disqualification on the ground of defection does not apply in the constitutionally specified case of a merger where at least two-thirds of the members of the legislature party agree to the merger with another political party.

The earlier provision allowing a split by one-third of the members was removed by the 91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003.

Decision-making authority

The Speaker or Chairman of the relevant House decides questions of disqualification under the Schedule. The decision is subject to judicial review, as recognised in Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992).

Constitutional issues and debates

  • The balance between political stability and freedom of legislative deliberation.
  • The role of the Speaker or Chairman as a decision-maker in politically sensitive disputes.
  • Delays in deciding disqualification petitions.
  • The relationship between party discipline and the representative role of legislators.
  • The scope of judicial review and the timing of intervention.

See Anti-Defection Law in the Elections module for detailed treatment.

12. Eleventh Schedule: Subjects for Panchayats

The Eleventh Schedule was added by the 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992. It contains 29 subjects that may be entrusted to Panchayats by State Legislatures under Article 243G.

  1. Agriculture, including agricultural extension.
  2. Land improvement, implementation of land reforms, land consolidation and soil conservation.
  3. Minor irrigation, water management and watershed development.
  4. Animal husbandry, dairying and poultry.
  5. Fisheries.
  6. Social forestry and farm forestry.
  7. Minor forest produce.
  8. Small-scale industries, including food-processing industries.
  9. Khadi, village and cottage industries.
  10. Rural housing.
  11. Drinking water.
  12. Fuel and fodder.
  13. Roads, culverts, bridges, ferries, waterways and other means of communication.
  14. Rural electrification, including distribution of electricity.
  15. Non-conventional energy sources.
  16. Poverty alleviation programmes.
  17. Education, including primary and secondary schools.
  18. Technical training and vocational education.
  19. Adult and non-formal education.
  20. Libraries.
  21. Cultural activities.
  22. Markets and fairs.
  23. Health and sanitation, including hospitals, primary health centres and dispensaries.
  24. Family welfare.
  25. Women and child development.
  26. Social welfare, including welfare of persons with disabilities.
  27. Welfare of weaker sections, particularly Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
  28. Public distribution system.
  29. Maintenance of community assets.

The Eleventh Schedule does not automatically transfer all 29 subjects to Panchayats. Article 243G enables State Legislatures to endow Panchayats with powers and responsibilities through law.

The actual extent of devolution therefore depends on State legislation, institutional capacity and the transfer of functions, funds and functionaries.

13. Twelfth Schedule: Subjects for Municipalities

The Twelfth Schedule was added by the 74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992. It contains 18 subjects that may be entrusted to Municipalities under Article 243W.

  1. Urban planning, including town planning.
  2. Regulation of land use and construction of buildings.
  3. Planning for economic and social development.
  4. Roads and bridges.
  5. Water supply for domestic, industrial and commercial purposes.
  6. Public health, sanitation, conservancy and solid waste management.
  7. Fire services.
  8. Urban forestry, protection of the environment and promotion of ecological aspects.
  9. Safeguarding the interests of weaker sections, including persons with disabilities.
  10. Slum improvement and upgradation.
  11. Urban poverty alleviation.
  12. Provision of urban amenities and facilities such as parks, gardens and playgrounds.
  13. Promotion of cultural, educational and aesthetic aspects.
  14. Burials and burial grounds, cremations, cremation grounds and electric crematoriums.
  15. Cattle pounds and prevention of cruelty to animals.
  16. Vital statistics, including registration of births and deaths.
  17. Public amenities, including street lighting, parking lots, bus stops and public conveniences.
  18. Regulation of slaughterhouses and tanneries.

As with the Eleventh Schedule, the actual devolution of functions depends on State legislation and the constitutional framework.

The Twelfth Schedule should be read alongside provisions concerning Metropolitan Planning Committees, District Planning Committees and municipal finances.

15. Important Supreme Court Judgments

Case Year Constitutional significance
Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu 1992 Recognised judicial review of decisions under the Tenth Schedule, subject to the principles laid down by the Court.
I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu 2007 Held that post-24 April 1973 Ninth Schedule insertions are open to basic-structure review.
Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah 2021 Considered the validity of the 97th Amendment's provisions concerning co-operative societies and the requirement of State ratification.

Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah (2021)

The Supreme Court considered the 97th Constitutional Amendment, which inserted Part IXB concerning co-operative societies. The Court held that the amendment required ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures insofar as it affected the legislative and executive powers of States over co-operative societies operating within their respective jurisdictions.

Since the required ratification had not been obtained, the relevant provisions were invalidated in that application. The provisions concerning multi-State co-operative societies and Union Territories were not invalidated on that ground.

16. Important Distinctions

  • First Schedule and Article 3: The Schedule records territorial arrangements; Article 3 provides the legislative mechanism for specified territorial changes.
  • Fifth and Sixth Schedules: Both provide special arrangements for tribal areas, but their institutional structures and territorial application differ.
  • Seventh Schedule and Article 246: The Schedule lists legislative subjects; Article 246 establishes the principal distribution of legislative competence.
  • Eighth Schedule and Part XVII: The Eighth Schedule lists languages; Part XVII contains the constitutional framework for official language.
  • Ninth Schedule and Article 31B: The Schedule identifies specified laws; Article 31B provides the related constitutional protection, subject to basic-structure review.
  • Tenth Schedule and Articles 102/191: The Schedule contains the grounds and procedure for defection-related disqualification; the Articles connect these grounds to membership of Parliament and State Legislatures.
  • Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules: They identify subjects that may be entrusted to rural and urban local bodies; they do not themselves guarantee complete devolution.

17. Analytical Significance

Schedules as instruments of constitutional detail

Schedules allow the Constitution to accommodate detailed territorial, administrative and institutional arrangements without placing every specification in the main body of the Articles.

Federal balance

The First, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth and Seventh Schedules have important implications for territorial organisation, representation, tribal autonomy and distribution of legislative powers.

Social justice and representation

The Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules contribute to constitutional objectives involving protection of vulnerable communities, linguistic diversity and decentralised development.

Constitutional accountability

The Tenth Schedule seeks to address political defections and preserve stability in representative institutions. Its operation must be balanced with the representative responsibilities of legislators and judicial safeguards.

Limits of constitutional protection

The Ninth Schedule demonstrates that constitutional protection cannot be understood as unlimited immunity. The basic structure doctrine continues to constrain constitutional amendments and the legal effects of provisions placed in the Schedule.
