Schedules of the Indian Constitution

Schedules are appendices to the Constitution that contain detailed information, lists, forms and supplementary provisions. They support the Articles by providing particulars that need greater detail than the main constitutional text.

The original Constitution had eight Schedules. Four additional Schedules were subsequently added, taking the total to twelve.

2. First Schedule: States and Union Territories

The First Schedule contains the names of the States and Union Territories of India and describes their territorial extent.

Articles 1 and 4 are particularly relevant. Article 1 describes India as a Union of States, while Article 4 permits Parliament to make supplementary, incidental and consequential provisions when laws are enacted under Articles 2 and 3.

Changes to the First Schedule may follow the formation of new States or alterations in the areas, boundaries or names of existing States and Union Territories.

3. Second Schedule: Emoluments and Privileges

The Second Schedule contains provisions relating to the emoluments, allowances, privileges and related matters of specified constitutional authorities.

It covers provisions concerning:

  • The President and Governors.
  • The Speaker and Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha.
  • The Chairman and Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha.
  • The Speaker and Deputy Speaker of State Legislative Assemblies.
  • The Chairman and Deputy Chairman of State Legislative Councils.
  • Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts.
  • The Comptroller and Auditor-General of India.

The relevant constitutional provisions and statutory laws govern the actual amounts and conditions.

4. Third Schedule: Oaths and Affirmations

The Third Schedule contains the forms of oaths and affirmations for specified constitutional offices and elected representatives.

These include:

  • Union Ministers.
  • Candidates for election to Parliament.
  • Members of Parliament.
  • Judges of the Supreme Court.
  • Comptroller and Auditor-General of India.
  • State Ministers.
  • Candidates for State Legislature elections.
  • Members of State Legislatures.
  • Judges of High Courts.

The President's and Governors' oaths are prescribed separately in Articles 60 and 159 respectively, rather than in the Third Schedule.

5. Fourth Schedule: Allocation of Seats in the Rajya Sabha

The Fourth Schedule provides for the allocation of seats to States and eligible Union Territories in the Council of States, or Rajya Sabha.

  • It is connected with Article 80.
  • Representation is broadly linked to population, subject to constitutional provisions.
  • The allocation may change when constitutional or territorial arrangements are altered.

The Fourth Schedule should be distinguished from the actual procedure for electing Rajya Sabha members, which is dealt with under Article 80 and the relevant election law.

6. Fifth Schedule: Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes

The Fifth Schedule deals with the administration and control of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes in States other than the tribal areas governed under the Sixth Schedule.

Important features

  • The President may declare an area to be a Scheduled Area and may alter its boundaries or discontinue such designation as provided by the Constitution.
  • The Governor has special responsibilities concerning Scheduled Areas.
  • A Tribes Advisory Council is provided for in States having Scheduled Areas and may also be established in certain other States with Scheduled Tribes.
  • The Governor submits reports concerning the administration of Scheduled Areas to the President.
  • The Union has an executive responsibility to give directions to the States regarding the administration of these areas.

The Fifth Schedule is linked to Article 244(1). The Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes framework should also be studied alongside the Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA).

7. Sixth Schedule: Tribal Areas in Four States

The Sixth Schedule provides a special administrative framework for specified tribal areas in:

  • Assam
  • Meghalaya
  • Tripura
  • Mizoram

It provides for Autonomous District Councils and, where applicable, Regional Councils.

Powers of Autonomous District Councils

  • Legislative powers over specified subjects, including certain customary and community matters.
  • Powers relating to land, non-reserved forests and shifting cultivation, subject to constitutional limitations.
  • Powers to establish village and other local courts for specified disputes.
  • Powers to levy certain taxes and collect specified revenues.
  • Powers to regulate specified local matters and institutions.

The Sixth Schedule is intended to protect tribal autonomy, customary practices and local self-government within the constitutional framework.

8. Seventh Schedule: Distribution of Legislative Powers

The Seventh Schedule distributes legislative subjects between the Union and the States through three lists. It is linked to Article 246 and other related constitutional provisions.

A. Union List

The Union List contains subjects on which Parliament has exclusive legislative competence, subject to the Constitution.

Examples include:

  • Defence and armed forces.
  • Foreign affairs.
  • Atomic energy.
  • Railways.
  • Currency and coinage.
  • Banking.
  • Citizenship and naturalisation.

B. State List

The State List contains subjects on which State Legislatures ordinarily have exclusive legislative competence, subject to constitutional exceptions.

Examples include:

  • Public order.
  • Police.
  • Public health and sanitation.
  • Agriculture.
  • Land.
  • Local government.

C. Concurrent List

Both Parliament and State Legislatures can ordinarily legislate on Concurrent List subjects. In case of repugnancy, Article 254 generally gives precedence to parliamentary law, subject to its constitutional exceptions.

Examples include:

  • Criminal law and criminal procedure.
  • Marriage and divorce.
  • Education.
  • Forests.
  • Trade unions and labour welfare.
  • Electricity.

The Seventh Schedule has been substantially affected by constitutional amendments. The 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 transferred certain subjects, including education, forests, protection of wild animals and birds, and weights and measures, from the State List to the Concurrent List.

The Eighth Schedule originally included 14 languages. Sindhi was added by the 21st Amendment Act, 1967. Konkani, Manipuri and Nepali were added by the 71st Amendment Act, 1992. Bodo, Dogri, Maithili and Santali were added by the 92nd Amendment Act, 2003. The 96th Amendment Act, 2011 changed the name Oriya to Odia.

Inclusion in the Eighth Schedule does not, by itself, make a language the national language or an official language of every State.

10. Ninth Schedule: Protection of Certain Laws

The Ninth Schedule was added by the First Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951. It is associated with Article 31B.

It was originally intended to protect specified laws, particularly land reform legislation, from being invalidated on certain Fundamental Rights grounds.

The protection is not absolute. In I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007), the Supreme Court held that laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 can be reviewed if their impact damages or destroys the basic structure of the Constitution.

11. Tenth Schedule: Anti-Defection Law

The Tenth Schedule was inserted by the 52nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1985. It deals with disqualification of members of Parliament and State Legislatures on grounds of defection.

Grounds of disqualification

  • A member voluntarily gives up membership of the political party on whose ticket the member was elected.
  • A member votes or abstains from voting contrary to a party direction without prior permission, and the party does not condone the action within the prescribed period.
  • An independently elected member joins a political party after the election.
  • A nominated member joins a political party after the expiry of six months from the date of taking the seat.

Exceptions

The merger exception applies when at least two-thirds of the members of a legislature party agree to the merger with another political party.

The earlier provision allowing a split by one-third of the members was removed by the 91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003.

Decision-making authority

The Speaker or Chairman of the relevant House decides questions of disqualification under the Schedule. The decision is subject to judicial review, as recognised in Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992).

12. Eleventh Schedule: Panchayats

The Eleventh Schedule was added by the 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992. It contains 29 subjects that may be devolved to Panchayats by State Legislatures under Article 243G.

  1. Agriculture, including agricultural extension.
  2. Land improvement, implementation of land reforms, land consolidation and soil conservation.
  3. Minor irrigation, water management and watershed development.
  4. Animal husbandry, dairying and poultry.
  5. Fisheries.
  6. Social forestry and farm forestry.
  7. Minor forest produce.
  8. Small-scale industries, including food-processing industries.
  9. Khadi, village and cottage industries.
  10. Rural housing.
  11. Drinking water.
  12. Fuel and fodder.
  13. Roads, culverts, bridges, ferries, waterways and other means of communication.
  14. Rural electrification, including distribution of electricity.
  15. Non-conventional energy sources.
  16. Poverty alleviation programmes.
  17. Education, including primary and secondary schools.
  18. Technical training and vocational education.
  19. Adult and non-formal education.
  20. Libraries.
  21. Cultural activities.
  22. Markets and fairs.
  23. Health and sanitation, including hospitals, primary health centres and dispensaries.
  24. Family welfare.
  25. Women and child development.
  26. Social welfare, including welfare of persons with disabilities.
  27. Welfare of weaker sections, particularly Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
  28. Public distribution system.
  29. Maintenance of community assets.

The Schedule does not automatically transfer all 29 subjects to Panchayats. The actual devolution of powers depends on State legislation.

13. Twelfth Schedule: Municipalities

The Twelfth Schedule was added by the 74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992. It contains 18 subjects that may be entrusted to Municipalities under Article 243W.

  1. Urban planning, including town planning.
  2. Regulation of land use and construction of buildings.
  3. Planning for economic and social development.
  4. Roads and bridges.
  5. Water supply for domestic, industrial and commercial purposes.
  6. Public health, sanitation, conservancy and solid waste management.
  7. Fire services.
  8. Urban forestry, protection of the environment and promotion of ecological aspects.
  9. Safeguarding the interests of weaker sections, including persons with disabilities.
  10. Slum improvement and upgradation.
  11. Urban poverty alleviation.
  12. Provision of urban amenities and facilities such as parks, gardens and playgrounds.
  13. Promotion of cultural, educational and aesthetic aspects.
  14. Burials and burial grounds, cremations, cremation grounds and electric crematoriums.
  15. Cattle pounds and prevention of cruelty to animals.
  16. Vital statistics, including registration of births and deaths.
  17. Public amenities, including street lighting, parking lots, bus stops and public conveniences.
  18. Regulation of slaughterhouses and tanneries.

As with the Eleventh Schedule, actual devolution depends on State legislation and the constitutional framework.

15. Important Supreme Court Cases

Case Year Significance
Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu 1992 Recognised judicial review of decisions under the Tenth Schedule, subject to the principles laid down by the Court.
I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu 2007 Held that post-24 April 1973 Ninth Schedule insertions are open to basic-structure review.
Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah 2021 Considered the constitutional validity of the 97th Amendment's provisions concerning co-operative societies and the requirement of State ratification.
