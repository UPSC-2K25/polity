Directive Principles of State Policy

The Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSPs) are constitutional guidelines for the State to establish social and economic democracy. They are contained in Part IV of the Constitution, under Articles 36 to 51.

1. Constitutional position

  • Part: Part IV of the Constitution.
  • Articles: Articles 36 to 51.
  • Source: Inspired mainly by the Constitution of Ireland, which in turn drew upon the Spanish constitutional tradition.
  • Objective: Establish social and economic democracy and promote a welfare state.
  • Nature: Non-justiciable; they cannot ordinarily be enforced by courts.

Article 37 declares that the provisions of Part IV are not enforceable by any court. However, they are fundamental in the governance of the country, and it is the duty of the State to apply them in making laws.

2. Meaning and scope of key terms

Article 36 – Definition of the State

For Part IV, the term "State" has the same meaning as in Part III dealing with Fundamental Rights, unless the context otherwise requires.

Article 37 – Application of the principles

DPSPs are not enforceable by courts, but they are fundamental to governance. The State must apply these principles while making laws and framing policies.

3. Classification of DPSPs

The Constitution does not formally classify DPSPs. For academic and examination purposes, they are commonly grouped into three categories: socialist, Gandhian and liberal-intellectual principles. Some provisions overlap these categories.

A. Socialist principles

These principles aim to establish social and economic justice, reduce inequalities and promote the welfare of all sections.

  • Article 38: Promote the welfare of the people by securing a social order informed by justice—social, economic and political. Minimise inequalities in income, status, facilities and opportunities.
  • Article 39: Secure adequate means of livelihood for men and women; distribute material resources to serve the common good; prevent concentration of wealth; ensure equal pay for equal work; protect workers and children from abuse and exploitation; and provide opportunities for healthy development of children.
  • Article 39A: Promote equal justice and provide free legal aid so that access to justice is not denied because of economic or other disabilities.
  • Article 41: Within its economic capacity and development, make effective provision for the right to work, education and public assistance in cases such as unemployment, old age, sickness and disablement.
  • Article 42: Make provision for just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief.
  • Article 43: Endeavour to secure a living wage, decent standard of life and social and cultural opportunities for workers.
  • Article 43A: Secure the participation of workers in the management of industries.
  • Article 47: Raise the level of nutrition, standard of living and public health.

B. Gandhian principles

These principles reflect Gandhian ideas of village self-government, decentralisation, cottage industries and the protection of weaker sections.

  • Article 40: Organise village panchayats and endow them with powers and authority necessary to function as units of self-government.
  • Article 43: Promote cottage industries on an individual or cooperative basis in rural areas.
  • Article 43B: Promote voluntary formation, autonomous functioning, democratic control and professional management of cooperative societies.
  • Article 46: Promote the educational and economic interests of Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and other weaker sections, and protect them from social injustice and exploitation.
  • Article 47: Endeavour to prohibit the consumption of intoxicating drinks and drugs injurious to health, except for medicinal purposes.
  • Article 48: Organise agriculture and animal husbandry on modern and scientific lines; preserve and improve breeds and prohibit the slaughter of cows, calves and other milch and draught cattle.

C. Liberal-intellectual principles

These provisions promote institutional reform, uniform civil laws, environmental protection, international peace and modern governance.

  • Article 44: Endeavour to secure a Uniform Civil Code for citizens throughout the territory of India.
  • Article 45: Provide early childhood care and education for all children until they complete the age of six years.
  • Article 48: Organise agriculture and animal husbandry on modern and scientific lines.
  • Article 48A: Protect and improve the environment and safeguard forests and wildlife.
  • Article 49: Protect monuments, places and objects of artistic or historic interest declared to be of national importance.
  • Article 50: Separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State.
  • Article 51: Promote international peace and security, maintain just and honourable relations between nations, foster respect for international law and treaty obligations, and encourage settlement of international disputes by arbitration.

5. Important constitutional amendments

Amendment Important change relating to DPSPs
42nd Amendment Act, 1976 Added Article 39A on equal justice and free legal aid, Article 43A on workers' participation in management, and Article 48A on environmental protection. It also amended Article 39(f).
44th Amendment Act, 1978 Added clause (2) to Article 38, directing the State to minimise inequalities in income, status, facilities and opportunities.
86th Amendment Act, 2002 Recast Article 45 to provide early childhood care and education for children below six years, following the introduction of Article 21A on the right to education for children aged six to fourteen years.
97th Amendment Act, 2011 Added Article 43B to promote cooperative societies.

6. DPSPs and Fundamental Rights

Fundamental Rights and DPSPs are complementary parts of the constitutional framework. Fundamental Rights primarily protect individual liberty and are enforceable by courts, while DPSPs guide the State in achieving social and economic welfare.

Basis Fundamental Rights DPSPs
Part Part III Part IV
Articles 12–35 36–51
Nature Generally impose enforceable limitations or obligations on the State and protect specified rights. Provide directions and goals for State policy.
Enforceability Enforceable through constitutional remedies. Not directly enforceable by courts.
Objective Political democracy and protection of liberty. Social and economic democracy.
Remedy Articles 32 and 226 provide constitutional remedies. No direct judicial remedy for non-implementation.

Article 31C, introduced by the 25th Amendment Act, 1971, protects laws giving effect to the principles in Article 39(b) and Article 39(c) from challenge on the grounds of Articles 14 and 19. The 42nd Amendment attempted to extend this protection to all DPSPs, but that extension was invalidated by the Supreme Court in Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980).

7. Important Supreme Court judgments

State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951)

The Supreme Court held that in case of conflict, Fundamental Rights prevailed over DPSPs. The judgment led to the First Constitutional Amendment, which introduced Article 15(4).

Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967)

The Court held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights. The decision formed part of the constitutional debate over the relationship between Fundamental Rights and social reform measures.

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)

The Supreme Court recognised Parliament's power to amend the Constitution but held that this power cannot destroy its basic structure. The judgment also examined the relationship between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs.

Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)

The Court struck down the extension of Article 31C protection to all DPSPs. It held that harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs is part of the Constitution's basic structure.

Unni Krishnan v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1993)

The Court recognised the right to education for children up to fourteen years as flowing from Article 21, read in the light of Article 45. This contributed to the later introduction of Article 21A through the 86th Amendment.

8. Significance of DPSPs

  • Provide a framework for establishing a welfare state.
  • Guide governments in making laws and public policies.
  • Promote social and economic justice and reduce inequalities.
  • Support protection of workers, children and weaker sections.
  • Encourage decentralisation and democratic self-government.
  • Promote public health, education and environmental protection.
  • Help assess government performance against constitutional goals.

9. Limitations and challenges

  • Non-justiciability: Citizens cannot directly compel implementation of a DPSP merely by filing a case for its enforcement.
  • Resource constraints: Several principles require substantial public expenditure and administrative capacity.
  • Policy priorities: Governments may differ in how they interpret and prioritise the principles.
  • Uneven implementation: Progress in areas such as public health, nutrition, education and local self-government varies across regions.
  • Rights balance: Laws implementing DPSPs must respect constitutional limitations and the basic structure of the Constitution.

Quick revision

  • DPSPs are contained in Part IV, Articles 36–51.
  • Article 37 makes them non-justiciable but fundamental to governance.
  • The Constitution does not formally classify DPSPs; the usual groups are socialist, Gandhian and liberal-intellectual.
  • Article 39(b) concerns distribution of material resources for the common good; Article 39(c) concerns preventing concentration of wealth.
  • Article 39A: free legal aid; Article 40: village panchayats; Article 44: Uniform Civil Code.
  • Article 45: early childhood care and education below six years.
  • Article 48A: environment; Article 50: separation of judiciary from executive.
  • Article 31C currently protects laws implementing Article 39(b) and (c), subject to the constitutional position established by the Supreme Court.
  • Minerva Mills emphasised the harmony between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs.
