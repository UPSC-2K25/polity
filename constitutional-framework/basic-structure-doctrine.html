Basic Structure Doctrine

The Basic Structure Doctrine is a principle evolved by the Supreme Court of India. It holds that Parliament may amend any provision of the Constitution under Article 368, but cannot alter or destroy its basic structure.

The doctrine protects the essential constitutional identity of India while permitting democratic and institutional change. It is a judicially developed limitation on the exercise of Parliament's constituent power.

Its central constitutional question is not whether a particular provision can be amended, but whether the amendment, in its substance and effect, damages or destroys an essential feature of the constitutional framework.

1. Meaning and Constitutional Basis

The Constitution does not expressly use the phrase "basic structure". The doctrine emerged through judicial interpretation of Parliament's amending power under Article 368.

  • Parliament possesses wide constituent power to amend the Constitution.
  • The power to amend is not unlimited.
  • An amendment may alter constitutional provisions but cannot destroy or damage the Constitution's basic structure.
  • The Supreme Court can review constitutional amendments against this limitation.

Why is the doctrine necessary?

A Constitution must be capable of adapting to changing circumstances. However, if an ordinary political majority could fundamentally alter the constitutional order without limitation, it could potentially dismantle the democratic and constitutional framework through formally valid amendments.

The doctrine seeks to reconcile two principles:

  • Constitutional flexibility: Parliament must be able to reform institutions and respond to changing needs.
  • Constitutional continuity: The essential identity and foundational principles of the Constitution must remain protected.

2. Evolution of the Doctrine

The doctrine developed through a series of Supreme Court decisions concerning Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights and the constitutional limits on that power.

Shankari Prasad v. Union of India (1951)

The Supreme Court upheld the First Constitutional Amendment. It held that a constitutional amendment under Article 368 was not "law" within the meaning of Article 13. Parliament could therefore amend Fundamental Rights.

Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan (1965)

The Court reaffirmed the position in Shankari Prasad and upheld Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights. However, separate opinions raised concerns about the possibility of unlimited amending power.

I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967)

The Supreme Court held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights. It treated a constitutional amendment as "law" for the purpose of Article 13 and applied the doctrine of prospective overruling.

24th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971

Parliament responded to Golaknath by amending Articles 13 and 368. The amendment expressly affirmed Parliament's power to amend any provision of the Constitution and made Presidential assent to a constitutional amendment bill mandatory.

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)

A thirteen-judge Bench of the Supreme Court examined the extent of Parliament's amending power. By a narrow majority, the Court held that Parliament could amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights, but could not alter or destroy its basic structure.

This judgment established the Basic Structure Doctrine. The Court did not provide an exhaustive list of basic features. Their identification and application were left to subsequent constitutional adjudication.

3. Features Recognised as Part of the Basic Structure

The Supreme Court has identified various constitutional principles as part of the basic structure in different judgments. There is no exhaustive or permanently closed list. The status of a feature and its application depend on constitutional interpretation and the context of the amendment under review.

Feature Constitutional significance
Supremacy of the Constitution All organs of the State derive authority from the Constitution and remain subject to its limitations.
Sovereign, democratic and republican character Government derives authority from the people through a representative democratic system and an elected head of State.
Secular character The State must act consistently with constitutional equality and freedom in matters of religion.
Federal character The constitutional distribution of powers and the federal relationship between the Union and States are protected.
Rule of law Governmental power is subject to law and arbitrary exercise of authority is constitutionally restricted.
Separation of powers Constitutional functions are distributed among the legislature, executive and judiciary, with checks and balances.
Judicial review Constitutional courts can review legislative and executive action, including constitutional amendments, on recognised grounds.
Independence of the judiciary Courts must be able to perform their constitutional role without improper interference.
Free and fair elections Democratic government requires genuine electoral choice and periodic elections.
Parliamentary system The constitutional relationship between the executive and legislature is an important feature of India's democratic structure.
Unity and integrity of India The constitutional order protects the unity and territorial integrity of the country.
Balance between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs Individual liberty and social welfare objectives must remain in constitutional harmony.
Limited amending power Parliament cannot use Article 368 to destroy the essential identity of the Constitution.
Constitutional balance and institutional independence The constitutional framework requires effective limits on power and institutions capable of performing their assigned roles.

These features should not be treated as a mechanical checklist. The Court examines the nature and effect of a constitutional amendment in its particular context.

4. Landmark Judgments After Kesavananda Bharati

Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975)

The Supreme Court invalidated a provision introduced by the 39th Amendment that sought to place the election of the Prime Minister and certain other high constitutional office-holders beyond ordinary judicial scrutiny. The judgment recognised democracy, free and fair elections and the rule of law as important constitutional principles forming part of the basic structure.

Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)

The Court struck down provisions of the 42nd Amendment that attempted to give Parliament unlimited amending power and extended Article 31C protection to all Directive Principles. It held that limited amending power and harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles are part of the basic structure.

Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981)

The Court held that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973, the date of the Kesavananda Bharati judgment, could be examined under the basic structure doctrine.

S.P. Sampath Kumar v. Union of India (1987)

The Supreme Court considered the constitutional position of administrative tribunals and the need to preserve effective judicial review within the constitutional framework.

L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997)

The Supreme Court held that the power of judicial review exercised by High Courts under Article 226 and by the Supreme Court under Article 32 is part of the basic structure. Tribunal decisions remain subject to scrutiny by constitutional courts.

I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007)

The Supreme Court held that laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 are subject to judicial review if their impact damages the basic structure through violation of rights that form part of that structure. Inclusion in the Ninth Schedule does not provide absolute immunity.

Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015)

The Supreme Court struck down the 99th Constitutional Amendment and the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act. The majority held that judicial independence is part of the basic structure and that the proposed appointments framework damaged that feature.

Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022)

A Constitution Bench upheld the 103rd Constitutional Amendment introducing reservation for Economically Weaker Sections. The majority rejected the challenges to the amendment, including the argument that the exclusion of Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes from the EWS category violated the basic structure.

The judgment illustrates that a constitutional amendment is not invalid merely because it changes an important policy arrangement. The constitutional question is whether the amendment violates a basic feature.

5. Ninth Schedule and Basic Structure Review

The Ninth Schedule was introduced by the First Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951. It was intended to protect specified laws, particularly land reform legislation, from challenges based on certain Fundamental Rights.

Article 31B provides protection to laws included in the Ninth Schedule. However, that protection is not absolute in light of the basic structure doctrine.

Evolution of judicial review

  • Before 24 April 1973: Ninth Schedule laws were generally assessed under the constitutional approach applicable before the Kesavananda Bharati ruling.
  • After 24 April 1973: The constitutional validity of later insertions could be examined against the basic structure limitation.
  • Waman Rao (1981): Applied the basic structure approach to post-Kesavananda Bharati insertions.
  • I.R. Coelho (2007): Clarified that post-1973 insertions are not automatically immune from judicial review and that their impact on protected constitutional rights must be examined.

Why the Ninth Schedule is not absolute immunity

Article 31B cannot be used as a constitutional device to destroy the basic structure. The Court examines the effect of the law and whether the rights affected form part of the basic structure.

The distinction is important: the mere placement of a law in the Ninth Schedule does not automatically validate it against every constitutional challenge.

6. Basic Structure Doctrine and Article 368

Aspect Constitutional position
Parliament's power Parliament may amend any provision of the Constitution under Article 368.
Nature of power The power is wide but subject to constitutional limitations.
Fundamental Rights They may be amended, provided the amendment does not damage or destroy the basic structure.
Judicial review Constitutional amendments may be reviewed on basic structure grounds.
Exhaustive list No exhaustive or permanently closed list of basic features has been laid down.
State ratification Where required by Article 368, ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures remains necessary.
Standard of review The Court examines the substance and effect of the amendment in relation to the Constitution's essential features.

7. Significance of the Doctrine

  • Protects constitutional identity: Prevents amendments that would destroy the essential character of the Constitution.
  • Limits arbitrary power: Ensures that Parliament's amending power is not treated as unlimited.
  • Preserves democracy: Protects democratic government, free and fair elections and constitutional accountability.
  • Strengthens judicial review: Ensures that constitutional amendments remain subject to constitutional scrutiny.
  • Maintains federal balance: Protects essential features of the Union-State constitutional arrangement.
  • Protects institutional independence: Preserves the ability of constitutional institutions to perform their functions.
  • Balances continuity and change: Allows reform while maintaining the Constitution's foundational principles.

8. Criticism and Constitutional Debate

Absence of express textual formulation

The phrase "basic structure" does not appear in Article 368. Critics argue that the doctrine introduces a limitation through judicial interpretation rather than an express constitutional provision.

Uncertainty

The absence of an exhaustive list makes the doctrine flexible but can also make its application difficult to predict in advance.

Judicial discretion

Identifying basic features gives courts substantial interpretative responsibility. This raises questions about the proper limits of judicial power.

Parliamentary sovereignty debate

The doctrine raises questions about the relationship between elected representatives and judicial review. In India's constitutional system, however, Parliament exercises constituent power within a written Constitution and is not sovereign in the same manner as the Westminster Parliament.

Constitutional protection argument

Supporters argue that the doctrine prevents a temporary political majority from dismantling constitutional democracy, federalism, judicial independence or the rule of law through formal amendments.

9. Basic Structure and Constitutional Governance

The doctrine has implications beyond the technical amendment procedure. It shapes the relationship among Parliament, constitutional courts and other institutions.

  • For Parliament: Constitutional reform must remain within the limits of the constituent power.
  • For the judiciary: Courts have a responsibility to review amendments against constitutional fundamentals, while giving due regard to democratic and institutional context.
  • For federalism: The doctrine protects the essential distribution of constitutional authority between the Union and States.
  • For citizens: It helps preserve democratic institutions, constitutional remedies and the foundational guarantees of the constitutional order.
  • For constitutional change: It distinguishes permissible constitutional reform from transformation that destroys the Constitution's essential identity.

Quick Revision

  • The Basic Structure Doctrine was established in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973).
  • Article 368 provides Parliament's constitutional amendment power.
  • Parliament may amend Fundamental Rights but cannot destroy the basic structure.
  • The Constitution does not contain an exhaustive list of basic features.
  • Recognised features include constitutional supremacy, democracy, secularism, federalism, rule of law, judicial review and judicial independence.
  • Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) applied the doctrine to free and fair elections and democratic principles.
  • Minerva Mills (1980) protected limited amending power and harmony between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs.
  • L. Chandra Kumar (1997) recognised judicial review under Articles 32 and 226 as part of the basic structure.
  • I.R. Coelho (2007) clarified that post-1973 Ninth Schedule insertions are subject to basic structure review.
  • Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association (2015) reaffirmed judicial independence as a basic feature.
  • Janhit Abhiyan (2022) upheld the 103rd Amendment introducing EWS reservation.
  • The doctrine balances constitutional continuity with the need for constitutional change.
