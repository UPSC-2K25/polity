Basic Structure Doctrine

The Basic Structure Doctrine is a principle developed by the Supreme Court of India. It holds that Parliament may amend any provision of the Constitution under Article 368, but cannot alter or destroy its basic structure. The doctrine protects the fundamental identity of the Constitution while allowing constitutional change.

1. Meaning and constitutional basis

The Constitution does not expressly use the phrase "basic structure". The doctrine emerged through judicial interpretation of Parliament's amending power under Article 368.

  • Parliament has wide powers to amend the Constitution.
  • The power to amend is not unlimited.
  • An amendment cannot damage or destroy the Constitution's basic structure.
  • The Supreme Court has the authority to review constitutional amendments against this limitation.

The doctrine seeks to maintain a balance between constitutional flexibility and continuity. It prevents temporary parliamentary majorities from fundamentally changing the character of the constitutional order.

2. Evolution of the doctrine

The doctrine developed through a series of Supreme Court decisions concerning the relationship between Fundamental Rights and Parliament's power to amend the Constitution.

Shankari Prasad v. Union of India (1951)

The Supreme Court upheld the First Constitutional Amendment. It held that a constitutional amendment made under Article 368 was not "law" within the meaning of Article 13. Parliament could therefore amend Fundamental Rights.

Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan (1965)

The Court reaffirmed the position in Shankari Prasad and upheld Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights. However, some judges expressed concerns about the possibility of unlimited amendment power.

Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967)

The Supreme Court held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights. It treated a constitutional amendment as "law" for the purpose of Article 13 and applied the doctrine of prospective overruling.

24th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971

Parliament responded to Golaknath by amending Article 13 and Article 368. The amendment expressly affirmed Parliament's power to amend any provision of the Constitution and made Presidential assent to a constitutional amendment bill mandatory.

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)

A thirteen-judge Bench of the Supreme Court examined the extent of Parliament's amending power. By a narrow majority, the Court held that Parliament could amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights, but could not alter or destroy its basic structure.

This judgment established the Basic Structure Doctrine. The Court did not provide an exhaustive list of basic features, leaving their identification to future cases.

3. Important features of the basic structure

The Supreme Court has identified various constitutional principles as part of the basic structure in different judgments. There is no exhaustive or permanently closed list.

Feature Constitutional significance
Supremacy of the Constitution The Constitution is the supreme law and all organs of the State derive their authority from it.
Sovereign, democratic and republican character India's constitutional identity rests on popular sovereignty, representative government and an elected head of State.
Secular character The State must maintain constitutional neutrality and equality in matters of religion.
Federal character Constitutional distribution of powers between the Union and the States is protected.
Rule of law Governmental power is subject to law and arbitrary exercise of authority is constitutionally restricted.
Separation of powers Constitutional functions are distributed among the legislature, executive and judiciary.
Judicial review Courts can review legislative and executive action for constitutional validity.
Independence of the judiciary Courts must be able to perform their constitutional role without improper interference.
Free and fair elections Democratic government depends on genuine electoral choice and periodic elections.
Parliamentary system The constitutional relationship between the executive and legislature is protected.
Unity and integrity of India The territorial and national integrity of the country is a fundamental constitutional value.
Balance between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs Individual liberty and social welfare objectives must remain in constitutional harmony.
Limited amending power Parliament cannot use Article 368 to destroy the basic structure.
Constitutional supremacy and judicial independence The constitutional framework requires independent institutions and effective constitutional limits.

4. Landmark judgments after Kesavananda Bharati

Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975)

The Supreme Court invalidated a provision introduced by the 39th Amendment that sought to place the election of the Prime Minister and certain other high constitutional office-holders beyond ordinary judicial scrutiny. The judgment recognised free and fair elections, democracy and the rule of law as important elements of the basic structure.

Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)

The Court struck down provisions of the 42nd Amendment that attempted to give Parliament unlimited amending power and extended Article 31C protection to all DPSPs. It held that limited amending power and the harmony between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs form part of the basic structure.

Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981)

The Court held that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973, the date of the Kesavananda Bharati judgment, could be examined under the basic structure doctrine.

S.P. Sampath Kumar v. Union of India (1987)

The Supreme Court considered the constitutional position of administrative tribunals and emphasised the importance of effective judicial review within the constitutional framework.

L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997)

The Supreme Court held that the power of judicial review exercised by the High Courts under Article 226 and by the Supreme Court under Article 32 is part of the basic structure. Tribunal decisions remain subject to scrutiny by constitutional courts.

I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007)

The Supreme Court held that laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 are subject to judicial review if their impact violates rights that form part of the basic structure. Inclusion in the Ninth Schedule does not provide absolute immunity from judicial review.

Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015)

The Supreme Court struck down the 99th Constitutional Amendment and the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act. The majority held that judicial independence is part of the basic structure and that the proposed appointments framework damaged that feature.

5. The Ninth Schedule and basic structure review

The Ninth Schedule was introduced by the First Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951. Its original purpose was to protect certain land reform laws from challenge on the grounds of violation of specified Fundamental Rights.

Article 31B provides protection to laws included in the Ninth Schedule, subject to the constitutional position developed by the Supreme Court.

  • Before 24 April 1973, Ninth Schedule laws were generally treated under the earlier approach to Article 31B.
  • After the Kesavananda Bharati judgment, the constitutional validity of later insertions could be examined under the basic structure doctrine.
  • In I.R. Coelho (2007), the Supreme Court clarified that post-24 April 1973 insertions are not automatically immune from judicial review.

The Ninth Schedule therefore cannot be used as a device to destroy the basic structure of the Constitution.

6. Basic Structure Doctrine and Article 368

Aspect Position
Parliament's power Parliament may amend any provision of the Constitution under Article 368.
Nature of power The power is wide but limited by the basic structure doctrine.
Fundamental Rights They may be amended, provided the amendment does not damage the basic structure.
Judicial review Constitutional amendments can be reviewed by courts on basic structure grounds.
Exhaustive list No exhaustive list of basic features has been laid down.
State ratification Where required by Article 368, ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures remains necessary.

7. Significance of the doctrine

  • Protects constitutional identity: Prevents fundamental changes that would destroy the essential character of the Constitution.
  • Limits arbitrary power: Prevents Parliament from using its amendment power without constitutional limits.
  • Preserves democracy: Protects democratic government, free and fair elections and constitutional accountability.
  • Strengthens judicial review: Ensures that constitutional amendments remain subject to constitutional scrutiny.
  • Maintains federal balance: Protects the essential federal features of the constitutional arrangement.
  • Balances continuity and change: Allows reform while preserving the Constitution's essential principles.

8. Criticism and debate

  • Not expressly stated: The doctrine is judicially evolved rather than expressly written into the Constitution.
  • Uncertainty: The absence of an exhaustive list can make the application of the doctrine difficult to predict.
  • Judicial discretion: Critics argue that identifying basic features gives courts substantial interpretative power.
  • Parliamentary sovereignty debate: The doctrine raises questions about the relationship between elected representatives and judicial review.
  • Constitutional protection: Supporters argue that the doctrine prevents a temporary political majority from dismantling constitutional democracy.

Quick revision

  • The Basic Structure Doctrine was established in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973).
  • Article 368 provides Parliament's constitutional amendment power.
  • Parliament may amend Fundamental Rights but cannot destroy the basic structure.
  • The Constitution does not contain an exhaustive list of basic features.
  • Important features include constitutional supremacy, democracy, secularism, federalism, rule of law, judicial review and independence of the judiciary.
  • Minerva Mills (1980) protected limited amending power and harmony between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs.
  • L. Chandra Kumar (1997) recognised judicial review under Articles 32 and 226 as part of the basic structure.
  • I.R. Coelho (2007) clarified that post-1973 Ninth Schedule insertions are subject to basic structure review.
  • The doctrine balances constitutional continuity with the need for constitutional change.
