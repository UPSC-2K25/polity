Constitutional Amendment

A constitutional amendment is a formal alteration of the Constitution through the procedure prescribed by the Constitution itself. It may add, vary or repeal constitutional provisions.

India's amendment system seeks to reconcile constitutional continuity with the need for institutional, political, social and economic adaptation. Parliament exercises constituent power under Article 368, but that power is subject to procedural requirements and the basic structure limitation developed by the Supreme Court.

The Indian Constitution is therefore neither wholly rigid nor wholly flexible. Different provisions require different procedures for alteration.

1. Meaning, Purpose and Constitutional Philosophy

Constitutional amendment is different from ordinary legislation. Ordinary laws are enacted within the framework of the Constitution; constitutional amendments formally alter that framework or its provisions.

Why is an amendment procedure necessary?

  • Changing circumstances: Enables the Constitution to respond to new social, political, economic and technological conditions.
  • Institutional reform: Permits changes to the composition, powers and accountability of constitutional institutions.
  • Democratic adaptation: Allows elected representatives to respond to evolving public needs through a constitutionally prescribed process.
  • Federal adjustment: Provides a mechanism for changing aspects of Union-State relations and the distribution of powers.
  • Rights and social justice: Enables the constitutional framework to respond to changing understandings of equality, liberty and social welfare.
  • Correction and clarification: Allows constitutional gaps, ambiguities and practical difficulties to be addressed.

Rigidity and flexibility

A completely rigid Constitution may become difficult to adapt to changing conditions. A completely flexible Constitution may allow temporary political majorities to alter foundational arrangements too easily. India's differentiated amendment procedures attempt to balance these competing concerns.

2. Article 368: Parliament's Constituent Power

Article 368 appears in Part XX of the Constitution. It deals with Parliament's power to amend the Constitution and the procedure for exercising that power.

Scope of the power

  • Parliament may amend the Constitution by way of addition, variation or repeal of any provision.
  • The power is exercised through a constitutional amendment bill.
  • Either House of Parliament may initiate the bill.
  • The bill may be introduced by a Minister or a private member.
  • Prior permission of the President is not required for introduction of a bill under Article 368.
  • Each House must pass the bill separately by the prescribed majority.
  • There is no joint sitting to resolve a disagreement between the two Houses on a constitutional amendment bill.
  • Specified federal amendments also require ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures.
  • After the constitutional requirements are fulfilled, the bill is presented to the President for assent.

Article 368 and the limits of constituent power

Article 368 does not confer an unlimited power to replace the constitutional identity of India. The Supreme Court has held that Parliament may amend any constitutional provision, including Fundamental Rights, but cannot destroy or damage the Constitution's basic structure.

The basic structure doctrine is examined separately in the chapter on Basic Structure Doctrine.

3. Three Broad Categories of Amendment

Constitutional changes are commonly classified into three categories according to the procedure and majority required. This is a conventional classification used for studying the Indian amendment system.

A. Simple majority of Parliament

Certain constitutional changes are made through ordinary legislative procedure and are not deemed amendments under Article 368. They generally require a simple majority of members present and voting in each House.

Illustrative examples include:

  • Formation of new States and alteration of areas, boundaries or names of States under Articles 2, 3 and 4.
  • Creation or abolition of a State Legislative Council under Article 169, following the required resolution of the State Legislative Assembly.
  • Changes to citizenship legislation under Article 11.
  • Changes to certain matters relating to parliamentary privileges, salaries, allowances and internal procedure where the Constitution permits ordinary legislation.

Article 4 expressly provides that laws made under Articles 2 and 3 may contain supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions, including amendments to the First and Fourth Schedules, and that such laws are not deemed amendments of the Constitution for Article 368.

B. Special majority of Parliament

Most constitutional provisions are amended through the special majority procedure under Article 368.

Each House must satisfy both conditions:

  1. A majority of the total membership of that House; and
  2. A majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting.

These are cumulative requirements. The required majority must be obtained separately in the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha.

C. Special majority plus State ratification

Amendments affecting specified federal provisions require:

  1. Passage by the special majority in each House of Parliament; and
  2. Ratification by the Legislatures of not less than one-half of the States.

The Constitution does not prescribe a special majority within each State Legislature for this ratification. Ratification is ordinarily by a simple majority of members present and voting in the State Legislature.

4. Matters Requiring State Ratification

The proviso to Article 368(2) requires ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures where an amendment seeks to make changes in the following matters.

Provision or subject Constitutional area
Articles 54 and 55 Election of the President and the manner of election.
Article 73 and Article 162 Extent of executive power of the Union and the States.
Article 241 High Courts for Union Territories.
Article 279A Goods and Services Tax Council.
Chapter IV of Part V Union Judiciary.
Chapter V of Part VI High Courts in the States.
Chapter I of Part XI Legislative relations between the Union and the States.
Any of the Lists in the Seventh Schedule Distribution of legislative subjects between the Union and the States.
Representation of States in Parliament Constitutional provisions governing State representation in Parliament.
Article 368 The constitutional amendment procedure itself.

Important points about ratification

  • Ratification by at least half of the States is sufficient; unanimous ratification is not required.
  • The relevant State Legislatures must ratify the amendment after its passage by Parliament.
  • The Constitution does not prescribe a fixed time limit for State ratification.
  • Parliament cannot substitute State ratification with a joint sitting of Parliament.
  • Union Territories are not counted as States for this requirement merely because they have legislatures.

5. Constitutional Amendment Procedure

  1. Introduction: A constitutional amendment bill may be introduced in either House of Parliament.
  2. Passage in the first House: The House must pass the bill by the required majority.
  3. Passage in the second House: The other House must independently pass the same bill by the required majority.
  4. State ratification, where required: If the amendment concerns a matter listed in the proviso to Article 368(2), at least half of the State Legislatures must ratify it.
  5. Presidential assent: The bill is presented to the President, who shall give assent.
  6. Amendment takes effect: The Constitution stands amended in accordance with the terms of the Constitution Amendment Act and any commencement provision contained in it.

What happens if the Houses disagree?

There is no provision for a joint sitting under Article 108 to resolve a disagreement on a constitutional amendment bill. The bill must be passed independently by both Houses. If either House does not pass it in the required form, the amendment cannot proceed.

Role of the President

The 24th Amendment Act, 1971 expressly clarified the requirement of Presidential assent. Article 368 provides that the President shall give assent to a duly passed constitutional amendment bill. The President does not have the ordinary legislative option of returning such a bill for reconsideration.

6. Important Features and Institutional Safeguards

  • No separate constituent assembly: Parliament exercises the constituent power under Article 368.
  • Either House may initiate: A constitutional amendment bill may originate in either the Lok Sabha or the Rajya Sabha.
  • No joint sitting: Both Houses must pass the bill separately.
  • State participation is selective: State ratification is required only for the specified federal matters.
  • No general referendum: The Constitution does not establish a general public referendum procedure for constitutional amendments.
  • Presidential assent is mandatory: The President shall assent after the prescribed requirements are met.
  • Judicial review: Constitutional amendments remain subject to judicial review, including review for violation of the basic structure.
  • Different levels of rigidity: The procedure varies according to the subject being amended.

8. Parliament's Amending Power and the Basic Structure Limitation

The Supreme Court has recognised Parliament's broad constituent power while maintaining that the Constitution cannot be transformed in a manner that destroys its basic structure.

  • Parliament may amend Fundamental Rights.
  • Parliament may amend provisions concerning the Union, States and constitutional institutions.
  • The amendment power is subject to constitutional limitations.
  • An amendment that damages or destroys the basic structure is invalid to that extent.
  • Judicial review is available to examine whether a constitutional amendment violates this limitation.

This doctrine is not an express list of unamendable provisions written into Article 368. It is a limitation developed by the Supreme Court through constitutional interpretation.

See Basic Structure Doctrine for the doctrine's elements, evolution and application.

9. Landmark Supreme Court Judgments

Shankari Prasad v. Union of India (1951)

The Supreme Court upheld the First Amendment and held that a constitutional amendment made under Article 368 was not "law" within the meaning of Article 13. Parliament could therefore amend Fundamental Rights.

Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan (1965)

The Court reaffirmed the position that Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights under Article 368. Separate opinions raised concerns about the extent of Parliament's amending power.

I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967)

The Supreme Court held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights. The judgment applied the doctrine of prospective overruling. This position was subsequently altered by the 24th Amendment and the decision in Kesavananda Bharati.

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)

The Supreme Court held that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights, but cannot alter or destroy its basic structure. This decision established the basic structure doctrine.

Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975)

The Supreme Court invalidated a constitutional amendment provision that sought to place the election of the Prime Minister beyond judicial scrutiny. The judgment applied basic structure limitations, including principles associated with democracy and free and fair elections.

Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)

The Court invalidated the expansion of Article 31C protection to all Directive Principles and held that limited amending power and the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles are part of the basic structure.

Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981)

The Court applied the basic structure doctrine to laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973, the date of the Kesavananda Bharati judgment.

I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007)

The Supreme Court held that laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 are open to judicial review where their impact damages the basic structure through violation of rights that form part of that structure.

Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah (2021)

The Supreme Court examined the 97th Amendment's provisions concerning cooperative societies. It held that the amendment required ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures insofar as it dealt with State cooperative societies. In the absence of such ratification, Part IXB could not operate in relation to those societies. The relevant provisions concerning multi-State cooperative societies were not invalidated on that ground.

11. Issues and Reform Considerations

Balance between flexibility and stability

The amendment process must allow constitutional adaptation without making foundational arrangements vulnerable to short-term political pressures. The special majority and State ratification requirements provide different levels of institutional restraint.

Federal participation

State ratification protects the federal character of specified constitutional arrangements. However, the limited scope of the ratification requirement also raises questions about the extent of State participation in amendments that indirectly affect federal governance.

Quality of constitutional deliberation

Constitutional amendments have long-term consequences. Parliamentary scrutiny, committee examination where appropriate, public consultation and reasoned debate can improve the quality and legitimacy of constitutional change.

Transparency and accountability

Clear explanations of the constitutional problem being addressed, the expected consequences and the reasons for choosing a particular amendment procedure can strengthen public understanding and institutional accountability.
