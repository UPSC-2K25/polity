Amendment of the Constitution

The Constitution of India provides a mechanism for adapting its provisions to changing social, political and economic conditions. Article 368 lays down the principal procedure for constitutional amendments and defines Parliament's constituent power, subject to limitations imposed by the Supreme Court.

1. Meaning and need for constitutional amendment

A constitutional amendment is a formal change to the text or provisions of the Constitution. It may involve adding, altering or repealing constitutional provisions.

Amendments are necessary to:

  • Respond to changing social and economic conditions.
  • Correct constitutional gaps and address practical difficulties.
  • Strengthen democratic institutions and governance.
  • Implement reforms and expand constitutional protections.
  • Adapt the federal structure and institutional arrangements to changing needs.

The Indian Constitution seeks to balance stability with flexibility: it is neither completely rigid nor completely flexible.

2. Article 368 – Power and procedure

Article 368 is contained in Part XX of the Constitution. It deals with Parliament's power to amend the Constitution and the procedure for doing so.

  • Parliament may amend the Constitution by way of addition, variation or repeal of any provision, subject to the limitations recognised by the Supreme Court.
  • A constitutional amendment bill may be introduced in either House of Parliament.
  • It may be introduced by a Minister or a private member.
  • Prior permission of the President is not required for introducing a bill under Article 368.
  • Each House must pass the bill separately by the prescribed majority.
  • There is no provision for a joint sitting to resolve a disagreement between the two Houses on a constitutional amendment bill.
  • Where the amendment affects specified federal provisions, ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures is required.
  • After the required parliamentary passage and, where applicable, State ratification, the bill is presented to the President, who shall give assent.

3. Types of constitutional amendments

Constitutional changes in India are commonly classified into three categories according to the majority and procedure required.

A. Amendment by simple majority of Parliament

Certain constitutional changes can be made by a simple majority of members present and voting in each House. These are generally treated as outside the procedure under Article 368.

Examples include:

  • Formation of new States and alteration of areas, boundaries or names of existing States under Articles 2, 3 and 4.
  • Creation or abolition of State Legislative Councils under Article 169, following the required resolution by the State Legislative Assembly.
  • Changes relating to citizenship under Article 11.
  • Changes to certain matters relating to parliamentary privileges, salaries and allowances, and internal parliamentary procedure, where the Constitution permits ordinary legislation.

Such laws may amend provisions of the Constitution but are not deemed amendments under Article 368.

B. Amendment by special majority of Parliament

Most provisions of the Constitution can be amended by a special majority in each House of Parliament.

The required majority consists of both:

  • A majority of the total membership of that House; and
  • A majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting.

Both conditions must be satisfied separately in the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha.

C. Special majority plus ratification by States

Amendments affecting specified federal features require:

  1. Passage by a special majority in each House of Parliament; and
  2. Ratification by the Legislatures of not less than one-half of the States.

The Constitution does not prescribe a special majority within each State Legislature for this ratification. The State ratification is ordinarily by a simple majority of members present and voting. The amendment is then presented to the President for assent.

4. Matters requiring State ratification

The proviso to Article 368(2) requires ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures when an amendment seeks to make changes relating to specified federal provisions.

5. Constitutional amendment procedure

  1. Introduction: A constitutional amendment bill is introduced in either House of Parliament.
  2. Passage in the first House: The bill must obtain the prescribed majority in the House where it is introduced.
  3. Passage in the second House: The other House must independently pass the bill by the same prescribed majority.
  4. State ratification: If the amendment concerns a matter listed in the proviso to Article 368(2), at least half of the State Legislatures must ratify it.
  5. Presidential assent: The bill is presented to the President, who is constitutionally required to give assent.
  6. Constitutional amendment: The Constitution stands amended in accordance with the terms of the Act.

The President cannot return a constitutional amendment bill for reconsideration. The 24th Amendment Act, 1971 expressly clarified the requirement of Presidential assent.

6. Important features of the amendment process

  • No separate constituent assembly: Parliament exercises the power to amend the Constitution under Article 368.
  • Either House can introduce the bill: There is no requirement that a constitutional amendment bill must originate in the Lok Sabha.
  • No joint sitting: Both Houses must pass the bill independently.
  • State ratification is limited: It is necessary only for the specified federal provisions listed in Article 368(2).
  • No time limit for State ratification: The Constitution does not prescribe a specific period within which States must ratify an amendment.
  • No direct public referendum: The Constitution does not provide a general referendum procedure for constitutional amendments.
  • Presidential assent is mandatory: Once the constitutional requirements are fulfilled, the President shall give assent.
  • Judicial review applies: Constitutional amendments are subject to review by the courts on constitutionally recognised grounds, including violation of the basic structure.

8. Parliament's amending power and its limitations

Parliament's power to amend the Constitution is extensive, but it is not unlimited. The Supreme Court has developed important principles governing the scope of this power.

  • Parliament can amend Fundamental Rights.
  • Parliament can amend provisions relating to the Union, States and constitutional institutions.
  • Parliament cannot use the amendment power to destroy or damage the basic structure of the Constitution.
  • Constitutional amendments are subject to judicial review.

The basic structure doctrine was laid down in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973). It is discussed separately in the next chapter.

9. Important Supreme Court judgments

Shankari Prasad v. Union of India (1951)

The Supreme Court upheld the First Amendment and held that constitutional amendments made under Article 368 were not "law" within the meaning of Article 13. Parliament could therefore amend Fundamental Rights.

Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan (1965)

The Court reaffirmed the position that Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights under Article 368. However, concerns about the extent of the amending power were expressed in separate opinions.

Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967)

The Supreme Court held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights. The judgment applied the doctrine of prospective overruling.

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)

The Supreme Court held that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights, but cannot alter or destroy its basic structure. This decision established the basic structure doctrine.

Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975)

The Supreme Court invalidated a constitutional amendment provision that sought to place the election of certain high constitutional office-holders beyond judicial scrutiny. The judgment applied basic structure limitations, including principles of democracy and free and fair elections.

Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)

The Court invalidated the expansion of Article 31C protection to all Directive Principles and held that limited amending power and the balance between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs are part of the basic structure.

Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981)

The Court applied the basic structure doctrine to laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973, the date of the Kesavananda Bharati judgment.

I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007)

The Supreme Court held that laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 are open to judicial review if they damage the basic structure by violating rights that form part of that structure.

Quick revision

  • Article 368 is in Part XX of the Constitution.
  • A constitutional amendment bill may be introduced in either House of Parliament.
  • Each House must pass the bill separately; there is no joint sitting.
  • Special majority means a majority of the total membership of the House and at least two-thirds of members present and voting.
  • At least half of the State Legislatures must ratify amendments affecting specified federal provisions.
  • The President is required to give assent to a duly passed constitutional amendment bill.
  • Some constitutional changes are made by simple majority outside Article 368.
  • The 24th Amendment clarified Parliament's amending power and the requirement of Presidential assent.
  • The 42nd Amendment is commonly called the Mini Constitution.
  • The 44th Amendment restored important safeguards following the Emergency period.
  • Parliament's amending power is limited by the basic structure doctrine.
