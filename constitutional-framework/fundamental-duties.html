Fundamental Duties

Fundamental Duties are the constitutional responsibilities expected of citizens towards the nation, society and the environment. They are contained in Part IVA of the Constitution under Article 51A. They complement Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles of State Policy.

1. Constitutional background

  • Part: Part IVA.
  • Article: Article 51A.
  • Original Constitution: Fundamental Duties were not included in the Constitution as adopted in 1950.
  • Introduced by: 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976.
  • Recommendation: The Swaran Singh Committee recommended their inclusion.
  • Number: Initially ten duties; the 86th Amendment Act, 2002 added the eleventh.
  • Inspiration: Commonly associated with the Constitution of the former USSR.

The 42nd Amendment inserted Part IVA and Article 51A during the Emergency period. The duties were intended to emphasise the responsibilities of citizens alongside their constitutional rights.

2. The eleven Fundamental Duties

Article 51A states that it shall be the duty of every citizen of India:

  1. Respect the Constitution and national symbols: To abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag and the National Anthem.
  2. Follow the ideals of the freedom struggle: To cherish and follow the noble ideals that inspired the national struggle for freedom.
  3. Protect sovereignty and integrity: To uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India.
  4. Defend the country: To defend the country and render national service when called upon to do so.
  5. Promote harmony and brotherhood: To promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood amongst all the people of India, transcending religious, linguistic and regional or sectional diversities, and to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women.
  6. Preserve cultural heritage: To value and preserve the rich heritage of India's composite culture.
  7. Protect the environment: To protect and improve the natural environment, including forests, lakes, rivers and wildlife, and to have compassion for living creatures.
  8. Develop scientific temper: To develop scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform.
  9. Safeguard public property and reject violence: To safeguard public property and to abjure violence.
  10. Strive for excellence: To strive towards excellence in all spheres of individual and collective activity so that the nation constantly rises to higher levels of endeavour and achievement.
  11. Provide educational opportunities to children: Who is a parent or guardian, to provide opportunities for education to their child or ward between the ages of six and fourteen years.

3. Classification for study

The Constitution does not formally divide Fundamental Duties into categories. For easier understanding, they can be grouped by their broad purpose.

National and constitutional responsibilities

  • Respect the Constitution, its ideals and institutions, the National Flag and the National Anthem.
  • Cherish the ideals of the freedom struggle.
  • Protect the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India.
  • Defend the country and render national service when called upon.

Social and cultural responsibilities

  • Promote harmony and common brotherhood.
  • Renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women.
  • Preserve India's composite cultural heritage.
  • Develop humanism and respect for others.

Environmental and civic responsibilities

  • Protect and improve the natural environment.
  • Show compassion for living creatures.
  • Safeguard public property and reject violence.

Personal and educational responsibilities

  • Develop scientific temper, inquiry and reform.
  • Strive for excellence in individual and collective activities.
  • Parents or guardians must provide educational opportunities to children aged six to fourteen years.

4. Important constitutional amendments

Amendment Provision
42nd Amendment Act, 1976 Inserted Part IVA and Article 51A, introducing ten Fundamental Duties. The amendment followed the recommendations of the Swaran Singh Committee.
86th Amendment Act, 2002 Added Article 51A(k), making it a duty of every parent or guardian to provide educational opportunities to a child or ward between six and fourteen years. The amendment also inserted Article 21A and substituted Article 45.

5. Recommendations of the Swaran Singh Committee

The Swaran Singh Committee was constituted in 1976 to examine constitutional amendments and related matters. It recommended the inclusion of Fundamental Duties in the Constitution.

The 42nd Amendment introduced a set of duties broadly based on the committee's recommendations, but not all of its suggestions were incorporated.

Among the committee's recommendations were:

  • Citizens should have a duty to pay taxes.
  • Parliament should have the power to impose penalties for non-compliance with duties.
  • Any law imposing such penalties should not be questioned in court on the ground that it violates Fundamental Rights or is inconsistent with another constitutional provision.

These recommendations were not adopted in the form proposed. There is no separate Fundamental Duty to pay taxes in Article 51A.

6. Legal nature and enforceability

Not directly enforceable

Fundamental Duties are generally non-justiciable. A citizen cannot ordinarily be taken to court solely for violating a duty under Article 51A unless a law provides a legal consequence for the relevant conduct.

Can be supported by legislation

Parliament and State Legislatures may enact laws that give effect to the values reflected in Fundamental Duties. Existing laws may penalise conduct that also amounts to a breach of a duty.

  • The Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971 deals with certain forms of disrespect to the National Flag and National Anthem.
  • The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 supports environmental protection.
  • The Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 provides protection for wildlife.
  • The Prevention of Damage to Public Property Act, 1984 addresses damage to public property.

Applicable to citizens

Article 51A expressly refers to every citizen of India. Unlike many Fundamental Rights, which are available to all persons, Fundamental Duties are framed as responsibilities of citizens.

7. Fundamental Duties and other constitutional provisions

Provision Relationship
Part III – Fundamental Rights Fundamental Duties remind citizens that the exercise of rights should be accompanied by respect for constitutional values and the rights of others.
Part IV – DPSPs DPSPs guide the State, while Fundamental Duties describe responsibilities expected of citizens.
Article 21A Provides the right to free and compulsory education for children aged six to fourteen years.
Article 51A(k) Places a duty on parents or guardians to provide educational opportunities to children in the same age group.
Article 48A Directs the State to protect and improve the environment; Article 51A(g) places a related duty on citizens.

8. Important Supreme Court judgments

Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala (1986)

The Supreme Court protected the rights of students who respectfully stood during the National Anthem but did not sing it because of their religious beliefs. The judgment recognised that respect for the National Anthem does not necessarily require compulsory singing.

AIIMS Students' Union v. AIIMS (2001)

The Supreme Court observed that Fundamental Duties are important constitutional obligations and can be considered in interpreting constitutional and legal provisions. They are not, by themselves, directly enforceable in the same manner as Fundamental Rights.

Aruna Roy v. Union of India (2002)

The Supreme Court considered the constitutional validity of value-based education and discussed the relevance of constitutional values, including the development of scientific temper and humanism.

Union of India v. Naveen Jindal (2004)

The Supreme Court recognised the right to fly the National Flag with dignity as an expression of freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a), subject to reasonable restrictions and laws governing the National Flag. The Court also discussed the importance of respecting the Flag.

Animal Welfare Board of India v. A. Nagaraja (2014)

The Supreme Court discussed compassion for living creatures under Article 51A(g), along with constitutional and statutory protections relating to animal welfare.

9. Significance of Fundamental Duties

  • Promote civic responsibility: Encourage citizens to participate responsibly in democratic life.
  • Strengthen national unity: Emphasise sovereignty, integrity, harmony and common brotherhood.
  • Protect cultural heritage: Encourage preservation of India's composite cultural traditions.
  • Support environmental protection: Promote responsible conduct towards forests, rivers, wildlife and other living creatures.
  • Encourage scientific outlook: Promote inquiry, humanism, reform and rational thinking.
  • Protect public resources: Encourage citizens to safeguard public property and reject violence.
  • Complement constitutional rights: Reinforce the idea that constitutional democracy requires responsible conduct as well as individual freedoms.

10. Criticism and challenges

  • Non-justiciability: Most duties cannot be directly enforced without supporting legislation.
  • Vague language: Expressions such as "noble ideals", "scientific temper" and "excellence" can be interpreted in different ways.
  • Limited awareness: Many citizens have limited knowledge of the duties and their constitutional significance.
  • No explicit duty to vote or pay taxes: These were discussed in public debate and committee recommendations, but are not separately listed in Article 51A.
  • Implementation gap: Environmental degradation, damage to public property and social discrimination show the difficulty of translating constitutional values into everyday conduct.

Quick revision

  • Fundamental Duties are in Part IVA, Article 51A.
  • They were introduced by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976, following the Swaran Singh Committee's recommendations.
  • Initially there were ten duties; the 86th Amendment Act, 2002 added the eleventh.
  • They are applicable to citizens, not framed as duties of every person.
  • They are generally non-justiciable but may be supported by legislation.
  • Article 51A(g) concerns environmental protection and compassion for living creatures.
  • Article 51A(h) concerns scientific temper, humanism, inquiry and reform.
  • Article 51A(k) concerns educational opportunities for children aged six to fourteen years.
  • The Constitution does not expressly list payment of taxes or voting as separate Fundamental Duties.
