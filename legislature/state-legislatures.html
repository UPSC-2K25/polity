LEGISLATURE · CHAPTER 7

State Legislatures

India has a parliamentary system not only at the Union level but also in the States. State Legislatures make laws on matters within the legislative competence of the States, approve State expenditure and hold the State executive accountable. Their structure may be unicameral or bicameral, depending on whether the State has one legislative House or two.

1. Meaning and Constitutional Position

A State Legislature is the law-making body of a State. Under Article 168, the Legislature of a State consists of the Governor and one or two Houses, depending on the structure adopted by that State.

In a State with a unicameral legislature, there is only one House: the Legislative Assembly, also called the Vidhan Sabha.

In a State with a bicameral legislature, there are two Houses: the Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha) and the Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad).

The Governor is a constitutional component of the State Legislature because the Governor summons the Houses, addresses them in specified circumstances and acts on Bills passed by the Legislature. However, the Governor is not a member of either House.

Understand the structure: The State Legislature is not simply the Legislative Assembly. In bicameral States, it includes both the Assembly and the Council, along with the Governor as a constitutional component.

2. Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha)

The Legislative Assembly is the directly elected House of the State Legislature. It represents the people of the State and plays the central role in State law-making, financial approval and control over the Council of Ministers.

Composition

Article 170 deals with the composition of the Legislative Assemblies. Subject to constitutional provisions and specific exceptions, an Assembly consists of not more than 500 and not fewer than 60 members chosen by direct election from territorial constituencies in the State.

The number of constituencies and their territorial distribution are governed by constitutional provisions and laws made by Parliament. The principle is that representation should broadly reflect population, subject to the constitutional framework.

The Constitution permits exceptions to the general numerical limits for certain States, including provisions relating to smaller States.

Reservation of seats

Seats are reserved in State Legislative Assemblies for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in accordance with the constitutional framework.

Article 332 provides for reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in the Legislative Assemblies of the States, subject to its provisions.

Qualifications for membership

Article 173 prescribes qualifications for membership of a State Legislature. A person must:

  • Be a citizen of India.
  • Make and subscribe the prescribed oath or affirmation.
  • Be at least 25 years old to become a member of the Legislative Assembly.
  • Possess other qualifications prescribed by Parliament by law.

Duration

Under Article 172, the normal duration of a Legislative Assembly is five years from the date appointed for its first meeting, unless it is dissolved earlier.

During a Proclamation of Emergency, Parliament may extend the duration of the Assembly by law for one year at a time. However, such an extension cannot continue beyond six months after the Proclamation has ceased to operate.

Presiding officers

The Legislative Assembly elects two of its members to be Speaker and Deputy Speaker under Article 178.

  • Speaker: Presides over meetings, maintains order, interprets procedural rules and performs other functions assigned by the Constitution and rules.
  • Deputy Speaker: Performs the Speaker's duties when the office is vacant or the Speaker is absent, as provided by the Constitution and rules.

Article 179 deals with vacation, resignation and removal of the Speaker and Deputy Speaker. The Speaker may resign by writing to the Deputy Speaker, and the Deputy Speaker may resign by writing to the Speaker.

They may be removed by a resolution of the Assembly passed by a majority of all the then members of the Assembly, subject to the required notice.

Quorum and voting

Article 189 deals with voting and quorum in State Legislatures. Unless the State Legislature provides otherwise by law, the quorum to constitute a meeting of a House is ten members or one-tenth of the total number of members of the House, whichever is greater.

3. Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad)

The Legislative Council is the upper House in a bicameral State Legislature. Unlike the Legislative Assembly, it is not directly elected entirely by the people. Its membership comes from different electoral categories, including local authorities, graduates, teachers and members elected by the Legislative Assembly.

The Council is intended to provide a revising chamber where proposed legislation and public issues may receive additional consideration.

Composition

Article 171 deals with the composition of Legislative Councils. The total number of members of a Legislative Council cannot exceed one-third of the total number of members of the Legislative Assembly of that State.

The total number of members cannot ordinarily be less than 40, subject to constitutional provisions for particular States.

Method of election and nomination

The Constitution provides for different categories of membership. As nearly as may be, the composition is arranged as follows:

  • One-third: Elected by electorates consisting of members of municipalities, district boards and other local authorities specified by Parliament by law.
  • One-twelfth: Elected by electorates consisting of graduates of at least three years' standing and residing in the State, or persons possessing equivalent qualifications prescribed by Parliament by law.
  • One-twelfth: Elected by electorates consisting of persons engaged in teaching in specified educational institutions for at least three years.
  • One-third: Elected by members of the Legislative Assembly from among persons who are not members of the Assembly.
  • Remainder: Nominated by the Governor from persons having special knowledge or practical experience in literature, science, art, the cooperative movement and social service.

Elections to the relevant categories are conducted through proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote, as provided by the Constitution.

Duration

The Legislative Council is a permanent House and is not subject to dissolution. Under Article 172, approximately one-third of its members retire every second year.

This arrangement ensures continuity while allowing periodic changes in membership.

Qualifications

Article 173 provides qualifications for membership of State Legislatures. A person must be at least 30 years old to become a member of a Legislative Council.

Presiding officers

Under Article 182, the Legislative Council chooses two of its members to be Chairman and Deputy Chairman.

  • Chairman: Presides over meetings and maintains order in the Council.
  • Deputy Chairman: Performs the Chairman's duties when the office is vacant or the Chairman is absent, as provided by the Constitution and rules.

4. Creation and Abolition of Legislative Councils

Unlike the Legislative Assembly, which is a feature of every State Legislature, a Legislative Council exists only in certain States.

Article 169 provides the procedure for creating or abolishing a Legislative Council in a State.

Constitutional procedure

  1. The Legislative Assembly of the concerned State passes a resolution seeking the creation or abolition of the Council.
  2. The resolution must be passed by a majority of the total membership of the Assembly.
  3. It must also be passed by a majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of the Assembly present and voting.
  4. Parliament may then pass a law providing for the creation or abolition of the Legislative Council.

Parliament's law under Article 169 is not treated as a constitutional amendment under Article 368. It may make consequential and supplemental provisions concerning representation and other related matters.

Important distinction: The State Assembly can initiate the process by passing the required resolution, but it cannot create or abolish the Legislative Council by itself. Parliament must pass the law.

Why might a State want a Legislative Council?

Supporters of a second chamber argue that it can provide additional scrutiny, represent specialised interests and offer an opportunity for experienced persons to contribute to law-making.

Critics argue that it can increase public expenditure, delay legislation and sometimes become a political rehabilitation mechanism for individuals who have not been elected to the Legislative Assembly.

The decision therefore involves balancing the potential value of a revising chamber against its costs and practical effectiveness.

5. Legislative Powers of the State Legislature

State Legislatures make laws on matters within the legislative competence of the States. Their legislative authority is governed primarily by the distribution of legislative powers between the Union and the States.

State List

The State List in the Seventh Schedule contains subjects on which State Legislatures ordinarily have exclusive legislative competence, subject to the Constitution.

Examples include:

  • Public order, subject to constitutional provisions.
  • Police, subject to constitutional provisions.
  • Public health and sanitation.
  • Agriculture.
  • Land, subject to the relevant constitutional framework.
  • Local government.

Concurrent List

Both Parliament and State Legislatures can legislate on subjects in the Concurrent List, subject to the constitutional rules governing conflicts between Union and State laws.

Examples include:

  • Criminal law and criminal procedure, subject to constitutional provisions.
  • Marriage and divorce.
  • Education.
  • Forests.
  • Trade unions and labour welfare.

Parliament's power to legislate on State List matters

The State Legislature's authority over State List subjects is not absolute. The Constitution provides circumstances in which Parliament may legislate on matters in the State List.

  • Article 249: Parliament may legislate on a State List matter if the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution supported by not less than two-thirds of members present and voting, declaring it necessary or expedient in the national interest.
  • Article 250: Parliament may legislate on State List matters while a Proclamation of Emergency is in operation.
  • Article 252: Parliament may legislate on a State List matter for two or more States if their Legislatures pass resolutions requesting it.
  • Article 253: Parliament may make laws for implementing international treaties, agreements and conventions.
  • Article 356: During President's Rule, Parliament may exercise the legislative powers of the State Legislature, subject to the constitutional framework.
Federalism connection: State Legislatures are central to India's federal structure, but the Constitution also provides mechanisms through which Parliament can legislate on State subjects in specified circumstances.

6. Legislative Procedure in State Legislatures

The procedure for passing Bills depends on whether the State has a unicameral or bicameral legislature and on the type of Bill being considered.

Ordinary Bills in a unicameral State

In a State with only a Legislative Assembly, an Ordinary Bill is considered and passed by that Assembly. It is then presented to the Governor for action under Article 200.

Ordinary Bills in a bicameral State

In a bicameral State, an Ordinary Bill may generally be introduced in either House, subject to the applicable rules. Both Houses participate in the legislative process, but the Legislative Assembly has greater authority where the Houses disagree.

The Legislative Council can delay an Ordinary Bill but cannot permanently block it in the manner that the Rajya Sabha can block an Ordinary Bill at the Union level.

What happens when the Houses disagree?

Article 197 deals with the restriction on the powers of the Legislative Council concerning Ordinary Bills.

If the Council rejects a Bill passed by the Assembly, does not pass it within the prescribed period, or proposes amendments with which the Assembly does not agree, the Assembly may pass the Bill again.

If the Council again rejects the Bill, fails to pass it within the prescribed period or proposes amendments not accepted by the Assembly, the Bill is deemed to have been passed by both Houses in the form in which it was passed by the Assembly for the second time, subject to Article 197.

The Council's maximum delaying role is generally three months during the first consideration and one month during the second consideration.

UPSC comparison: Unlike Parliament, where Article 108 provides for joint sittings in specified cases, a State Legislature has no joint-sitting mechanism to resolve disagreement between its Assembly and Council. Article 197 instead limits the Council's power to delay Ordinary Bills.

7. Money Bills in State Legislatures

Article 199 defines a Money Bill in relation to a State Legislature. It covers matters such as taxation, borrowing by the State, custody and withdrawal of money from the Consolidated Fund of the State and appropriation of funds.

Special procedure

  • A Money Bill can be introduced only in the Legislative Assembly.
  • It cannot be introduced in the Legislative Council.
  • It requires the Governor's recommendation for introduction.
  • The Legislative Council cannot amend or reject it.
  • The Council may make recommendations to the Assembly.
  • The Council must return the Bill within 14 days.
  • The Assembly may accept or reject any or all recommendations.
  • If the Council does not return the Bill within 14 days, it is deemed to have been passed by both Houses in the form passed by the Assembly.

The Speaker of the Legislative Assembly certifies whether a Bill is a Money Bill.

8. Governor's Role in State Legislation

The Governor is a constitutional component of the State Legislature under Article 168. The Governor performs important functions in the legislative process, including summoning the Houses and acting on Bills passed by the Legislature.

Summoning, prorogation and dissolution

Article 174 empowers the Governor to summon the House or Houses of the State Legislature, prorogue the House or either House, and dissolve the Legislative Assembly.

The Constitution requires that six months should not intervene between the last sitting in one session and the first sitting in the next session.

Governor's address

Under Article 176, the Governor addresses both Houses assembled together at the commencement of the first session after each general election to the Legislative Assembly and at the commencement of the first session of each year.

The address outlines the policies and programmes of the State Government.

Assent to Bills: Article 200

When a Bill has been passed by the State Legislature, it is presented to the Governor. Under Article 200, the Governor may:

  • Give assent to the Bill.
  • Withhold assent.
  • Reserve the Bill for the consideration of the President.
  • Return a Bill, other than a Money Bill, for reconsideration by the House or Houses with a message requesting that the Legislature reconsider the Bill or specified provisions.

If the Bill is passed again by the House or Houses, with or without amendments, and presented to the Governor, the Governor shall not withhold assent. The constitutional framework still permits reservation for the President where applicable.

Reservation of Bills for the President

The Governor may reserve a Bill for the consideration of the President. The Constitution also requires reservation where, in the Governor's opinion, a Bill would so derogate from the powers of the High Court as to endanger the position which that Court is designed to fill.

President's consideration: Article 201

When a Bill is reserved for the President, Article 201 governs the President's action. The President may assent to the Bill or withhold assent.

If the Bill is not a Money Bill, the President may direct the Governor to return it to the State Legislature for reconsideration. The Legislature must reconsider it within six months from the date of receipt of the message.

If the Bill is passed again and presented to the President, the Constitution does not make assent mandatory in the same way that Article 111 does for a returned Parliamentary Bill.

Important distinction: Article 200 deals with the Governor's action on State Bills. Article 201 deals with the President's action when a State Bill is reserved for the President's consideration.

9. Financial Powers of State Legislatures

State Legislatures exercise control over State finances. The State Government cannot spend public money without legislative authorisation in accordance with the Constitution.

Annual Financial Statement

Article 202 provides for the Annual Financial Statement of a State. It is commonly known as the State Budget and presents the estimated receipts and expenditure of the State Government for a financial year.

Demands for grants

Under Article 203, demands for grants are submitted to the Legislative Assembly. The Assembly may assent to a demand, refuse it or reduce the amount specified in it.

Expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund of the State may be discussed but is not submitted to the vote of the Legislative Assembly.

Appropriation Bill

Article 204 provides for an Appropriation Bill. It authorises withdrawal of money from the Consolidated Fund of the State to meet expenditure charged on the Fund and grants made by the Legislative Assembly.

Supplementary, additional and excess grants

Article 205 provides for supplementary, additional and excess grants when the original authorisation is insufficient, a new service requires expenditure or expenditure has exceeded the amount granted.

Votes on account and other grants

Article 206 provides for votes on account, votes of credit and exceptional grants in the State financial framework.

**Remember:** In a bicameral State, the Legislative Council cannot vote on demands for grants. The Legislative Assembly has the decisive role in State financial matters.

10. Control over the State Executive

The State Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly under Article 164(2).

This principle is central to the parliamentary system at the State level. The government must retain the confidence of the Legislative Assembly to continue in office.

The Assembly exercises control through:

  • Questions to Ministers.
  • Debates and discussions on government policy.
  • Adjournment and other admissible motions.
  • Budget discussions and voting on demands for grants.
  • Committee scrutiny of departments and expenditure.
  • No-confidence motions.

The Legislative Council can debate government policies and scrutinise legislation, but it cannot remove the State Council of Ministers through a No-Confidence Motion. Collective responsibility is to the Legislative Assembly.

11. Legislative Privileges and Immunities

Article 194 deals with the powers, privileges and immunities of State Legislatures and their members.

These privileges help ensure that members can perform their legislative responsibilities without improper interference.

They include constitutional protection for freedom of speech in the Legislature, subject to the Constitution and the rules and standing orders regulating its procedure.

Members are also protected from legal proceedings in respect of anything said or any vote given by them in the Legislature or a committee, within the scope of Article 194.

The privileges of a State Legislature are not a general exemption from the law. They exist to protect the effective functioning of the legislative institution.

12. Disqualification and Anti-Defection

Members of State Legislatures may be disqualified under constitutional provisions and laws made by Parliament.

Article 191

Article 191 provides grounds on which a person may be disqualified from being chosen as, or for being, a member of a State Legislature. These include specified offices of profit, unsoundness of mind declared by a competent court, insolvency, loss of citizenship and disqualifications prescribed by parliamentary law.

Anti-defection law

The Tenth Schedule of the Constitution deals with disqualification on the ground of defection. It applies to members of State Legislatures as well as Parliament.

Broadly, a member may be disqualified on grounds such as:

  • Voluntarily giving up membership of the political party on whose ticket the member was elected.
  • Voting or abstaining contrary to a direction issued by the political party or authorised person, without prior permission and without condonation within the period specified in the Schedule.
  • An independent member joining a political party after election.
  • A nominated member joining a political party after the constitutionally permitted period.

The Speaker or Chairman of the relevant House decides questions of disqualification under the Tenth Schedule, subject to judicial review in accordance with constitutional principles.

16. Quick Revision: Test Your Understanding

Question 1

Which Article provides for the composition of State Legislatures?

Answer: Article 168.

Question 2

Which Article provides for the creation or abolition of a Legislative Council?

Answer: Article 169.

Question 3

What is the minimum age for membership of a Legislative Council?

Answer: 30 years.

Question 4

Is the Legislative Council subject to dissolution?

Answer: No. It is a permanent House, with approximately one-third of its members retiring every second year.

Question 5

Can the Legislative Council permanently block an Ordinary Bill passed by the Legislative Assembly?

Answer: No. Article 197 limits its power to delay such Bills.

Question 6

Which House of a State Legislature can remove the State Council of Ministers through a No-Confidence Motion?

Answer: The Legislative Assembly, because the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to it under Article 164(2).

Question 7

Which Article deals with the Governor's assent to State Bills?

Answer: Article 200.

Question 8

Which Article deals with the President's consideration of a State Bill reserved for the President?

Answer: Article 201.

Question 9

Can a Money Bill be introduced in the Legislative Council?

Answer: No. It can be introduced only in the Legislative Assembly.

Question 10

Which Article provides for the State Annual Financial Statement?

Answer: Article 202.

17. Chapter Summary

State Legislatures are essential institutions of India's federal parliamentary system. Every State has a Legislative Assembly, while some States also have a Legislative Council.

The Assembly is directly elected and exercises the decisive role in law-making, financial matters and control over the State executive. The Council is a permanent, indirectly constituted House that provides additional legislative scrutiny but has limited powers.

Article 169 provides the process for creating or abolishing a Legislative Council. Articles 196 to 201 regulate important aspects of State legislative procedure, including the passage of Bills and the Governor's role.

State Legislatures exercise their powers within the constitutional distribution of legislative subjects. Their authority is particularly important in areas such as public health, agriculture, police, public order and local government, subject to the Constitution.

Through legislation, financial approval, questions, debates and motions, State Legislatures hold State Governments accountable and give institutional expression to democratic representation at the State level.
