LEGISLATURE · CHAPTER 5

Parliamentary Committees

Parliament is responsible for making laws, approving public expenditure and holding the government accountable. However, the volume and complexity of parliamentary business make it difficult for the entire House to examine every matter in detail. Parliamentary committees address this difficulty by allowing smaller groups of Members of Parliament to examine legislation, expenditure, government policies and administrative functioning more closely.

1. Meaning and Purpose of Parliamentary Committees

A parliamentary committee is a group of Members of Parliament constituted or elected by a House, or appointed by its presiding officer, to examine particular matters and submit findings or recommendations.

Committees perform detailed work that cannot always be undertaken effectively during debates in the full House. They examine documents, question officials, study government programmes, review expenditure and consider the implications of proposed legislation.

A committee is not a separate legislature. It works under the authority of Parliament and within the rules and procedures prescribed by the relevant House.

Understand the idea: The House provides the broad political forum for discussion and decision-making. Committees provide a smaller working forum for detailed examination and scrutiny.

Why are committees necessary?

  • Detailed examination: Complex subjects can be studied more carefully by a smaller group of members.
  • Executive accountability: Committees examine government departments, expenditure and implementation of policies.
  • Legislative scrutiny: Bills can be examined clause by clause, allowing members to identify practical and legal difficulties.
  • Specialisation: Members can develop familiarity with particular ministries and policy areas.
  • Continuity: Committee work allows parliamentary scrutiny to continue beyond individual debates in the House.

2. Constitutional and Procedural Basis

The Constitution does not provide a complete, detailed list of all parliamentary committees. Much of the committee system has developed through the rules of procedure of the two Houses and parliamentary practice.

Article 118 empowers each House of Parliament to make rules for regulating its procedure and the conduct of its business. These rules provide an important basis for the functioning of parliamentary committees.

Some constitutional provisions are also relevant to the committee system. For example, Article 105 deals with the powers, privileges and immunities of Parliament and its members, while Article 109 establishes the special procedure for Money Bills.

Important distinction: Parliamentary committees are not all directly created by the Constitution. Many are established and regulated through parliamentary rules and resolutions.

3. Classification of Parliamentary Committees

Parliamentary committees can broadly be classified into two categories: Standing Committees and Ad Hoc Committees.

A. Standing Committees

Standing Committees are permanent or regularly constituted committees that continue from year to year, although their membership may change periodically.

They deal with recurring parliamentary responsibilities such as financial scrutiny, examination of government departments and consideration of particular institutional matters.

B. Ad Hoc Committees

Ad Hoc Committees are constituted for a specific purpose. They cease to exist after completing the task assigned to them or after submitting their report, depending on the terms of their constitution.

Select Committees and Joint Committees on Bills are examples of committees formed to examine particular legislative proposals. Parliamentary committees of inquiry may also be constituted to investigate specific matters.

Basis Standing Committees Ad Hoc Committees
Nature Permanent or regularly constituted Temporary and purpose-specific
Purpose Recurring parliamentary functions A particular inquiry or task
Duration Continue over successive periods Usually end after completing the assigned task
Examples Public Accounts Committee, Estimates Committee Select Committee on a Bill, Joint Committee on a Bill

4. Financial Committees

Financial Committees are among the most important instruments of parliamentary control over public finance. They help Parliament examine whether public money has been authorised, spent properly and used efficiently.

The three principal financial committees are:

  1. Public Accounts Committee
  2. Estimates Committee
  3. Committee on Public Undertakings

4.1 Public Accounts Committee (PAC)

The Public Accounts Committee examines the accounts showing the appropriation of sums granted by Parliament for government expenditure. It also examines reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General of India relating to such accounts and other matters assigned to it under the applicable rules.

Its central concern is whether public money was spent for the purpose authorised by Parliament and in accordance with applicable financial rules.

The PAC therefore plays an important role in examining financial irregularities, excess expenditure, weaknesses in financial control and cases where public funds may not have been used as authorised.

Composition

  • 22 members in total.
  • 15 members elected by the Lok Sabha.
  • 7 members elected by the Rajya Sabha.
  • Members are elected annually through proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote.
  • A Minister cannot be elected as a member of the committee.

By convention, the Chairperson of the Public Accounts Committee is appointed from the Opposition. This convention strengthens its role as an instrument of legislative scrutiny, although it is not a constitutional requirement.

4.2 Estimates Committee

The Estimates Committee examines estimates included in the budget and suggests improvements in public expenditure and administration. Its work is primarily concerned with economy, efficiency and administrative improvement.

It may suggest alternative policies that would make administration more efficient and examine whether money is being spent in a manner consistent with sound economic principles.

Composition

  • 30 members, all elected by the Lok Sabha.
  • Members are elected annually through proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote.
  • A Minister cannot be elected as a member.
  • It is a Lok Sabha committee; the Rajya Sabha does not contribute members to it.

4.3 Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU)

The Committee on Public Undertakings examines the reports and accounts of specified public undertakings and considers reports of the CAG relating to public undertakings, along with other matters assigned under the rules.

It examines whether public enterprises are being managed in accordance with sound business principles and prudent commercial practices.

Composition

  • 22 members in total.
  • 15 members elected by the Lok Sabha.
  • 7 members elected by the Rajya Sabha.
  • Members are elected annually through proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote.
  • A Minister cannot be elected as a member.

Comparison of the Three Financial Committees

Committee Main focus Composition
Public Accounts Committee Whether expenditure was authorised and properly incurred 15 Lok Sabha + 7 Rajya Sabha
Estimates Committee Economy, efficiency and improvements in expenditure 30 Lok Sabha members
Committee on Public Undertakings Accounts, performance and management of public undertakings 15 Lok Sabha + 7 Rajya Sabha
UPSC distinction: The PAC mainly examines expenditure after it has been incurred. The Estimates Committee examines budget estimates and suggests improvements. The Committee on Public Undertakings focuses on specified public enterprises.

5. Department-related Standing Committees (DRSCs)

Department-related Standing Committees were introduced in 1993 to strengthen parliamentary scrutiny of government ministries and departments. Their purpose is to allow Parliament to examine the work of the executive in greater detail than is generally possible during ordinary House proceedings.

These committees examine the demands for grants of the ministries and departments assigned to them, bills referred to them, annual reports and important policy documents.

Composition

  • There are 24 Department-related Standing Committees.
  • Each committee has 31 members.
  • 21 members are nominated by the Speaker of the Lok Sabha.
  • 10 members are nominated by the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha.
  • A Minister cannot be a member of a Department-related Standing Committee.
  • The term of each committee does not exceed one year.

These committees are organised around groups of ministries and departments. Their work helps Parliament examine government policy and expenditure in a more systematic manner.

Important functions

  1. Demands for Grants: Examine the demands for grants of the ministries and departments assigned to them.
  2. Bills: Examine bills referred to them by the presiding officer of the relevant House or as provided under parliamentary procedure.
  3. Annual Reports: Examine the annual reports of ministries and departments.
  4. Policy documents: Consider policy documents referred to them.
Important limitation: Department-related Standing Committees do not examine the day-to-day administration of the ministries. They also do not normally examine matters concerning the day-to-day administration of the departments or matters which are already under consideration by a parliamentary committee.

6. Other Important Parliamentary Committees

6.1 Committee on Petitions

The Committee on Petitions examines petitions presented to Parliament and referred to it under the rules of the relevant House. It may consider whether the issues raised in a petition require parliamentary attention or appropriate recommendations.

6.2 Committee of Privileges

The Committee of Privileges examines questions involving breach of parliamentary privilege or contempt of the House when matters are referred to it.

Parliamentary privileges protect the ability of Parliament and its members to perform their constitutional functions effectively. The committee assists the House in examining alleged violations of those protections.

6.3 Committee on Subordinate Legislation

Parliament often delegates limited rule-making powers to the executive through Acts of Parliament. Rules, regulations, orders and notifications made under such authority are commonly described as subordinate or delegated legislation.

The Committee on Subordinate Legislation examines whether the powers delegated by Parliament have been exercised properly and whether the rules and regulations remain within the authority granted by the parent Act.

6.4 Committee on Government Assurances

Ministers make assurances, promises and undertakings during parliamentary proceedings. The Committee on Government Assurances examines the extent to which such assurances have been implemented within the prescribed period or whether further action is required.

6.5 Business Advisory Committee

The Business Advisory Committee recommends the allocation of time for the discussion of government and other business in the House. It helps organise parliamentary business and manage the available time for debates.

6.6 Rules Committee

The Rules Committee considers matters relating to the rules of procedure and conduct of business in the House and may recommend amendments or additions to those rules.

6.7 General Purposes Committee

The General Purposes Committee considers matters concerning the affairs of the House which do not appropriately fall within the jurisdiction of another parliamentary committee.

7. Select Committees and Joint Committees on Bills

Parliamentary committees may be used to examine proposed legislation in detail. A Bill may be referred to a committee so that its provisions can be studied carefully and recommendations can be made.

Select Committee

A Select Committee is constituted by one House to examine a Bill referred to it. Its membership consists of members of that House.

Joint Committee

A Joint Committee on a Bill consists of members from both Houses. It examines the Bill referred to it and reports its findings.

Such committees may invite evidence, examine stakeholders and consider the implications of individual provisions. Their reports can help members understand the consequences of proposed legislation before Parliament takes a decision.

Remember: A Select Committee belongs to one House. A Joint Committee on a Bill includes members from both Houses.

8. How Parliamentary Committees Work

The exact procedure differs according to the type of committee and the rules governing it. However, committee examination generally follows a structured process.

  1. Constitution or referral: The committee is constituted under the relevant rules or a matter is referred to it for examination.
  2. Selection of issues: The committee identifies the specific matters or documents that require examination.
  3. Collection of information: It may seek written information, call for records and invite evidence from officials, experts or other stakeholders, subject to its powers and rules.
  4. Examination: Members discuss the evidence and analyse the matter under consideration.
  5. Preparation of report: The committee prepares its findings and recommendations.
  6. Presentation: The report is presented to the relevant House or Houses according to the applicable procedure.

Committee meetings are generally held in private. This allows members to examine sensitive or complex matters without the pressure of immediate public debate. The rules governing a particular committee determine how its proceedings and reports are handled.

9. Powers and Limitations

Powers

Parliamentary committees exercise powers assigned to them by the Constitution, parliamentary rules, resolutions and other applicable provisions. Depending on the committee, these may include:

  • Calling for information and records.
  • Inviting officials, experts and other witnesses to give evidence.
  • Examining documents and government reports.
  • Preparing findings and recommendations for Parliament.
  • Examining financial and administrative matters within their assigned jurisdiction.

Limitations

  • Recommendations are generally advisory: Committee reports do not ordinarily have the force of law.
  • Parliament retains final authority: Committees assist the Houses; they do not replace the decision-making authority of Parliament.
  • Defined jurisdiction: A committee must work within the subject and authority assigned to it.
  • No independent law-making power: A committee cannot enact legislation in place of Parliament.
  • Implementation depends on follow-up: The effectiveness of recommendations depends partly on the response of the government and further parliamentary scrutiny.

10. Importance of Parliamentary Committees in Indian Democracy

Parliamentary committees strengthen representative government by making legislative scrutiny more informed and systematic. They provide members with opportunities to examine issues beyond the limited time available during ordinary House proceedings.

Their importance can be understood through the following dimensions:

Strengthening executive accountability

Committees examine the work of ministries and departments, making it more difficult for administrative decisions and public expenditure to escape parliamentary scrutiny.

Improving the quality of legislation

Detailed examination of Bills can identify drafting problems, unintended consequences and implementation difficulties before legislation is enacted.

Improving financial discipline

Financial committees help Parliament examine whether public resources have been used for authorised purposes and whether expenditure could be made more efficient.

Encouraging informed debate

Reports and findings provide members with evidence and detailed analysis that can improve discussion in Parliament.

Providing continuity and institutional memory

Committees can follow issues over time and build knowledge about particular ministries, programmes and policy areas.

11. Challenges and Reforms

Despite their importance, parliamentary committees face several challenges that can reduce the effectiveness of legislative scrutiny.

  • Limited referrals of Bills: Not every Bill is referred to a parliamentary committee for detailed examination.
  • Time constraints: Committees may have limited time to examine complex subjects and large volumes of evidence.
  • Vacancies and attendance: Irregular attendance or vacancies can affect the quality and continuity of work.
  • Research support: Committees need adequate professional research and technical assistance to examine increasingly specialised policy areas.
  • Follow-up: Recommendations require systematic monitoring to ensure that government responses are meaningful.

Possible improvements include strengthening committee research support, ensuring timely submission of government information, improving attendance and follow-up mechanisms, and referring appropriate Bills for detailed examination.

Analytical point for UPSC: Parliamentary committees are important not simply because they produce reports, but because they improve the quality of legislative scrutiny and make executive accountability more informed.

These Articles provide the broader constitutional context. The detailed composition, jurisdiction and procedure of individual committees are primarily governed by parliamentary rules and established practice.

14. Quick Revision: Test Your Understanding

Question 1

Which committee examines whether public expenditure has been used for the purpose authorised by Parliament?

Answer: Public Accounts Committee.

Question 2

Which financial committee consists entirely of Lok Sabha members?

Answer: Estimates Committee.

Question 3

How many Department-related Standing Committees are there?

Answer: 24.

Question 4

Which Article empowers each House to make rules regulating its procedure?

Answer: Article 118.

Question 5

What is the difference between a Select Committee and a Joint Committee on a Bill?

Answer: A Select Committee consists of members of one House, whereas a Joint Committee on a Bill includes members from both Houses.

Question 6

Are committee recommendations automatically binding on the government?

Answer: No. Committee recommendations are generally advisory, and the government is expected to respond according to the applicable parliamentary procedure.

15. Chapter Summary

Parliamentary committees are an essential part of India's parliamentary system. They enable detailed examination of legislation, government expenditure, public undertakings and administrative performance.

Standing Committees deal with recurring responsibilities, while Ad Hoc Committees are constituted for specific tasks. The three financial committees — PAC, Estimates Committee and Committee on Public Undertakings — are particularly important for parliamentary control over public finance.

Department-related Standing Committees strengthen scrutiny of ministries and departments. Other committees deal with petitions, privileges, subordinate legislation, government assurances and parliamentary business.

Ultimately, committees make Parliament more informed, systematic and effective in holding the executive accountable. Their recommendations do not replace the authority of the Houses, but they significantly improve the quality of parliamentary oversight.
