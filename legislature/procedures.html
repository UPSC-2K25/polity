LEGISLATURE · CHAPTER 6

Parliamentary Procedures

Parliament does not conduct its business through unstructured discussion. Its proceedings follow constitutional provisions, rules of procedure and established parliamentary conventions. These procedures determine when Parliament meets, how members raise questions, how Bills are considered, how public money is authorised and how the government is held accountable.

1. Meaning and Importance of Parliamentary Procedure

Parliamentary procedure refers to the rules and processes through which the Houses of Parliament conduct their business. It regulates debates, questions, motions, voting, legislative proposals and financial matters.

These procedures are important because Parliament consists of representatives with different political views and interests. Without agreed rules, debates could become disorderly and decisions could be taken without adequate discussion.

Parliamentary procedure therefore performs several functions:

  • Ensures orderly conduct of parliamentary business.
  • Provides opportunities for members to express different views.
  • Allows the legislature to scrutinise the executive.
  • Regulates the passage of laws.
  • Ensures parliamentary control over public expenditure.
  • Protects the rights of members and the functioning of the Houses.
Central idea: Parliamentary procedure is not merely a set of formal rules. It is the mechanism through which representative democracy operates inside the legislature.

2. Sessions of Parliament

A session is the period during which a House meets to conduct its business. The President summons each House of Parliament from time to time.

Article 85 of the Constitution deals with the summoning, prorogation and dissolution of Parliament. It also requires that six months should not intervene between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next session.

Three commonly recognised parliamentary sessions

Budget Session

The Budget Session is generally the longest parliamentary session. It is associated with the presentation and examination of the Union Budget, demands for grants and financial legislation.

Monsoon Session

The Monsoon Session is generally held during the middle part of the year. Parliament considers legislative proposals, government business and matters of public importance.

Winter Session

The Winter Session is generally held towards the end of the year. It provides another period for legislative business, discussion and parliamentary scrutiny.

These are established parliamentary patterns, not three sessions specifically prescribed by the Constitution. The Constitution requires Parliament to meet within the prescribed six-month interval.

Summoning, adjournment, prorogation and dissolution

These terms describe different stages in the life and functioning of Parliament. They should not be treated as interchangeable.

Term Meaning Effect
Summoning Calling a House to meet Begins a session of Parliament.
Adjournment Suspending a sitting for a specified period Ends a sitting, not the session itself.
Adjournment sine die Ending a sitting without fixing a date for the next sitting Does not by itself formally end the session.
Prorogation Formal termination of a session by the President Ends the session of the House.
Dissolution Ending the life of the Lok Sabha Ends the Lok Sabha; the Rajya Sabha is not dissolved.
Important distinction: Adjournment concerns a sitting, prorogation concerns a session, and dissolution concerns the existence of the Lok Sabha itself.

What happens to pending business?

  • Prorogation does not generally cause Bills pending before Parliament to lapse.
  • A Bill pending in the Rajya Sabha but not passed by the Lok Sabha does not lapse on dissolution of the Lok Sabha.
  • A Bill pending in the Lok Sabha, or passed by the Lok Sabha and pending in the Rajya Sabha, generally lapses on dissolution of the Lok Sabha, subject to the constitutional rules.
  • A Bill on which the President has notified a joint sitting before dissolution does not lapse merely because the Lok Sabha is subsequently dissolved.

3. Conduct of Business in the House

Parliamentary business is organised according to the rules of procedure and the decisions of the presiding officer. The daily programme generally includes questions, papers laid on the table, statements, motions, Bills and other scheduled business.

Question Hour

Question Hour is the period during which members ask questions to Ministers about the functioning of their ministries and the policies and actions of the government.

It is an important instrument of executive accountability because Ministers are required to respond to questions concerning public administration.

Types of questions

  • Starred Question: A question for which an oral answer is sought. Supplementary questions may be asked after the Minister's reply.
  • Unstarred Question: A question for which a written answer is provided. Supplementary questions are not permitted in the same manner as for starred questions.
  • Short Notice Question: A question relating to a matter of urgent public importance, asked with shorter notice than ordinarily required, subject to the applicable rules.
**Remember:** A starred question involves an oral answer and permits supplementary questions. An unstarred question receives a written answer.

Zero Hour

Zero Hour is the period when members raise matters of urgent public importance, generally after Question Hour and before the regular listed business of the House.

Unlike Question Hour, Zero Hour is not specifically mentioned in the Constitution or the formal rules of procedure. It developed through parliamentary practice.

Members use it to draw the attention of the government and the House to issues that require immediate discussion or a response.

Matters under Rule 377 and Special Mentions

Members may also raise matters of public importance through procedures provided in the rules of the respective Houses. In the Lok Sabha, matters may be raised under Rule 377. In the Rajya Sabha, members may raise matters through Special Mentions.

These procedures provide opportunities to place local, regional and national issues before the House, subject to the relevant rules and the permission of the presiding officer.

4. Parliamentary Motions and Resolutions

A motion is a formal proposal placed before the House for its consideration or decision. It allows members to initiate discussion, seek a decision or express the opinion of the House on a matter.

A resolution is a particular form of proposal through which the House expresses an opinion or makes a recommendation or declaration. Some resolutions may be binding in their legal effect where the Constitution or law provides for that consequence; others are expressions of the House's opinion.

Important types of motions

4.1 No-Confidence Motion

A No-Confidence Motion is a motion through which the Lok Sabha expresses whether it continues to have confidence in the Council of Ministers.

Under Article 75(3), the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. This means that the government must retain the confidence of the Lok Sabha to remain in office.

If a No-Confidence Motion is admitted, discussed and passed by the Lok Sabha, the Council of Ministers cannot continue in office on the assumption that it retains the confidence of the House.

The motion is associated with the Lok Sabha because the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to that House, not to the Rajya Sabha.

4.2 Censure Motion

A Censure Motion is moved to express disapproval of a specific policy, action or decision of the government or a Minister.

Unlike a No-Confidence Motion, it is generally directed at a particular action or policy and is expected to state the grounds for criticism.

4.3 Adjournment Motion

An Adjournment Motion is an exceptional parliamentary device used in the Lok Sabha to draw attention to a definite matter of urgent public importance by interrupting the normal business of the House.

It is an instrument of parliamentary control over the executive. Its admission is subject to strict conditions under the rules.

4.4 Calling Attention Motion

Through a Calling Attention procedure, a member may, with the permission of the Speaker, call the attention of a Minister to a matter of urgent public importance and seek an authoritative statement from the Minister.

It provides a way to obtain a government response without necessarily converting the matter into a full debate or a motion of censure.

4.5 Privilege Motion

A Privilege Motion relates to an alleged breach of parliamentary privilege or contempt of the House. It raises the question of whether the rights and privileges necessary for parliamentary functioning have been violated.

4.6 Motion of Thanks

After the President addresses the two Houses assembled together under Article 87, each House discusses and votes on a Motion of Thanks to the President's Address.

The discussion provides an opportunity for members to debate the policies and programmes of the government outlined in the address.

4.7 No-Day-Yet-Named Motion

A motion admitted by the Speaker or Chairman for which no date has yet been fixed for discussion is commonly described as a No-Day-Yet-Named Motion.

Difference between important motions

Motion Main purpose
No-Confidence Motion Tests whether the government retains the confidence of the Lok Sabha.
Censure Motion Expresses disapproval of a particular policy or action.
Adjournment Motion Raises a definite matter of urgent public importance by interrupting normal business.
Calling Attention Seeks a Minister's statement on an urgent matter of public importance.
Privilege Motion Raises an alleged breach of parliamentary privilege.

5. Legislative Procedure: How a Bill Becomes an Act

A Bill is a proposal for legislation. It becomes an Act only after completing the constitutionally prescribed legislative process and receiving the President's assent.

The procedure differs according to the type of Bill. Ordinary Bills, Money Bills and Constitution Amendment Bills follow different constitutional routes.

5.1 Ordinary Bill

An Ordinary Bill may generally be introduced in either House of Parliament, subject to the constitutional provisions and rules governing its introduction.

First Reading: Introduction

The Bill is introduced in the House. This stage formally places the legislative proposal before Parliament.

Second Reading: Consideration

The House considers the general principles of the Bill and then examines its provisions in detail. The Bill may be considered clause by clause, and amendments may be moved and voted upon.

The House may also refer the Bill to a Select Committee, a Joint Committee or, where appropriate, a Department-related Standing Committee for detailed examination.

Third Reading: Final Passage

The House considers whether the Bill should be passed in its final form. Debate at this stage is generally limited, and the House votes on passing the Bill.

Consideration by the Other House

After being passed by one House, an Ordinary Bill is transmitted to the other House. The second House may pass it, reject it, suggest amendments or fail to agree on its provisions.

Both Houses must agree to the Bill in the same form, subject to the constitutional provisions governing joint sittings.

President's Assent

After both Houses have passed the Bill, it is presented to the President. Under Article 111, the President may give assent, withhold assent or, in the case of a Bill other than a Money Bill, return it to Parliament for reconsideration.

If Parliament passes a returned Bill again, with or without amendments, and presents it to the President, the President shall not withhold assent.

Remember: An Ordinary Bill generally requires agreement between both Houses. If the Houses disagree in the circumstances specified by Article 108, a joint sitting may be summoned.

5.2 Joint Sitting of Parliament

Article 108 provides for a joint sitting of both Houses in certain cases of disagreement over an Ordinary Bill.

A joint sitting may be summoned when:

  • One House rejects a Bill passed by the other House.
  • The Houses finally disagree on amendments to a Bill.
  • More than six months pass without the other House passing the Bill, subject to the constitutional rules for calculating the period.

The President may notify the intention to summon a joint sitting in the circumstances provided by Article 108. The joint sitting is presided over by the Speaker of the Lok Sabha, or by another person specified under the constitutional and procedural rules if the Speaker is unavailable.

When is a joint sitting not available?

  • A Money Bill cannot be referred to a joint sitting because Article 109 provides a special procedure for such Bills.
  • A Constitution Amendment Bill cannot be passed through a joint sitting. Article 368 requires each House to pass the amendment with the prescribed special majority, and where applicable, ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures.

5.3 Money Bill

A Money Bill is a Bill that contains only provisions dealing with the matters specified in Article 110, such as taxation, government borrowing, custody and withdrawal of public funds, appropriation and related matters.

A Bill is not a Money Bill merely because it involves financial expenditure. It must satisfy the constitutional definition in Article 110.

Special procedure

  • A Money Bill can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha.
  • It requires the President's recommendation for introduction.
  • The Rajya Sabha cannot amend or reject a Money Bill.
  • The Rajya Sabha may make recommendations to the Lok Sabha.
  • The Lok Sabha may accept or reject any or all recommendations.
  • The Rajya Sabha must return the Bill within 14 days.
  • If it is not returned within 14 days, it is deemed to have been passed by both Houses in the form passed by the Lok Sabha.
  • The Speaker of the Lok Sabha certifies whether a Bill is a Money Bill.
**Important:** The Rajya Sabha has a limited role in the passage of Money Bills. Its recommendations are not binding on the Lok Sabha.

5.4 Constitution Amendment Bill

Constitution Amendment Bills are governed by Article 368. They may be introduced in either House of Parliament.

A Constitution Amendment Bill must be passed separately by each House by:

  • A majority of the total membership of that House; and
  • A majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting.

Certain amendments affecting federal provisions also require ratification by the legislatures of not less than half of the States.

There is no provision for a joint sitting to resolve disagreement between the two Houses over a Constitution Amendment Bill.

Once the Bill has been passed in the required manner and, where necessary, ratified by the States, it is presented to the President, who shall give assent.

6. The Union Budget and Parliamentary Financial Procedure

Parliament exercises control over public finance by examining the government's revenue and expenditure proposals and authorising withdrawals from the Consolidated Fund of India.

Article 112 provides for the Annual Financial Statement, commonly known as the Union Budget. It presents the estimated receipts and expenditure of the Government of India for a financial year.

Stages of the Budget process

1. Presentation of the Budget

The Union Budget is presented to Parliament. It contains the government's estimates of receipts and expenditure and explains its financial proposals.

2. General Discussion

Members discuss the broad principles and policies underlying the Budget. At this stage, the detailed demands for grants are not normally put to vote.

3. Examination by Department-related Standing Committees

During the period when the Houses are not sitting, the relevant Department-related Standing Committees examine the demands for grants of the ministries and departments assigned to them and submit reports.

4. Voting on Demands for Grants

The Lok Sabha votes on the demands for grants. Each demand relates to the amount required for a particular ministry or department.

The Rajya Sabha may discuss the Budget but does not vote on demands for grants.

5. Appropriation Bill

After the demands for grants have been voted upon, an Appropriation Bill is introduced to authorise withdrawal of money from the Consolidated Fund of India to meet the expenditure charged on the Fund and the grants made by the Lok Sabha.

6. Finance Bill

The Finance Bill gives effect to the government's taxation proposals. A Finance Bill may be a Money Bill if it satisfies the requirements of Article 110.

**Key distinction:** The Appropriation Bill authorises expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India. The Finance Bill gives effect to the government's taxation proposals.

Charged expenditure and voted expenditure

The Constitution distinguishes between expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund of India and other expenditure.

  • Charged expenditure: It may be discussed in Parliament but is not submitted to the vote of the Lok Sabha.
  • Voted expenditure: It is submitted to the vote of the Lok Sabha through demands for grants.

Article 113 deals with the procedure in Parliament with respect to estimates. It provides the constitutional basis for the consideration of demands for grants.

Cut Motions

A cut motion is a proposal moved in the Lok Sabha during the discussion on demands for grants to reduce the amount of a particular demand.

Cut motions allow members to express criticism of government expenditure or policy and seek parliamentary accountability.

Types of cut motions

  • Policy Cut: Seeks to reduce the demand to Re. 1. It represents disapproval of the policy underlying the demand.
  • Economy Cut: Seeks to reduce the demand by a specified amount, expressing the view that the proposed expenditure can be reduced.
  • Token Cut: Seeks to reduce the demand by Rs. 100 to raise a specific grievance within the responsibility of the government.

Guillotine

The Lok Sabha has limited time to examine all demands for grants. When the allotted time expires, the Speaker puts the remaining demands to vote together, without further discussion.

This procedure is known as the Guillotine. It ensures that the financial business is completed within the available parliamentary timetable.

7. Other Financial Procedures

Vote on Account

A Vote on Account is an advance grant made by the Lok Sabha to enable the government to meet expenditure for a part of the financial year while the full budgetary process is completed.

It is provided for under Article 116 and is generally used when the government needs temporary authorisation to meet expenditure before the regular budgetary process is completed.

Vote of Credit

A Vote of Credit is a grant made for meeting an unexpected demand upon the resources of India when, because of the magnitude or indefinite character of the service, the demand cannot be stated with the details ordinarily given in a budget.

Exceptional Grant

An Exceptional Grant is a grant made for a special purpose that does not form part of the current service of any financial year.

Supplementary, Additional and Excess Grants

  • Supplementary Grant: Required when the amount authorised for a particular service is insufficient for the current financial year.
  • Additional Grant: Required when a need arises for expenditure on a new service not contemplated in the original budget for that year.
  • Excess Grant: Required when money has been spent on a service beyond the amount granted for that service in a financial year. It is presented for parliamentary regularisation after the expenditure has occurred.

8. Voting and Decision-Making in Parliament

Parliament makes decisions through voting in accordance with the Constitution and the rules of procedure.

Ordinary majority

Unless the Constitution provides otherwise, questions in either House are generally decided by a majority of members present and voting.

This is commonly described as a simple or effective voting majority for the particular question. Members who abstain are not counted as members voting.

Special majority

Certain constitutional decisions require a special majority. For example, a Constitution Amendment Bill under Article 368 requires:

  • A majority of the total membership of each House; and
  • A majority of not less than two-thirds of members present and voting in each House.

Quorum

Quorum is the minimum number of members required to be present for a meeting of the House to conduct business.

Under Article 100(3), until Parliament otherwise provides by law, the quorum to constitute a meeting of either House is one-tenth of the total number of members of that House.

Speaker's casting vote

Under Article 100(1), the presiding officer of a House does not ordinarily vote in the first instance. In the event of an equality of votes, the presiding officer exercises a casting vote.

This means the Speaker or Chairman can decide a question when the votes are equally divided.

9. Parliamentary Control over the Executive

India's parliamentary system is based on the principle that the executive is accountable to the legislature. The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha under Article 75(3).

Parliamentary procedures provide several ways to exercise this accountability.

  • Questions: Members seek information and explanations from Ministers.
  • Debates: Members discuss government policies and decisions.
  • Motions: Members may seek formal decisions or express disapproval.
  • Budgetary control: The Lok Sabha examines demands for grants and authorises expenditure.
  • Committee scrutiny: Committees examine administrative and financial performance in detail.
  • No-Confidence Motion: The Lok Sabha can withdraw its confidence from the Council of Ministers.

These procedures ensure that the government must explain its actions and maintain the confidence of the elected House.

12. Test Your Understanding

Question 1

Which Article provides for summoning and prorogation of Parliament?

Answer: Article 85.

Question 2

What is the minimum quorum required for a meeting of either House of Parliament under Article 100(3), unless Parliament provides otherwise by law?

Answer: One-tenth of the total number of members of that House.

Question 3

Can the Rajya Sabha reject a Money Bill?

Answer: No. It may make recommendations, but the Lok Sabha is not bound to accept them.

Question 4

Which Article provides for a joint sitting of Parliament?

Answer: Article 108.

Question 5

Can a Constitution Amendment Bill be passed through a joint sitting?

Answer: No. Each House must separately pass it with the prescribed special majority.

Question 6

Which House votes on demands for grants?

Answer: The Lok Sabha. The Rajya Sabha may discuss the Budget but does not vote on demands for grants.

Question 7

What is the difference between an Adjournment Motion and a No-Confidence Motion?

Answer: An Adjournment Motion raises a definite matter of urgent public importance by interrupting normal business. A No-Confidence Motion tests whether the Council of Ministers retains the confidence of the Lok Sabha.

Question 8

What happens when the Lok Sabha's allotted time for voting on demands for grants expires?

Answer: The remaining demands are put to vote together through the Guillotine procedure.

13. Chapter Summary

Parliamentary procedures provide the framework through which Parliament conducts its legislative, financial and accountability functions. They regulate sessions, debates, questions, motions, voting and the passage of legislation.

The distinction between adjournment, prorogation and dissolution is important for understanding the life and functioning of Parliament. Question Hour, Zero Hour and parliamentary motions provide opportunities to question the government and raise matters of public importance.

Legislative procedure varies according to the type of Bill. Ordinary Bills generally require agreement between both Houses. Money Bills follow the special procedure in Articles 109 and 110. Constitution Amendment Bills require the special majority under Article 368 and, in certain cases, ratification by the States.

Through the Budget, demands for grants, cut motions and financial committees, Parliament exercises control over public expenditure. These procedures are central to the accountability of the executive in India's parliamentary democracy.
