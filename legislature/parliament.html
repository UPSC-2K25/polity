CHAPTER 01 · UNION LEGISLATURE

Parliament of India The constitutional institution of national representation.

Parliament is the central legislative institution of the Union. It represents the people and the States in the national law-making process, authorises public expenditure, deliberates on national issues and holds the Union executive accountable.

Chapter contents

01 · FOUNDATION

Meaning and role of Parliament

In a parliamentary democracy, the legislature is one of the principal organs of government. It provides a formal institution through which representatives participate in public decision-making, make laws and scrutinise the actions of the executive.

In India, Parliament performs these responsibilities at the Union level. It is not merely a law-making body. It is also a forum for political debate, financial supervision, representation and accountability.

Why does India need a Parliament?

India is a large and diverse constitutional democracy. Public decisions affect different States, regions, communities and economic interests. Parliament provides an institutional forum in which these interests can be represented and debated before national decisions are made.

Its importance can be understood through five interconnected responsibilities:

  • Representation: Elected representatives articulate the concerns and interests of citizens.
  • Legislation: Parliament makes laws on subjects within its constitutional legislative competence.
  • Financial control: The Union government cannot ordinarily spend public money without parliamentary authorisation in accordance with the Constitution.
  • Executive accountability: Ministers are answerable to Parliament, particularly the Lok Sabha, for the conduct of government.
  • Deliberation: Members debate public issues, government policies and matters of national importance.
Important distinction

Parliament makes laws, but it does not administer the country on a day-to-day basis. Administration is primarily the responsibility of the executive. Parliament exercises legislative and supervisory functions within the constitutional framework.

02 · CONSTITUTIONAL DEVELOPMENT

Historical and constitutional background

India's parliamentary system developed through constitutional and legislative changes during British rule. These developments gradually introduced representative institutions, legislative councils and greater participation by Indians in governance.

Development before the Constitution

The Indian Councils Act of 1861 introduced legislative councils with limited Indian participation. Subsequent reforms expanded the legislative institutions and introduced additional representative elements.

The Indian Councils Act of 1909 expanded legislative councils and introduced separate electorates for certain communities. The Government of India Act, 1919 introduced a bicameral central legislature.

The Government of India Act, 1935 provided for a federal legislative structure, although its proposed all-India federation did not come into operation as originally envisaged.

The Constituent Assembly

The Constituent Assembly deliberated on the institutional structure of independent India. The Constitution adopted a parliamentary form of government and established a bicameral Parliament at the Union level.

The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 and came into force on 26 January 1950.

Why is the historical background important?

It helps explain why the Indian Constitution combines representative government, parliamentary accountability and a federal second chamber. These are not isolated features; they are part of the institutional design of the Constitution.

03 · ARTICLE 79

Composition of Parliament

Article 79 provides that there shall be a Parliament for the Union consisting of the President and two Houses, known respectively as the Council of States and the House of the People.

These two Houses are commonly known as the Rajya Sabha and Lok Sabha.

Constitutional composition

Parliament = President + Rajya Sabha + Lok Sabha.

The President is a constituent part of Parliament, but is not a member of either House.

Why is the President included in Parliament?

The President is included because the Constitution assigns an essential role to the office in the legislative process. Parliament's legislative functioning involves constitutional functions such as summoning the Houses, addressing them and giving assent to Bills.

A Bill passed by Parliament ordinarily requires the President's assent to become law, subject to the special constitutional provisions governing different types of Bills.

The two Houses of Parliament

Lok Sabha

House of the People

  • Directly elected House.
  • Represents the people.
  • Normally has a five-year term.
  • Can be dissolved.
  • Has special financial powers.
  • Exercises decisive control over the Council of Ministers.

Rajya Sabha

Council of States

  • Predominantly indirectly elected.
  • Represents the States and Union territories.
  • Is a permanent House.
  • Is not subject to dissolution.
  • One-third of its members retire every second year.
  • Has special powers under Articles 249 and 312.

Why does Parliament have two Houses?

Bicameralism means that the legislature has two chambers. India adopted a bicameral Parliament to combine popular representation with representation of the States.

The Lok Sabha represents citizens directly through elections. The Rajya Sabha provides a continuing federal chamber in which States participate in national legislative deliberations.

The second chamber also provides an additional opportunity to examine proposed legislation and reconsider decisions taken by the first House.

04 · INSTITUTIONAL RESPONSIBILITIES

Powers and functions of Parliament

1. Legislative functions

Parliament makes laws for the whole or any part of the territory of India, subject to the distribution of legislative powers established by the Constitution.

The Seventh Schedule distributes legislative subjects among the Union List, State List and Concurrent List. Parliament has exclusive legislative competence over the Union List and can legislate on the Concurrent List, subject to constitutional rules.

Parliament may also legislate on State List matters in specified circumstances, including under Articles 249, 250 and 252.

2. Financial functions

Parliament exercises control over Union finances. Government expenditure, taxation and public borrowing operate within the constitutional financial framework.

The annual financial statement, commonly called the Union Budget, is laid before Parliament under Article 112. The Lok Sabha has the primary role in voting on demands for grants.

The Rajya Sabha may discuss the annual financial statement but does not vote on demands for grants. Money Bills also follow a special procedure under Article 109.

3. Control over the executive

India follows the parliamentary system in which the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha under Article 75(3).

This means that the Council of Ministers must retain the confidence of the Lok Sabha to remain in office. If it loses that confidence, it cannot ordinarily continue as the government.

Parliament also examines government performance through questions, debates, motions, discussions and parliamentary committees.

4. Constituent functions

Parliament participates in constitutional amendment under Article 368. A constitutional amendment Bill must satisfy the special procedure and voting requirements laid down in that Article.

Certain amendments also require ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures.

5. Electoral functions

Elected members of both Houses of Parliament participate in the election of the President along with elected members of State Legislative Assemblies and the relevant Union territory Assemblies.

Members of both Houses also participate in the election of the Vice-President.

6. Judicial and quasi-judicial functions

Parliament exercises certain constitutional functions involving removal or impeachment proceedings against specified constitutional officeholders.

These include the impeachment of the President, removal of Supreme Court and High Court judges under the prescribed constitutional procedure, and removal proceedings relating to certain other constitutional authorities.

7. Deliberative functions

Parliament provides a national platform for discussing public policy, national problems, international developments and government decisions.

These discussions enable representatives to communicate public concerns and place government actions under public and parliamentary scrutiny.

06 · QUICK REVISION

What you should remember

  1. Article 79 establishes Parliament as consisting of the President and two Houses.
  2. The two Houses are the Rajya Sabha and Lok Sabha.
  3. The President is part of Parliament but is not a member of either House.
  4. Lok Sabha represents the people directly. Rajya Sabha provides representation of States and certain Union territories.
  5. Rajya Sabha is a permanent House, while Lok Sabha is subject to dissolution.
  6. The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to Lok Sabha under Article 75(3).
  7. Parliament performs legislative, financial, executive-control, electoral, constituent, judicial and deliberative functions.

Check your understanding

Question 1. Which of the following correctly describes the constitutional composition of Parliament?

Answer: The President, Rajya Sabha and Lok Sabha.

Question 2. Is the President a member of either House of Parliament?

Answer: No. The President is a constituent part of Parliament but is not a member of either House.

Question 3. To which House is the Union Council of Ministers collectively responsible?

Answer: The Lok Sabha, under Article 75(3).
