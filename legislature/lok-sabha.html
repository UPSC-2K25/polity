Indian Legislature · Chapter 02

Lok Sabha

The Lok Sabha is the directly elected House of the Indian Parliament. It represents the people of India and plays the central role in legislation, financial control and holding the Union government accountable.

1. Meaning and constitutional position

The Lok Sabha is the lower House of the Parliament of India. It is commonly called the House of the People because its members are elected directly by citizens through general elections.

India follows a parliamentary system of government. In this system, the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. This makes the Lok Sabha especially important in determining whether the Union government can continue in office.

The Constitution does not treat the Lok Sabha as an independent legislature separate from Parliament. It is one of the two Houses of Parliament, the other being the Rajya Sabha. The President is also a part of Parliament under the constitutional scheme.

Understand the distinction: The Lok Sabha represents the people through direct elections. The Rajya Sabha represents the States and Union Territories through an indirect electoral process, subject to constitutional provisions. Both participate in ordinary law-making, but their powers are not identical in every matter.

2. Composition of the Lok Sabha

Article 81 deals with the composition of the House of the People. It provides the constitutional framework for the representation of States and Union Territories in the Lok Sabha.

Members representing States are chosen through direct elections from territorial constituencies. The allocation of seats among States is based on the constitutional framework, including the principle of population, subject to the provisions and adjustments contained in the Constitution.

Parliament may by law provide for the representation of Union Territories in the Lok Sabha. Their representation is therefore governed by the Constitution and the laws made under it.

Territorial constituencies

For the purpose of elections, the country is divided into territorial constituencies. Each constituency elects a representative to the Lok Sabha.

The general principle is that constituencies should provide representation on a population basis, subject to constitutional provisions. Delimitation is the process of determining or readjusting constituency boundaries and the allocation of seats.

Reserved constituencies

The Constitution provides for reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in the Lok Sabha under Article 330. These constituencies are reserved for candidates belonging to the relevant communities, while all eligible voters in the constituency participate in the election.

Important distinction: A reserved constituency does not mean that only members of the reserved community can vote. All eligible electors in that constituency vote, but only candidates belonging to the relevant reserved category may contest from that reserved seat.

3. Election of Lok Sabha members

Members of the Lok Sabha are elected directly by the people. Elections are conducted on the basis of adult suffrage, subject to the constitutional and statutory qualifications and disqualifications.

Universal adult suffrage

Article 326 provides for elections to the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies on the basis of adult suffrage. Every citizen who is at least 18 years of age and is not otherwise disqualified under the Constitution or any law made by Parliament is entitled to be registered as a voter, subject to the applicable electoral laws.

First-past-the-post system

Lok Sabha elections generally follow the first-past-the-post system. Under this system, the candidate who receives the highest number of valid votes in a constituency is elected.

A candidate does not need to secure more than half of all votes cast. Winning more votes than each individual rival is sufficient.

Example: Suppose three candidates receive 40,000, 35,000 and 25,000 votes. The candidate receiving 40,000 votes wins, even though that candidate has not received more than 50% of the total votes.

Role of the Election Commission

Article 324 vests the superintendence, direction and control of elections to Parliament, State Legislatures and the offices specified in the Article in the Election Commission of India. The Commission administers the electoral process within this constitutional framework.

4. Qualifications for membership

Article 84 lays down the constitutional qualifications for membership of Parliament. Further qualifications and disqualifications are provided under parliamentary law, particularly the Representation of the People Act, 1951.

A person must satisfy the following basic requirements:

  • Be a citizen of India.
  • Make and subscribe the prescribed oath or affirmation.
  • Be at least 25 years of age for election to the Lok Sabha.
  • Possess such other qualifications as may be prescribed by or under a law made by Parliament.

Disqualifications

Article 102 specifies constitutional grounds on which a person may be disqualified from being chosen as, or from continuing as, a member of either House of Parliament.

These include, subject to constitutional exceptions:

  • Holding an office of profit under the Union or a State government.
  • Being of unsound mind and so declared by a competent court.
  • Being an undischarged insolvent.
  • Not being a citizen of India, or acquiring certain foreign citizenship or allegiance.
  • Being disqualified under a law made by Parliament.

Disqualification under the Tenth Schedule, which deals with defection, is also an important ground affecting membership of the House.

UPSC distinction: Article 84 deals with qualifications for membership. Article 102 deals with disqualifications. These provisions should not be confused.

5. Duration and dissolution

Article 83(2) provides that the Lok Sabha, unless sooner dissolved, continues for five years from the date appointed for its first meeting. The expiry of this period results in the dissolution of the House.

The President may dissolve the Lok Sabha before the completion of its normal term, in accordance with the parliamentary constitutional framework.

While a Proclamation of Emergency is in operation, Parliament may extend the normal term of the Lok Sabha by law for a period not exceeding one year at a time. Such an extension cannot continue beyond six months after the Proclamation has ceased to operate.

What happens after dissolution?

Once the Lok Sabha is dissolved, its members cease to hold membership of that House. A general election is held to constitute a new Lok Sabha.

The Rajya Sabha, unlike the Lok Sabha, is a permanent House and is not subject to dissolution. This is one of the fundamental institutional differences between the two Houses.

6. Speaker and Deputy Speaker

The Lok Sabha elects one of its members to be the Speaker and another member to be the Deputy Speaker. Article 93 provides for these offices.

Speaker

The Speaker presides over the sittings of the Lok Sabha and is responsible for maintaining order and ensuring that the proceedings of the House are conducted according to the Constitution, parliamentary rules and established conventions.

The important functions of the Speaker include:

  • Presiding over sittings of the Lok Sabha.
  • Maintaining order and decorum in the House.
  • Deciding points of order under the applicable rules.
  • Interpreting and applying the rules of procedure.
  • Certifying whether a Bill is a Money Bill under Article 110.
  • Exercising powers relating to disqualification under the Tenth Schedule, subject to judicial review.
  • Presiding over joint sittings of Parliament under Article 108.

Deputy Speaker

The Deputy Speaker performs the duties of the Speaker when the office is vacant or when the Speaker is absent from a sitting. The Deputy Speaker is also elected by the Lok Sabha from among its members.

Removal and resignation

Under Article 94, the Speaker or Deputy Speaker may resign by writing to the other presiding officer, as applicable. Either may also be removed by a resolution of the Lok Sabha passed by a majority of all the then members of the House, subject to the constitutional notice requirement.

Important: The Speaker does not automatically vacate office immediately upon dissolution of the Lok Sabha. Under Article 94, the Speaker continues in office until immediately before the first meeting of the new Lok Sabha.

7. Powers and functions of the Lok Sabha

7.1 Legislative powers

The Lok Sabha participates in the law-making process of Parliament. Ordinary Bills may generally be introduced in either House and must ordinarily be passed by both Houses before being presented to the President for assent.

In case of a disagreement between the two Houses over an ordinary Bill, a joint sitting may be summoned under Article 108, subject to the constitutional conditions.

7.2 Financial powers

The Lok Sabha has a special position in financial matters. A Money Bill can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha and only on the recommendation of the President.

After the Lok Sabha passes a Money Bill, it is transmitted to the Rajya Sabha. The Rajya Sabha may make recommendations, but it cannot amend or reject the Bill. It must return the Bill within 14 days. The Lok Sabha may accept or reject any or all of its recommendations.

The Lok Sabha also votes on Demands for Grants. No demand for a grant can be made except on the recommendation of the President.

7.3 Control over the executive

The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha under Article 75(3). This principle forms the foundation of parliamentary accountability.

The Lok Sabha exercises control through parliamentary questions, debates, motions, discussions and committees. A successful no-confidence motion demonstrates that the government no longer enjoys the confidence of the House.

7.4 Constituent powers

The Lok Sabha participates in the amendment of the Constitution under Article 368. A Constitution Amendment Bill must be passed by each House separately with the special majority prescribed by that Article.

Where an amendment requires ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures, that ratification must also be obtained before the amendment is presented for the President's assent.

7.5 Electoral functions

Elected members of the Lok Sabha participate in the election of the President of India. The elected members of both Houses of Parliament also participate in the election of the Vice-President.

The Lok Sabha also elects its Speaker and Deputy Speaker.

7.6 Judicial and quasi-judicial functions

Parliament has certain constitutional functions relating to the removal of high constitutional authorities. These functions are exercised through prescribed procedures and cannot be treated as ordinary legislative decisions.

For example, the Lok Sabha participates in the impeachment process of the President and in proceedings for the removal of judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, subject to the relevant constitutional requirements.

8. Why is the Lok Sabha more powerful in financial matters?

The Lok Sabha is directly elected by the people and the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to it. The Constitution therefore gives the Lok Sabha a decisive role in financial control over the Union government.

This does not mean that the Rajya Sabha has no role in financial legislation. It participates in the process, but its powers regarding Money Bills and Demands for Grants are constitutionally limited.

UPSC trap: Every Financial Bill is not a Money Bill. Article 110 defines a Money Bill. Article 117 deals with Financial Bills. The two categories have different constitutional procedures.

9. Sessions and proceedings

Article 85 empowers the President to summon each House of Parliament to meet at such time and place as the President thinks fit. However, six months must not intervene between the last sitting in one session and the date appointed for the first sitting in the next session.

Parliament commonly meets in three sessions in a year: the Budget Session, Monsoon Session and Winter Session. These are established parliamentary practices rather than three sessions specifically named in the Constitution.

Question Hour

Question Hour is the period during which members ask questions to Ministers about government policies, administration and public issues. It is an important instrument of executive accountability.

Zero Hour

Zero Hour is a parliamentary practice that allows members to raise matters of urgent public importance. It is not formally mentioned in the Constitution or the Rules of Procedure as a separate constitutional institution.

Adjournment and prorogation

Adjournment suspends the sitting of the House for a specified period. Prorogation terminates a session of Parliament. Dissolution ends the life of the Lok Sabha. These are distinct parliamentary concepts.

10. Anti-defection law and Lok Sabha membership

The Tenth Schedule of the Constitution contains provisions relating to disqualification on the ground of defection. It was inserted by the 52nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1985, and later modified by the 91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003.

A member may face disqualification on grounds such as voluntarily giving up membership of the political party or voting, or abstaining from voting, contrary to a direction issued by the party, without obtaining permission and without condonation within the prescribed period.

The Speaker of the Lok Sabha decides questions of disqualification under the Tenth Schedule in relation to members of the Lok Sabha. The decision is subject to judicial review.

Remember: The Tenth Schedule deals with disqualification on the ground of defection. It is different from the general grounds of disqualification contained in Article 102.

12. Quick revision: What you should remember

  • Lok Sabha is the directly elected House of Parliament.
  • Article 81 deals with its composition.
  • Article 83(2) provides its normal five-year duration.
  • Article 84 deals with qualifications for membership.
  • Article 93 provides for the Speaker and Deputy Speaker.
  • Article 110 defines a Money Bill.
  • Article 75(3) establishes collective responsibility of the Council of Ministers to the Lok Sabha.
  • The Lok Sabha has the decisive role in Money Bill procedure and voting on Demands for Grants.
  • The Lok Sabha may be dissolved; the Rajya Sabha is a permanent House.
  • The Tenth Schedule deals with disqualification on the ground of defection.

13. Check your understanding

Question 1

Which Article deals with the composition of the Lok Sabha?

Answer: Article 81.

Question 2

Can a Money Bill be introduced in the Rajya Sabha?

Answer: No. It can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha.

Question 3

Is the Speaker's decision on disqualification under the Tenth Schedule completely beyond judicial review?

Answer: No. Such decisions are subject to judicial review.

Question 4

Does the Lok Sabha continue for five years in every circumstance?

Answer: No. It may be dissolved earlier, and its term may be extended during a Proclamation of Emergency in accordance with Article 83(2).
