Judicial Review

Judicial review is the power of constitutional courts to examine the validity of legislative enactments, executive actions and, within constitutional limits, constitutional amendments. It protects the supremacy of the Constitution and ensures that public authorities exercise power within their legal and constitutional boundaries.

1. Meaning and Constitutional Significance

India has a written Constitution that distributes powers among the Union, the States and different organs of government. The Constitution is the supreme law of the land. Parliament, State Legislatures and executive authorities derive their powers from it and must act within its limitations.

Judicial review is the authority of the Supreme Court and High Courts to determine whether a law or governmental action is consistent with the Constitution. Where a measure violates constitutional provisions, the courts may declare it invalid to the extent of the inconsistency.

The Indian Constitution does not place judicial review in one single article. Instead, the power emerges from several provisions dealing with fundamental rights, writ jurisdiction, appellate jurisdiction, legislative competence and constitutional supremacy.

Core principle: Judicial review makes the Constitution enforceable against the legislature and the executive. It is an essential safeguard against arbitrary or unconstitutional exercise of public power.

2. Constitutional Provisions Supporting Judicial Review

Several constitutional provisions collectively establish the framework for judicial review in India.

Important distinction: Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right. Article 226 gives High Courts a wider writ jurisdiction because it extends not only to Fundamental Rights but also to other legal purposes.

3. Judicial Review under Article 13

Article 13 is one of the principal constitutional foundations of judicial review. It gives effect to the supremacy of Fundamental Rights by restricting the law-making power of the State.

Article 13(1): Pre-Constitution laws

All laws in force in India immediately before the commencement of the Constitution are void to the extent that they are inconsistent with Fundamental Rights.

Article 13(2): Post-Constitution laws

The State shall not make any law that takes away or abridges the Fundamental Rights conferred by Part III. A law made in contravention of this provision is void to the extent of the contravention.

Article 13(3): Meaning of law

The expression "law" includes ordinances, orders, bye-laws, rules, regulations, notifications, customs and usages having the force of law.

Article 13(4): Constitutional amendments

Article 13 does not apply to constitutional amendments made under Article 368. However, this does not make constitutional amendments immune from judicial scrutiny. Under the basic structure doctrine, the Supreme Court can invalidate an amendment that damages or destroys the basic structure of the Constitution.

UPSC point: Do not confuse Article 13(4) with complete immunity for constitutional amendments. The basic structure doctrine provides a separate constitutional limitation on the amending power.

4. Grounds of Judicial Review

Courts may examine the constitutional validity of laws and government actions on several grounds. The precise grounds depend on the nature of the measure and the constitutional provision involved.

4.1 Violation of Fundamental Rights

A law or executive action may be invalidated if it violates a Fundamental Right. For example, an arbitrary State action may be challenged under Article 14, while restrictions on speech may be examined under Article 19.

4.2 Lack of legislative competence

Parliament and State Legislatures must legislate within the fields assigned to them by the Constitution. A law may be challenged if the legislature that enacted it lacked the necessary constitutional competence.

4.3 Violation of constitutional procedure

A law or constitutional measure may be challenged where mandatory constitutional requirements governing its enactment have not been followed.

4.4 Repugnancy between Union and State laws

Under Article 254, where a State law is repugnant to a valid Parliamentary law on a Concurrent List subject, the Parliamentary law generally prevails and the State law is void to the extent of repugnancy. Article 254 also contains a qualification concerning State laws reserved for and receiving Presidential assent.

4.5 Arbitrariness and unreasonableness

State action can be examined for arbitrariness, particularly under Article 14. Administrative decisions must satisfy applicable constitutional and legal standards.

4.6 Violation of the basic structure

A constitutional amendment may be reviewed if it damages or destroys the basic structure of the Constitution.

5. Basic Structure Doctrine

The basic structure doctrine is one of the most significant contributions of the Indian Supreme Court to constitutional law. It places substantive limits on Parliament's power to amend the Constitution under Article 368.

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)

In this landmark judgment, a thirteen-judge Bench of the Supreme Court held by a narrow majority that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights, but cannot alter or destroy its basic structure.

The Court did not provide an exhaustive list of basic features. Their identification has developed through subsequent judgments.

Features recognised as part of the basic structure

  • Supremacy of the Constitution.
  • Republican and democratic form of government.
  • Secular character of the Constitution.
  • Federal character of the Constitution.
  • Separation of powers.
  • Independence of the judiciary.
  • Judicial review.
  • Rule of law.
  • Free and fair elections.
  • Parliamentary system of government.
  • Unity and integrity of India.
  • Balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.
Remember: The basic structure doctrine does not prevent Parliament from amending the Constitution. It prevents amendments that damage or destroy the Constitution's basic identity.

6. Important Constitutional Amendments and Judicial Review

Amendment Important change Connection with judicial review
24th Amendment, 1971 Expressly affirmed Parliament's constituent power to amend the Constitution and made changes to Articles 13 and 368. Was examined in Kesavananda Bharati.
25th Amendment, 1971 Introduced Article 31C in relation to laws implementing specified Directive Principles. Its provisions were considered in Kesavananda Bharati.
42nd Amendment, 1976 Attempted to expand the protection under Article 31C and restrict judicial scrutiny of constitutional amendments through changes to Article 368. Important provisions were invalidated in Minerva Mills.
43rd Amendment, 1977 Removed several changes made by the 42nd Amendment that had restricted judicial powers. Restored important aspects of judicial review and constitutional adjudication.
44th Amendment, 1978 Introduced important safeguards relating to Fundamental Rights and emergency provisions. Strengthened constitutional safeguards, including protection against certain abuses of emergency power.

Article 31C: A significant qualification

Article 31C protects laws genuinely giving effect to the principles specified in Article 39(b) and Article 39(c) from challenge under Articles 14 and 19, subject to constitutional interpretation and judicial review. The broader protection attempted by the 42nd Amendment was invalidated in Minerva Mills.

7. Important Supreme Court Judgments

Case Year Principle established or clarified
Shankari Prasad v. Union of India 1951 Held that a constitutional amendment was not "law" within the meaning of Article 13 as then interpreted.
Golaknath v. State of Punjab 1967 Held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights; this position was later changed by Kesavananda Bharati.
Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala 1973 Established the basic structure limitation on Parliament's amending power.
Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain 1975 Applied the basic structure doctrine to invalidate a constitutional amendment provision concerning election disputes.
Minerva Mills v. Union of India 1980 Reaffirmed limited amending power and the importance of judicial review and the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.
S.P. Sampath Kumar v. Union of India 1987 Considered the role of tribunals in relation to the jurisdiction of High Courts.
L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India 1997 Held that judicial review under Articles 32 and 226/227 is part of the basic structure and tribunal decisions remain subject to scrutiny by High Courts.
S.R. Bommai v. Union of India 1994 Confirmed that the exercise of power under Article 356 is subject to judicial review.
I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu 2007 Held that laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 can be reviewed if they damage the basic structure through violation of protected constitutional principles.

8. Judicial Review of Ninth Schedule Laws

The Ninth Schedule was introduced by the First Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951, along with Article 31B. Its original purpose was to provide protection to specified laws from challenges based on certain Fundamental Rights.

Initially, laws placed in the Ninth Schedule received substantial protection. However, this protection is not absolute.

I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007)

The Supreme Court held that laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973, the date of the Kesavananda Bharati judgment, are open to judicial review. If such a law violates Fundamental Rights in a manner that damages or destroys the basic structure, it can be declared unconstitutional.

UPSC distinction: Placement of a law in the Ninth Schedule does not automatically make it immune from judicial review. The date of insertion and the effect of the law on the basic structure are important.

9. Judicial Review by the Supreme Court and High Courts

Basis Supreme Court High Courts
Constitutional provision Article 32 and other jurisdictional provisions Articles 226 and 227
Fundamental Rights Can issue writs for enforcement of Fundamental Rights. Can issue writs for enforcement of Fundamental Rights.
Other legal purposes Article 32 is specifically for enforcement of Fundamental Rights. Article 226 extends to other legal purposes as well.
Territorial reach Jurisdiction extends throughout India, subject to the applicable constitutional provisions. Generally operates within the territorial jurisdiction specified under Article 226.
Supervision No general supervisory jurisdiction over all subordinate courts comparable to Article 227. Exercises superintendence over subordinate courts and tribunals under Article 227, subject to exceptions.

High Courts play a particularly important role because a person can approach the relevant High Court under Article 226 for enforcement of Fundamental Rights as well as other legal rights. The Supreme Court's jurisdiction under Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right, but it is confined to the enforcement of Fundamental Rights.

10. Judicial Review of Executive Action

Judicial review is not limited to legislation. Courts can also examine executive decisions, administrative orders and actions of public authorities.

Executive action may be challenged where it is:

  • Beyond the authority granted by law.
  • Contrary to constitutional provisions.
  • Arbitrary or discriminatory.
  • Based on an error of law.
  • Affected by mala fides or improper purpose.
  • In violation of principles of natural justice, where those principles apply.
  • Unreasonable or procedurally unfair under the applicable legal standard.

Judicial review generally examines the legality and constitutionality of administrative action. It does not ordinarily mean that the court substitutes its own preferred administrative decision for that of the competent authority.

11. Judicial Review and Judicial Activism

Judicial review and judicial activism are related but distinct concepts. Judicial review refers to the constitutional or legal power of courts to examine the validity of governmental action. Judicial activism describes a more interventionist approach to adjudication in which courts may interpret rights and constitutional duties expansively in appropriate cases.

Judicial Review Judicial Activism
A constitutional and legal power of courts. An approach or style of judicial decision-making.
Examines the legality and constitutionality of laws and actions. May involve proactive interpretation and directions to address rights violations or governance failures.
Can result in invalidation of unconstitutional measures. May lead to broader remedies or judicial directions within legal limits.
Is an established feature of constitutional adjudication. Is debated in relation to institutional boundaries and separation of powers.

Judicial activism must operate within the Constitution. Courts are expected to respect the roles of the legislature and executive while protecting constitutional rights and enforcing legal limitations.

12. Importance of Judicial Review

  • Constitutional supremacy: Ensures that all organs of government remain subject to the Constitution.
  • Protection of Fundamental Rights: Provides remedies against unconstitutional State action.
  • Federal balance: Helps resolve disputes concerning the distribution of legislative and executive powers.
  • Rule of law: Prevents arbitrary exercise of public authority.
  • Checks and balances: Restrains unconstitutional actions by the legislature and executive.
  • Limited government: Ensures that constitutional powers are not treated as unlimited.
  • Democratic accountability: Requires public authorities to act within constitutional boundaries.

13. Limitations and Concerns

Although judicial review is essential to constitutional government, its exercise also raises institutional and practical concerns.

13.1 Judicial overreach

Courts may face criticism when their directions appear to enter matters primarily assigned to elected institutions or specialised administrative authorities.

13.2 Separation of powers

Judicial review must respect the constitutional roles of the legislature, executive and judiciary. Courts enforce constitutional boundaries but do not ordinarily govern in place of elected institutions.

13.3 Delay in adjudication

A large number of pending cases can delay constitutional remedies, reducing the practical effectiveness of judicial review.

13.4 Institutional capacity

Some policy questions involve technical, economic or administrative choices. Courts must consider the limits of their institutional competence while reviewing legality and constitutionality.

13.5 Access to justice

Cost, distance, procedural complexity and lack of legal awareness may prevent people from effectively approaching constitutional courts.

14. Important Distinctions for UPSC

Concept Meaning
Judicial review Examination of the constitutional or legal validity of laws and governmental actions.
Judicial activism An interventionist approach to judicial interpretation and remedies.
Judicial restraint A cautious approach that respects institutional boundaries and avoids unnecessary interference.
Basic structure doctrine A limitation on Parliament's power to amend the Constitution under Article 368.
Writ jurisdiction Power of constitutional courts to issue writs for specified constitutional and legal purposes.
Constitutional supremacy The principle that all public authorities derive their powers from and remain subject to the Constitution.

15. Quick Revision

  • Article 13 is a major foundation of judicial review concerning Fundamental Rights.
  • Article 32 empowers the Supreme Court to enforce Fundamental Rights.
  • Article 226 gives High Courts writ jurisdiction for Fundamental Rights and other purposes.
  • Article 227 provides High Courts with supervisory jurisdiction over subordinate courts and tribunals, subject to constitutional exceptions.
  • Kesavananda Bharati (1973) established the basic structure doctrine.
  • Minerva Mills (1980) reaffirmed limited amending power and judicial review.
  • S.R. Bommai (1994) confirmed judicial review of action under Article 356.
  • L. Chandra Kumar (1997) affirmed the basic structure status of judicial review under Articles 32 and 226/227.
  • I.R. Coelho (2007) clarified judicial review of post-Kesavananda Ninth Schedule insertions.

16. Self-Assessment Questions

Question 1

Which constitutional provision expressly deals with laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights?

Answer: Article 13.

Question 2

Which judgment established the basic structure doctrine?

Answer: Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973).

Question 3

Which article gives High Courts writ jurisdiction for purposes beyond the enforcement of Fundamental Rights?

Answer: Article 226.

Question 4

Can a law placed in the Ninth Schedule be reviewed by courts?

Answer: Yes. Under I.R. Coelho, post-24 April 1973 insertions can be reviewed where they damage or destroy the basic structure.

Question 5

Which judgment held that judicial review under Articles 32 and 226/227 is part of the basic structure?

Answer: L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997).
