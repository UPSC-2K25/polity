Tribunals in India

Tribunals are specialised adjudicatory institutions established to decide particular categories of disputes. They supplement the ordinary court system by providing specialised expertise and procedures in areas such as administrative service matters, taxation, company law, environmental protection and public utility regulation.

1. Meaning and Concept of Tribunals

A tribunal is a statutory or constitutionally recognised adjudicatory body established to decide disputes within a specified area of jurisdiction. It performs judicial or quasi-judicial functions, although its structure and procedures may differ from those of ordinary courts.

Tribunals are generally created by legislation. Their powers, composition, jurisdiction, procedure and appeal mechanisms depend on the law under which they are established.

They may include judicial members, technical members or administrative experts, depending on the subject matter they deal with.

Key idea: Tribunals provide specialised adjudication, but they remain subject to the Constitution and judicial review by the constitutional courts.

2. Constitutional Basis: Articles 323A and 323B

The 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976 inserted Part XIV-A into the Constitution, providing for tribunals.

Part XIV-A contains two important provisions:

Article Subject Scope
Article 323A Administrative tribunals Tribunals for adjudication of disputes and complaints relating to recruitment and conditions of service of persons appointed to public services and posts in connection with the affairs of the Union, States, local authorities and other specified authorities.
Article 323B Tribunals for other matters Tribunals for specified subjects such as taxation, foreign exchange, industrial and labour disputes, land reforms, ceiling on urban property, elections to specified legislatures, food-related matters and rent and tenancy issues.

3. Article 323A: Administrative Tribunals

Article 323A empowers Parliament to provide by law for the adjudication of disputes and complaints relating to recruitment and conditions of service of persons appointed to public services and posts connected with the affairs of the Union, States and specified public authorities.

Important features

  • Parliament has the power to establish administrative tribunals under Article 323A.
  • Such tribunals deal with service-related disputes.
  • They may be established for the Union, individual States or for two or more States.
  • Their jurisdiction and procedure are governed by the relevant parliamentary law.

Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT)

The Central Administrative Tribunal was established under the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985, pursuant to Article 323A.

It adjudicates disputes and complaints concerning recruitment and conditions of service of persons covered by the Act, including eligible Central Government employees and other specified categories.

State Administrative Tribunals

The Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 also provides for the establishment of State Administrative Tribunals. Their establishment and functioning are subject to the statutory requirements and the applicable legal framework.

4. Article 323B: Tribunals for Other Matters

Article 323B empowers the appropriate legislature to provide for tribunals dealing with specified categories of disputes. Unlike Article 323A, which concerns administrative service matters and gives the power to Parliament, Article 323B contemplates legislation by the appropriate legislature within its constitutional competence.

Subjects covered under Article 323B

  • Taxation.
  • Foreign exchange, import and export.
  • Industrial and labour disputes.
  • Land reforms and related matters.
  • Ceiling on urban property.
  • Election disputes relating to specified legislatures.
  • Production, procurement, supply and distribution of foodstuffs and other specified essential goods.
  • Rent, its regulation and control, and related tenancy issues.
Difference to remember: Article 323A concerns administrative tribunals for service matters and is a parliamentary power. Article 323B covers specified other subjects and permits legislation by the appropriate legislature.

5. Important Tribunals in India

5.1 Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT)

CAT deals with service-related disputes involving eligible Central Government employees and other persons covered by the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985.

5.2 State Administrative Tribunals (SATs)

State Administrative Tribunals deal with specified service matters of State Government employees where such tribunals have been established under the statutory framework.

5.3 National Green Tribunal (NGT)

The National Green Tribunal was established under the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010. It deals with civil cases involving substantial questions relating to the environment and the implementation of specified environmental laws.

Its work includes environmental protection, conservation of forests and natural resources, and providing relief and compensation for environmental damage within its statutory jurisdiction.

5.4 National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT)

NCLT was constituted under the Companies Act, 2013. It deals with specified company-law matters, including corporate insolvency proceedings under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016.

5.5 National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT)

NCLAT hears appeals against specified orders of NCLT and certain other authorities, as provided by the relevant laws. It also exercises appellate jurisdiction in specified competition-law matters.

5.6 Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT)

ITAT is a specialised appellate tribunal dealing with appeals under the income-tax law. It plays an important role in resolving disputes relating to tax assessments and other matters within its jurisdiction.

5.7 Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT)

CESTAT is a specialised appellate tribunal dealing with specified disputes under customs and indirect-tax laws, including matters assigned to it by legislation.

5.8 Securities Appellate Tribunal (SAT)

SAT hears appeals against orders of specified financial market regulators and authorities, including the Securities and Exchange Board of India, within the scope of the applicable legislation.

5.9 Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal (TDSAT)

TDSAT adjudicates specified disputes in the telecommunications sector and exercises appellate functions under the relevant statutory framework.

5.10 Armed Forces Tribunal (AFT)

The Armed Forces Tribunal was established under the Armed Forces Tribunal Act, 2007. It adjudicates service matters and appeals arising from court-martial proceedings within its statutory jurisdiction.

5.11 Debt Recovery Tribunals (DRTs)

Debt Recovery Tribunals were established under the Recovery of Debts and Bankruptcy Act, 1993. They adjudicate specified claims by banks and financial institutions for recovery of debts.

5.12 Appellate Tribunal for Electricity (APTEL)

APTEL hears appeals against orders of specified electricity regulatory commissions and exercises jurisdiction under the Electricity Act, 2003.

6. Tribunals and Ordinary Courts: Differences

Basis Ordinary Courts Tribunals
Establishment Constitution or laws establishing the regular judicial system. Generally established under specific statutes or constitutional provisions.
Jurisdiction Broad civil, criminal and constitutional jurisdiction according to law. Specialised jurisdiction limited by the relevant law.
Expertise Primarily judicial expertise. May include judicial, technical and subject-specific expertise.
Procedure Generally governed by established procedural laws. Often follows specialised statutory procedures, subject to legal safeguards.
Purpose General administration of justice. Specialised and often more focused dispute resolution.
Judicial review Constitutional courts exercise judicial review. Tribunal decisions remain subject to constitutional judicial review, as applicable.

7. Judicial Review of Tribunal Decisions

Tribunals are not substitutes for the constitutional authority of the Supreme Court and High Courts. Their decisions remain subject to judicial review within the constitutional framework.

L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997)

This is a landmark judgment on the constitutional position of tribunals.

The Supreme Court held that the power of judicial review exercised by High Courts under Articles 226 and 227 and by the Supreme Court under Article 32 forms part of the basic structure of the Constitution.

The Court held that decisions of tribunals are subject to scrutiny by a Division Bench of the High Court within whose territorial jurisdiction the tribunal falls.

Tribunals may perform a supplementary role in the justice delivery system, but they cannot completely replace the constitutional jurisdiction of High Courts and the Supreme Court.

UPSC takeaway: Judicial review by High Courts and the Supreme Court cannot be excluded by an ordinary legislative provision. It is part of the basic structure.

8. Important Judicial Decisions on Tribunals

8.1 S.P. Sampath Kumar v. Union of India (1987)

The Supreme Court considered the constitutional validity of the administrative tribunal framework. It recognised that tribunals could function as effective alternatives to courts if they provided an adequate and effective mechanism for adjudication.

8.2 L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997)

The Court held that judicial review under Articles 226, 227 and 32 is part of the basic structure and cannot be excluded by tribunal legislation.

8.3 Union of India v. R. Gandhi (2010)

The Supreme Court examined the constitution and functioning of the National Company Law Tribunal and National Company Law Appellate Tribunal. It emphasised the importance of preserving judicial independence and appropriate judicial qualifications in tribunal structures.

8.4 Madras Bar Association cases

In a series of decisions, the Supreme Court examined the composition, appointments, tenure and administration of tribunals. These decisions emphasised that tribunal structures must preserve judicial independence and comply with constitutional requirements.

9. Tribunal Reforms

Tribunal reforms have sought to improve efficiency, rationalise the number of tribunals and address concerns relating to appointments, tenure, service conditions and administration.

Tribunal Reforms Act, 2021

The Tribunal Reforms Act, 2021 introduced changes relating to the abolition of certain appellate bodies and the transfer of their functions to other judicial or administrative authorities.

Its provisions concerning tribunal appointments and service conditions have also been examined by the Supreme Court in the context of judicial independence.

In Madras Bar Association v. Union of India (2021), the Supreme Court considered provisions of the 2021 Act and addressed constitutional concerns relating to tribunal independence, including tenure and eligibility.

10. Advantages of Tribunals

  • Specialisation: Members may possess expertise in technical or specialised subject areas.
  • Focused jurisdiction: Tribunals concentrate on defined categories of disputes.
  • Procedural flexibility: Specialised procedures may help simplify adjudication.
  • Reduced burden on courts: They can help distribute the workload of ordinary courts.
  • Expert decision-making: Technical knowledge can assist in resolving complex disputes.
  • Accessibility: Specialised forums may provide a more direct route for particular categories of litigants.

11. Problems and Challenges

11.1 Vacancies and delays

Vacancies in tribunal posts and delays in appointments can prevent tribunals from delivering timely justice.

11.2 Executive influence

Excessive executive control over appointments, tenure, administration or infrastructure may undermine the independence of tribunals.

11.3 Lack of infrastructure

Inadequate facilities, staffing and technological support can affect the quality and speed of adjudication.

11.4 Overlapping jurisdiction

Unclear boundaries between tribunals and ordinary courts may create confusion and additional litigation.

11.5 Inconsistent standards

Differences in the composition, qualifications and service conditions of tribunal members can affect institutional consistency.

11.6 Limited public awareness

Citizens may not know which tribunal has jurisdiction over a particular dispute or how to approach it.

11.7 Dependence on parent ministries

Administrative dependence on ministries whose decisions may be challenged before tribunals can create concerns about impartiality and institutional autonomy.

12. Measures to Strengthen Tribunals

  • Ensure timely and transparent appointments of members.
  • Provide adequate infrastructure, staffing and financial resources.
  • Protect tribunal independence in appointments, tenure and administration.
  • Maintain appropriate representation of judicial and technical expertise.
  • Clearly define jurisdiction and appeal procedures.
  • Use technology to improve case management and access to decisions.
  • Provide regular training and capacity-building for members and staff.
  • Ensure effective judicial review by constitutional courts.
  • Rationalise tribunal structures without weakening access to justice.
