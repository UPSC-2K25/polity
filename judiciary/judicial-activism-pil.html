Judicial Activism and Public Interest Litigation

Judicial activism and Public Interest Litigation have played an important role in expanding access to justice in India. They have enabled constitutional courts to address violations of rights, particularly where affected people are unable to approach courts themselves. At the same time, they raise important questions about judicial restraint, separation of powers and institutional accountability.

1. Meaning of Judicial Activism

Judicial activism refers to an approach to judicial decision-making in which courts interpret constitutional and legal provisions dynamically and may adopt proactive remedies to protect rights, uphold constitutional values and address failures in the implementation of law.

It is not a separate power expressly granted by the Constitution. Rather, it describes the manner in which courts exercise their existing constitutional and statutory jurisdiction.

In India, judicial activism has developed through the interpretation of Fundamental Rights, expansion of access to constitutional remedies, Public Interest Litigation and judicial directions in cases involving serious public concerns.

Core idea: Judicial activism is an approach to adjudication, not an independent constitutional institution or an unlimited power to govern.

2. Meaning of Public Interest Litigation (PIL)

Public Interest Litigation is a legal proceeding initiated before a court to protect a public interest or address a matter affecting a group of people, particularly those who are disadvantaged or unable to approach the court themselves.

Under the traditional rule of locus standi, a person generally had to demonstrate that their own legal right or interest had been affected before approaching a court. PIL relaxed this requirement in appropriate cases involving public injury, exploitation or violation of the rights of disadvantaged sections.

A public-spirited individual or organisation may approach the Supreme Court or a High Court in an appropriate case, even when the petitioner is not personally affected, provided the proceeding is genuinely intended to protect public interest.

Constitutional basis

  • Article 32: Enables the Supreme Court to enforce Fundamental Rights.
  • Article 226: Enables High Courts to issue writs for Fundamental Rights and other legal purposes.
  • Article 39A: Directs the State to ensure equal justice and provide free legal aid so that opportunities for securing justice are not denied because of economic or other disabilities.
Important: PIL is a judicially developed procedural mechanism. It is not a separate writ or an independently enumerated Fundamental Right.

3. Evolution of PIL in India

The development of PIL in India is closely connected with efforts to make constitutional justice accessible to people who were economically, socially or otherwise disadvantaged.

Early phase

During the late 1970s and 1980s, the Supreme Court began relaxing procedural requirements in cases involving prisoners, bonded labourers, undertrial detainees and other vulnerable groups.

Role of judicial interpretation

Courts expanded the practical meaning of access to justice and interpreted Article 21 in a manner that recognised several protections necessary for a dignified life.

Expansion of subject areas

PIL later extended to environmental protection, public health, prison conditions, corruption, governance, human rights and protection of public resources.

Phase Broad development
Late 1970s Relaxation of standing rules and greater access to constitutional courts.
1980s Focus on prisoners, bonded labourers, undertrial detainees and disadvantaged communities.
1990s Expansion into environmental protection, public accountability and governance.
2000s onwards Greater scrutiny of public institutions and continued emphasis on preventing misuse of PIL.

4. Important Pioneers and Judicial Contributions

The growth of PIL in India is associated with judges who helped develop a more accessible and socially responsive approach to constitutional adjudication.

Justice P.N. Bhagwati

Justice P.N. Bhagwati played a major role in developing PIL and relaxing traditional rules of locus standi. He supported a more accessible judicial process for disadvantaged groups and contributed to the development of epistolary jurisdiction, under which letters and other communications could, in appropriate cases, be treated as petitions.

Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer

Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer contributed significantly to a socially responsive interpretation of law, especially in matters concerning prisoners, access to justice and the rights of vulnerable people.

Justice Y.V. Chandrachud and other judges

The development of PIL was a collective judicial process involving several judges and benches. Decisions across different areas of constitutional law helped establish its principles and practical scope.

5. Important Judgments on PIL and Judicial Activism

Case Year Significance
Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar 1979 Highlighted the plight of undertrial prisoners and recognised speedy trial as an essential aspect of Article 21.
S.P. Gupta v. Union of India 1981 Significantly liberalised locus standi in public interest matters.
People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India 1982 Addressed labour exploitation and the enforcement of rights under Articles 21, 23 and related provisions.
Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India 1984 Addressed bonded labour and the responsibility of the State to protect vulnerable workers.
Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation 1985 Recognised the relationship between livelihood and the right to life under Article 21.
M.C. Mehta v. Union of India 1980s onwards Generated important principles in environmental protection, including absolute liability in the Oleum Gas Leak case.
Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan 1997 Laid down guidelines against sexual harassment at the workplace in the absence of specific legislation at the time.
Vineet Narain v. Union of India 1997 Issued directions concerning the functioning and independence of investigative agencies.
State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal 2010 Emphasised safeguards against frivolous and motivated PILs.

6. Epistolary Jurisdiction

Epistolary jurisdiction refers to the practice of treating letters, postcards or other informal communications as petitions in appropriate cases involving serious violations of rights or public interest.

It developed as part of the effort to reduce procedural barriers for people who lacked the resources, education or ability to approach constitutional courts through conventional legal procedures.

This approach helped courts respond to reports of bonded labour, prison abuse, custodial conditions and other serious human rights concerns.

Distinction: Epistolary jurisdiction concerns the manner in which a court may receive and treat a communication. PIL is the broader mechanism through which public-interest issues are brought before the court.

7. Areas in Which PIL Has Been Used

7.1 Human rights and social justice

PIL has been used to address bonded labour, trafficking, prison conditions, custodial violence, child labour and the treatment of vulnerable communities.

7.2 Environmental protection

Environmental PILs have addressed air and water pollution, industrial hazards, forest conservation, waste management and protection of ecological resources.

7.3 Public health

Courts have considered matters involving access to essential healthcare, public health risks, sanitation and the duties of public authorities.

7.4 Governance and accountability

PIL has been used to examine allegations involving corruption, public administration, transparency and failures in the performance of statutory duties.

7.5 Electoral and constitutional issues

Public-interest proceedings may also raise questions concerning electoral fairness, constitutional obligations and the protection of democratic institutions, subject to applicable rules of maintainability and jurisdiction.

8. Judicial Activism: Major Contributions

  • Expansion of Article 21: Judicial interpretation has recognised several rights and protections connected with life and personal liberty.
  • Access to justice: Relaxation of procedural barriers has enabled disadvantaged people to seek judicial remedies.
  • Environmental protection: Courts have developed and applied important environmental principles.
  • Human rights protection: Judicial intervention has addressed exploitation, custodial abuse and other rights violations.
  • Administrative accountability: Courts have required public authorities to comply with constitutional and statutory duties.
  • Protection of constitutional values: Judicial decisions have reinforced equality, dignity, liberty and the rule of law.

9. Criticism of Judicial Activism

9.1 Judicial overreach

Judicial activism may become controversial when courts appear to enter policy areas that are primarily within the responsibility of elected governments or specialised administrative bodies.

9.2 Separation of powers

Excessive intervention may blur the boundaries between the legislature, executive and judiciary. Constitutional governance requires each institution to perform its assigned functions.

9.3 Democratic accountability

Judges are not directly elected. Critics argue that courts should exercise caution when making broad policy choices that ordinarily require democratic deliberation and political accountability.

9.4 Administrative difficulties

Courts may lack the institutional capacity, technical expertise or resources needed to supervise complex public programmes over extended periods.

9.5 Inconsistent outcomes

Broad judicial directions may sometimes create uncertainty if implementation responsibilities, measurable standards and institutional limits are not clearly defined.

10. Misuse of Public Interest Litigation

The relaxation of locus standi has made justice more accessible, but it has also created the possibility of misuse. Some petitions presented as public-interest matters may actually be motivated by private disputes, political rivalry, publicity or commercial interests.

Common forms of misuse

  • Personal or private disputes disguised as public-interest cases.
  • Petitions filed for political motives or publicity.
  • Attempts to settle commercial or property disputes through PIL.
  • Petitions based on unverified or misleading allegations.
  • Proceedings intended to harass public officials or private parties.

Safeguards developed by courts

In State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010), the Supreme Court emphasised the need to verify the credentials of petitioners, examine the correctness of the information presented and ensure that the proceeding is genuinely in public interest.

Courts may dismiss frivolous petitions and impose costs where appropriate to discourage abuse of judicial process.

11. Judicial Activism, Judicial Restraint and Judicial Overreach

Concept Meaning Institutional concern
Judicial activism Proactive interpretation and remedies to protect rights and constitutional values. Can improve access to justice, but requires respect for constitutional limits.
Judicial restraint A cautious approach that avoids unnecessary interference with decisions of other branches. Protects institutional boundaries but should not prevent enforcement of constitutional rights.
Judicial overreach Intervention that exceeds the proper constitutional or institutional role of the judiciary. May undermine separation of powers and democratic accountability.

The objective is not to eliminate judicial activism or judicial restraint. It is to maintain a constitutional balance in which courts protect rights and enforce the law without assuming the ordinary functions of elected institutions.

12. Way Forward

  • Maintain a balance between judicial intervention and institutional restraint.
  • Strengthen legal aid and access to justice for disadvantaged groups.
  • Apply clear screening standards to prevent misuse of PIL.
  • Ensure that judicial directions are legally grounded and capable of implementation.
  • Improve coordination between courts, governments and statutory authorities.
  • Strengthen accountability and transparency in public institutions.
  • Reduce judicial delays through better case management and procedural reforms.

13. Important Distinctions for UPSC

Basis Judicial Activism Public Interest Litigation
Nature An approach to judicial interpretation and decision-making. A procedural mechanism for bringing public-interest issues before courts.
Purpose Protect constitutional values and provide effective remedies. Facilitate access to justice in matters affecting public interest.
Initiation May arise in ordinary litigation, constitutional cases or PIL. Usually initiated by a public-spirited petitioner or organisation in an appropriate case.
Constitutional basis Exercised through existing judicial powers and jurisdiction. Primarily operates through Articles 32 and 226, among other applicable jurisdictions.

14. Quick Revision

  • PIL relaxes traditional rules of locus standi in appropriate public-interest cases.
  • Articles 32 and 226 are important constitutional routes for PIL.
  • Article 39A supports equal access to justice and free legal aid.
  • Justice P.N. Bhagwati and Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer made major contributions to the development of PIL.
  • Hussainara Khatoon (1979) highlighted speedy trial and undertrial prisoners' rights.
  • S.P. Gupta (1981) significantly liberalised locus standi.
  • Vishaka (1997) laid down workplace sexual harassment guidelines.
  • Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010) emphasised safeguards against misuse of PIL.
  • Judicial activism must operate within the framework of separation of powers.

15. Self-Assessment Questions

Question 1

What is the main purpose of Public Interest Litigation?

Answer: To facilitate access to justice in matters involving public interest, particularly for disadvantaged or affected groups who may be unable to approach courts themselves.

Question 2

Which constitutional provisions are most directly associated with PIL before the Supreme Court and High Courts?

Answer: Articles 32 and 226.

Question 3

Which case is associated with the liberalisation of locus standi?

Answer: S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981).

Question 4

Which case laid down guidelines against sexual harassment at the workplace in 1997?

Answer: Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan.

Question 5

What is the difference between judicial activism and judicial overreach?

Answer: Judicial activism is proactive judicial interpretation within constitutional limits. Judicial overreach refers to intervention that exceeds the proper institutional or constitutional role of the judiciary.
