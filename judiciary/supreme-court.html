UNION JUDICIARY · ARTICLES 124–147

Supreme Court of India The apex judicial institution and the final interpreter of the Constitution.

The Supreme Court of India is the highest court in the country's integrated judicial system. It stands at the apex of the judicial hierarchy and exercises constitutional, appellate, original and advisory jurisdiction.

The Court protects fundamental rights, interprets the Constitution, resolves specified federal disputes and ensures that legislative and executive authorities act within constitutional limits. Its decisions establish binding legal principles for courts throughout India.

CONSTITUTIONAL POSITION

Articles 124–147: The Union Judiciary

Part V, Chapter IV of the Constitution deals with the Union Judiciary. Article 124 provides for the establishment and constitution of the Supreme Court. The succeeding Articles deal with the appointment and conditions of service of judges, jurisdiction, powers, procedure and other matters concerning the Court.

The Supreme Court is not merely the highest appellate court. It is also a constitutional court. Its role includes interpreting the Constitution, enforcing fundamental rights and maintaining the constitutional distribution of powers.

01 · FOUNDATION

Establishment and constitutional background

The Supreme Court of India was inaugurated on 28 January 1950, two days after the Constitution came into force. It replaced the Federal Court of India, which had been established under the Government of India Act, 1935.

Before independence, the Federal Court exercised limited federal and appellate jurisdiction. The Privy Council in London continued to be the highest appellate authority in many matters. With the commencement of the Constitution, the Supreme Court became the highest judicial authority in independent India.

Why was a Supreme Court necessary?

A constitutional democracy requires an institution capable of interpreting the Constitution and resolving disputes arising from its application. The Supreme Court performs this role by:

  • Interpreting the provisions of the Constitution.
  • Protecting fundamental rights through constitutional remedies.
  • Resolving specified disputes between the Union and States.
  • Hearing appeals from High Courts and other courts in constitutionally permitted cases.
  • Ensuring consistency in the interpretation and application of law.
  • Maintaining the supremacy of the Constitution.
Important distinction

The Supreme Court is the final judicial authority in India, but it is not a branch of the executive or legislature. It exercises judicial power independently within the constitutional framework.

02 · ARTICLE 124

Composition of the Supreme Court

Article 124 provides for a Supreme Court consisting of the Chief Justice of India and other Judges. The Constitution originally contemplated a smaller Court, but Parliament has increased the sanctioned strength through legislation over time.

Chief Justice of India

The Chief Justice of India is the head of the Supreme Court and the highest judicial office in the country. The Chief Justice performs administrative responsibilities and participates in judicial proceedings as a judge.

The Chief Justice also has an important role in constituting benches and allocating judicial work. These administrative responsibilities are exercised within the institutional rules and established judicial procedures.

Other judges

The other judges assist the Court in discharging its judicial responsibilities. Cases may be heard by benches of different strengths depending on the nature and importance of the matter.

A bench may consist of two or three judges in ordinary matters. Constitutionally important questions may require a larger bench.

Constitution Bench

Article 145(3) provides that a minimum of five judges must hear a case involving a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution. Such a bench is commonly called a Constitution Bench.

Constitution Benches are particularly important because their decisions may settle major constitutional questions and establish principles that guide future cases.

Remember

A Constitution Bench must consist of at least five judges when Article 145(3) applies. The Constitution does not prescribe that every constitutional matter must automatically be heard by five judges.

03 · ARTICLES 124 AND 127

Appointment of Supreme Court judges

Under Article 124, judges of the Supreme Court are formally appointed by the President of India by warrant under the President's hand and seal. The constitutional text provides for consultation with judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts as specified in the Article.

The present appointment process has developed through judicial interpretation. The collegium system is the result of important Supreme Court decisions concerning the meaning of consultation and the independence of the judiciary.

The collegium system

The collegium system is the mechanism through which the higher judiciary recommends appointments and transfers of judges. It is not expressly described by the word "collegium" in the original constitutional text.

For appointments to the Supreme Court, the collegium is headed by the Chief Justice of India and includes the senior-most judges of the Supreme Court in accordance with the principles laid down by judicial decisions.

Evolution of the collegium system

Case Year Constitutional significance
First Judges Case 1981 Initially gave primacy to the executive in the appointment process.
Second Judges Case 1993 Established the collegium system and gave primacy to the judiciary in appointments.
Third Judges Case 1998 Clarified the composition and consultation process of the collegium.
Fourth Judges Case 2015 Invalidated the 99th Constitutional Amendment and the NJAC Act, restoring the collegium framework.

National Judicial Appointments Commission

The Constitution (Ninety-ninth Amendment) Act, 2014 sought to introduce the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) in place of the collegium system. Parliament also enacted the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, 2014.

In 2015, the Supreme Court, in the Fourth Judges Case, declared the 99th Constitutional Amendment and the NJAC Act unconstitutional. The Court held that the proposed arrangement threatened the independence of the judiciary, which forms part of the basic structure of the Constitution.

UPSC distinction

The President formally appoints Supreme Court judges. The collegium recommends names under the judicially evolved appointment process. These are two different stages of the same constitutional appointment framework.

04 · ARTICLE 124(3)

Qualifications of Supreme Court judges

Article 124(3) lays down the qualifications required for appointment as a judge of the Supreme Court. A person must be a citizen of India and must satisfy at least one of the following conditions:

  1. The person must have been a judge of a High Court, or of two or more such Courts in succession, for at least five years.
  2. The person must have been an advocate of a High Court, or of two or more such Courts in succession, for at least ten years.
  3. The person must, in the opinion of the President, be a distinguished jurist.

Important points

  • Citizenship of India is essential.
  • The Constitution does not prescribe a minimum age for appointment as a Supreme Court judge.
  • The qualification of a distinguished jurist is expressly recognised by the Constitution.
  • The person must satisfy at least one of the specified professional or juristic qualifications.
Common examination trap

The Constitution does not require every Supreme Court judge to have served as a High Court judge. The qualification of an advocate of a High Court and that of a distinguished jurist are also recognised.

05 · ARTICLES 124 AND 125

Tenure, resignation and removal of judges

Retirement age

A judge of the Supreme Court holds office until attaining the age of 65 years. Unlike the President or members of the Lok Sabha, a Supreme Court judge does not hold office for a fixed number of years.

Resignation

A judge may resign by writing under the judge's hand addressed to the President of India.

Removal of a judge

Article 124(4) provides that a Supreme Court judge can be removed only by an order of the President after an address by each House of Parliament, supported by the prescribed special majority, has been presented to the President in the same session.

Removal is permitted only on the grounds of proved misbehaviour or incapacity.

Parliamentary majority required

The address must be supported by:

  • A majority of the total membership of that House; and
  • A majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting.

Both Houses must pass the address in the same session. The President then issues the removal order in accordance with Article 124(4).

Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968

The Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968 provides the statutory procedure relating to investigation of allegations of misbehaviour or incapacity. It regulates the process through which the charges are examined before Parliament considers the removal address.

Important terminology

The Constitution uses the expression "removal of a judge". The term "impeachment" is specifically used for the removal of the President under Article 61. In ordinary discussion, the removal of judges is often loosely called impeachment, but the constitutional procedures are distinct.

06 · JUDICIAL POWERS

Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court

Jurisdiction refers to the legal authority of a court to hear and decide particular types of cases. The Supreme Court exercises several forms of jurisdiction under the Constitution.

A. Original jurisdiction — Article 131

Original jurisdiction means that a case can be instituted directly before the Supreme Court without first being decided by a lower court.

Article 131 gives the Supreme Court exclusive original jurisdiction in specified disputes involving the Union and States or disputes between States.

The disputes covered include:

  • A dispute between the Government of India and one or more States.
  • A dispute between the Government of India and one or more States on one side and one or more other States on the other side.
  • A dispute between two or more States.

The dispute must involve a question, whether of law or fact, on which the existence or extent of a legal right depends.

This jurisdiction is an important instrument for resolving federal disputes within the constitutional framework.

B. Writ jurisdiction — Article 32

Article 32 guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of fundamental rights. The Court may issue directions, orders or writs for this purpose.

The five traditional writs are:

Writ Meaning and purpose
Habeas Corpus Protects a person against unlawful detention and requires the detained person to be produced before the court.
Mandamus Commands a public authority to perform a public or legal duty that it has failed or refused to perform.
Prohibition Prevents a lower court or tribunal from continuing proceedings beyond its jurisdiction.
Certiorari Quashes an order of a lower court or tribunal where the legal conditions for judicial interference are satisfied.
Quo Warranto Questions the legal authority by which a person holds a public office.

C. Appellate jurisdiction — Articles 132–134

The Supreme Court hears appeals from High Courts in specified constitutional, civil and criminal matters. The Constitution provides different routes for these appeals.

  • Article 132: Appeals involving a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution.
  • Article 133: Appeals in civil proceedings where the constitutional conditions are satisfied.
  • Article 134: Appeals in criminal proceedings in constitutionally specified circumstances.
  • Article 134A: Certificate for appeal to the Supreme Court.

D. Special Leave Petition — Article 136

Article 136 gives the Supreme Court discretionary power to grant special leave to appeal from a judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order passed by a court or tribunal in India, subject to the constitutional exclusion relating to courts or tribunals constituted under laws relating to the Armed Forces.

A Special Leave Petition (SLP) is not an appeal as of right. A litigant must seek the Court's permission, and the Court decides whether special leave should be granted.

This power gives the Supreme Court considerable discretion to intervene in appropriate cases, particularly where substantial injustice or serious legal error is involved.

E. Advisory jurisdiction — Article 143

Article 143 empowers the President to refer certain questions of law or fact of public importance to the Supreme Court for its opinion.

The Court may report its opinion after such hearing as it considers appropriate. Under Article 143(1), the Court may decline to give an opinion. A reference under Article 143(2) concerns specified disputes arising out of pre-Constitution treaties, agreements and similar instruments, and the Court is required to report its opinion.

The advisory opinion is not equivalent to an ordinary binding judgment in an adversarial dispute. Its constitutional significance lies in providing judicial guidance to the President on important questions.

F. Review jurisdiction — Article 137

Article 137 empowers the Supreme Court to review its own judgments or orders, subject to the provisions of any law made by Parliament and the rules made under Article 145.

Review is not an ordinary appeal or a general opportunity to reargue a case. It is an exceptional remedy available on limited grounds recognised by law.

G. Curative jurisdiction

The Supreme Court has recognised a limited curative jurisdiction to prevent a grave miscarriage of justice in exceptional cases even after dismissal of a review petition.

This principle was developed in Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002). Curative petitions are extraordinary and are subject to strict procedural conditions.

07 · CONSTITUTIONAL AUTHORITY

Other important powers of the Supreme Court

Article 129: Court of record

Article 129 declares the Supreme Court to be a Court of Record. Its records have evidentiary value and cannot be questioned merely on the ground that they are not formally proved.

As a Court of Record, the Supreme Court also has the power to punish for contempt of itself. This power protects the authority and proper functioning of the judicial institution.

Article 141: Law declared by the Supreme Court

Article 141 provides that the law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all courts within the territory of India.

This provision ensures consistency in the interpretation of law. The legal principles established by the Supreme Court guide subordinate courts and High Courts.

Article 142: Complete justice

Article 142 empowers the Supreme Court to pass such decree or make such order as is necessary for doing complete justice in any cause or matter pending before it.

This power enables the Court to mould relief in exceptional situations where ordinary legal remedies may not adequately address the demands of justice.

However, Article 142 is not an unlimited authority to disregard substantive law. It operates within the constitutional framework and has been interpreted through judicial decisions.

Article 144: Authorities to act in aid of the Supreme Court

Article 144 requires all civil and judicial authorities in the territory of India to act in aid of the Supreme Court.

This provision supports the effective implementation of the Court's orders and judgments.

Article 145: Rules of Court

Article 145 empowers the Supreme Court, subject to the Constitution and laws made by Parliament, to make rules regulating its practice and procedure.

These rules cover matters such as filing, listing, hearings, appeals and other procedural aspects of the Court's work.

Article 146: Officers and servants

Article 146 deals with the appointment and conditions of service of officers and servants of the Supreme Court. It provides an institutional framework for the Court's administrative establishment.

08 · CONSTITUTIONAL SUPREMACY

Supreme Court and judicial review

Judicial review is the power of courts to examine the constitutional validity of legislative enactments and executive actions. Where a law or action violates the Constitution, the Court may declare it invalid to the extent of the inconsistency.

The Supreme Court plays a central role in maintaining constitutional supremacy. It examines whether public authorities have acted within the powers granted to them by the Constitution.

Constitutional provisions supporting judicial review

  • Article 13: Laws inconsistent with or in derogation of fundamental rights.
  • Article 32: Constitutional remedies for enforcement of fundamental rights.
  • Article 131: Specified federal disputes.
  • Article 136: Special leave to appeal.
  • Article 141: Binding force of law declared by the Supreme Court.
  • Article 226: Writ jurisdiction of High Courts.
  • Article 368: Constitutional amendment procedure, subject to constitutional limitations.

Basic structure doctrine

In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), the Supreme Court held that Parliament's power to amend the Constitution under Article 368 does not extend to destroying or damaging its basic structure.

The basic structure doctrine places a substantive constitutional limitation on the amending power. It preserves essential features of the Constitution, including constitutional supremacy, democracy, secularism, federalism and judicial review.

The exact content of the basic structure is not contained in a single exhaustive list. It has developed through judicial interpretation in subsequent cases.

09 · INSTITUTIONAL SAFEGUARDS

Independence of the Supreme Court

Judicial independence means that judges must be able to decide cases impartially and according to law, without improper pressure or interference.

Independence is essential because courts are expected to review government action, protect constitutional rights and resolve disputes involving powerful institutions.

Constitutional safeguards

  • Security of tenure: Judges cannot be removed by the executive at its discretion. Removal requires the special constitutional procedure.
  • Protected salaries and allowances: Article 125 provides for the salaries and related conditions of service of Supreme Court judges, subject to constitutional provisions.
  • Consolidated Fund: Specified expenditure relating to the Supreme Court is charged on the Consolidated Fund of India.
  • Restrictions after retirement: Article 124(7) prohibits a retired Supreme Court judge from pleading or acting in any court or before any authority within India.
  • Judicial powers: Courts exercise their constitutional jurisdiction independently of the executive and legislature.
  • Separation of judiciary: Article 50 directs the State to take steps to separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State.
Independence and accountability

Judicial independence does not mean that judges are above the law or free from accountability. It protects impartial adjudication while the Constitution provides mechanisms for judicial discipline and removal.

10 · IMPORTANT JUDGMENTS

Landmark Supreme Court judgments

Important judgments explain how constitutional provisions have been interpreted and how the role of the Supreme Court has developed. The following cases are especially relevant to the study of the Union Judiciary.

Case Year Core principle
Shankari Prasad v. Union of India 1951 Held that a constitutional amendment was not "law" within the meaning of Article 13, in the context of fundamental rights.
Golaknath v. State of Punjab 1967 Held that Parliament could not amend fundamental rights; this position was later reconsidered in Kesavananda Bharati.
Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala 1973 Established the basic structure limitation on Parliament's amending power.
Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India 1978 Expanded the interpretation of Article 21 and emphasised fairness and reasonableness in procedure.
Minerva Mills v. Union of India 1980 Reaffirmed limits on the amending power and the importance of harmony between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.
Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India 1993 Established the collegium system in the Second Judges Case.
In re Presidential Reference 1998 Clarified the consultation process and collegium composition for judicial appointments.
I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu 2007 Held that laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 could be tested against the basic structure through their impact on fundamental rights.
Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India 2015 Invalidated the NJAC framework and reaffirmed judicial independence as part of the basic structure.

12 · QUICK REVISION

What you should remember

  1. The Supreme Court is the apex court of India's integrated judicial system.
  2. Articles 124–147 in Part V, Chapter IV deal with the Union Judiciary.
  3. The President formally appoints judges under Article 124, following the constitutionally evolved appointment process.
  4. The retirement age of a Supreme Court judge is 65 years.
  5. Article 131 provides exclusive original jurisdiction in specified federal disputes.
  6. Article 32 provides the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of fundamental rights.
  7. Article 136 provides discretionary special leave to appeal.
  8. Article 141 makes the law declared by the Supreme Court binding on all courts.
  9. Article 142 empowers the Court to do complete justice in matters pending before it.
  10. Article 145(3) requires a minimum five-judge bench for cases involving a substantial question of law as to constitutional interpretation.

Check your understanding

Question 1. Which Article provides the Supreme Court with exclusive original jurisdiction in specified disputes between the Union and States?

Answer: Article 131.

Question 2. What is the retirement age of a Supreme Court judge?

Answer: 65 years.

Question 3. Which Article makes the law declared by the Supreme Court binding on all courts?

Answer: Article 141.

Question 4. Is a Special Leave Petition under Article 136 an appeal as of right?

Answer: No. It is a discretionary power of the Supreme Court.
