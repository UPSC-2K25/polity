STATE JUDICIARY · ARTICLES 233–237

Subordinate Judiciary The foundation of the judicial system and the first level of ordinary justice delivery.

The subordinate judiciary consists of district courts and other courts below the High Courts. These courts handle a large share of civil and criminal disputes and provide the ordinary institutional route through which people access the justice system.

The Constitution provides for the appointment of district judges, recruitment to the judicial service and the control of High Courts over subordinate courts. These arrangements are designed to preserve judicial independence at the district and local levels.

CONSTITUTIONAL FRAMEWORK

Articles 233–237: Subordinate Courts

Part VI, Chapter VI of the Constitution deals with subordinate courts. It covers the appointment of district judges, recruitment to the judicial service, control of subordinate courts by High Courts and the application of these provisions to certain classes of magistrates.

The subordinate judiciary operates under the constitutional supervision of the High Court, while its structure, jurisdiction and procedures are also governed by applicable central and State laws.

01 · FOUNDATION

Meaning and constitutional position

The term subordinate judiciary refers to the courts functioning below the High Court in the judicial hierarchy. These courts are often called district and lower courts, although their exact names, structure and jurisdiction vary across States.

They perform the day-to-day work of the judicial system. Civil disputes, criminal trials, family matters, land-related cases and many other proceedings are handled at this level.

Why is the subordinate judiciary important?

  • It brings the justice system closer to people at the district and local levels.
  • It handles the bulk of ordinary civil and criminal litigation.
  • It establishes the factual record in many cases that may later reach appellate courts.
  • It provides the first formal judicial forum for a large number of disputes.
  • It supports the rule of law through the regular application of statutes and legal principles.
Important distinction

The subordinate judiciary is subordinate to the High Court in the judicial hierarchy and is subject to its constitutional control. It is not subordinate to the State executive in the performance of judicial functions.

02 · COURT STRUCTURE

Hierarchy of subordinate courts

The judicial hierarchy below the High Court is not identical in every State. It is shaped by the applicable civil and criminal laws, local administrative arrangements and the distribution of judicial work.

General judicial hierarchy

Level Typical court or institution General role
State level High Court Highest court within the State's judicial hierarchy; exercises appellate, writ and supervisory jurisdiction.
District level District and Sessions Court Principal civil court of original jurisdiction in the district and principal criminal court of sessions jurisdiction.
Below district level Senior and junior civil courts Handle civil cases according to their territorial and pecuniary jurisdiction.
Criminal trial level Judicial Magistrates and other criminal courts Conduct criminal proceedings and trials within the powers conferred by criminal procedure law.
Specialised level Family Courts and other statutory courts Handle specialised categories of disputes under the laws establishing them.

District and Sessions Court

The District Court is generally the principal civil court of original jurisdiction in a district, subject to statutory arrangements and the jurisdiction of other courts.

The same judicial officer may also function as the Sessions Judge for criminal matters. In that capacity, the officer exercises criminal jurisdiction under applicable criminal procedure law.

Civil courts

Civil courts adjudicate disputes involving private rights and obligations. These may concern property, contracts, inheritance, compensation, injunctions and other civil matters.

Their jurisdiction is determined by laws governing civil procedure, court fees, pecuniary limits and territorial jurisdiction.

Criminal courts

Criminal courts deal with offences against the law. Their powers and procedures are governed by criminal statutes, including the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, which replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 from 1 July 2024, subject to applicable transitional provisions.

03 · ARTICLE 233

Appointment of district judges

Article 233 deals with the appointment, posting and promotion of district judges in a State.

Appointments of persons to be, and the posting and promotion of, district judges in any State are made by the Governor of the State in consultation with the High Court exercising jurisdiction in relation to that State.

Appointment from the Bar

A person who is not already in the service of the Union or the State is eligible for appointment as a district judge if the person has been an advocate or pleader for at least seven years and is recommended by the High Court for appointment.

Constitutional requirements

  • The Governor makes the appointment.
  • Consultation with the High Court is constitutionally required.
  • A person from the Bar must have at least seven years' experience as an advocate or pleader.
  • The High Court must recommend the person for appointment from the Bar.
UPSC point

Article 233 expressly requires consultation with the High Court for the appointment, posting and promotion of district judges. It does not give the State executive unrestricted power over these judicial appointments.

04 · ARTICLE 234

Recruitment to the judicial service

Article 234 deals with the recruitment of persons other than district judges to the judicial service of a State.

Such appointments are made by the Governor in accordance with rules made by the Governor after consultation with the State Public Service Commission and the High Court exercising jurisdiction in relation to the State.

Role of the State Public Service Commission

The State Public Service Commission participates in the recruitment process in accordance with the applicable rules. The High Court is also consulted because judicial recruitment must protect the independence and professional standards of the judiciary.

Judicial service examinations

States conduct judicial service recruitment examinations under their respective rules. The exact eligibility requirements, examination stages and recruitment arrangements may vary.

The recruitment process commonly includes a preliminary examination, a main written examination and an interview or personality test, depending on the State's rules.

Distinguish Articles 233 and 234

Article 233 concerns district judges. Article 234 concerns recruitment of persons other than district judges to the judicial service of a State.

05 · ARTICLE 235

Control of subordinate courts by High Courts

Article 235 vests control over district courts and courts subordinate to them in the High Court.

This provision is one of the most important constitutional safeguards for the independence of the subordinate judiciary. It places important administrative and disciplinary responsibilities in the hands of the High Court rather than the State executive.

What does control include?

  • Posting of judicial officers.
  • Promotion of judicial officers.
  • Grant of leave.
  • Disciplinary control, subject to applicable service rules and constitutional requirements.
  • Supervision of the functioning of subordinate courts.

The High Court's control is not unlimited. It must be exercised consistently with the Constitution, applicable service rules and the principles of natural justice.

Why is Article 235 important?

If subordinate judges were placed under the direct administrative control of the State executive, there could be a risk of improper influence in judicial decisions. Article 235 helps prevent this by giving the High Court a constitutionally protected role in the administration of the subordinate judiciary.

Important distinction

The Governor appoints district judges under Article 233 in consultation with the High Court. The High Court exercises control over the subordinate judiciary under Article 235. Appointment and control are related but distinct constitutional functions.

06 · ARTICLE 237

Application to certain classes of magistrates

Article 237 empowers the Governor to apply the provisions of the constitutional chapter dealing with subordinate courts to certain classes of magistrates.

This may be done by public notification and subject to such exceptions and modifications as may be specified in the notification.

The provision supports the constitutional objective of bringing the relevant judicial magistracy within the framework of judicial independence and High Court control.

07 · ARTICLE 50

Separation of the judiciary from the executive

Article 50 is a Directive Principle of State Policy. It directs the State to take steps to separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State.

The objective is to ensure that judicial officers can perform their functions independently and impartially, without being subject to executive control in the discharge of judicial duties.

Constitutional significance

  • Strengthens judicial independence at the district level.
  • Reduces the possibility of executive influence over judicial decisions.
  • Supports impartial adjudication and the rule of law.
  • Complements the institutional safeguards in Articles 233–235.
Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles

Article 50 is a Directive Principle, not a Fundamental Right. It nevertheless expresses an important constitutional objective that informs the organisation of the judicial system.

08 · SPECIALISED JUSTICE DELIVERY

Specialised courts and statutory forums

Apart from ordinary civil and criminal courts, the judicial system includes specialised courts and statutory forums created to deal with particular categories of disputes.

Family Courts

Family Courts are established under the Family Courts Act, 1984 to promote conciliation and secure speedy settlement of disputes relating to marriage and family affairs.

Commercial Courts

Commercial Courts are established under the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 to deal with specified commercial disputes. The legislation provides a specialised framework intended to improve the resolution of commercial litigation.

Special courts

Various statutes provide for special courts to try particular offences or categories of cases. Examples include special courts under laws dealing with corruption, offences against children and other specified matters.

The precise legal character of each forum depends on the statute establishing it. Not every specialised forum is necessarily part of the ordinary subordinate court hierarchy.

09 · ADMINISTRATION OF JUSTICE

Challenges faced by the subordinate judiciary

The effectiveness of the judicial system depends heavily on the functioning of district and subordinate courts. Several structural and administrative challenges affect their ability to deliver timely justice.

Major challenges

  • Case pendency: Large numbers of pending cases increase the time required for dispute resolution.
  • Vacancies: Vacant judicial posts place additional pressure on serving judges and court staff.
  • Infrastructure: Some courts face shortages of courtrooms, equipment, staff and other facilities.
  • Access to justice: Distance, cost, language barriers and lack of legal awareness may prevent people from effectively accessing courts.
  • Technology: Uneven digital infrastructure can affect the adoption of e-filing, virtual hearings and electronic case management.
  • Training: Continuous professional development is important as laws, procedures and technology evolve.

Measures to strengthen the system

  • Filling judicial vacancies in a timely manner.
  • Improving court infrastructure and supporting staff.
  • Expanding e-Courts and digital case management.
  • Strengthening legal aid and access to justice.
  • Promoting mediation and other appropriate alternative dispute resolution methods.
  • Providing continuing judicial education and training.

12 · QUICK REVISION

What you should remember

  1. Articles 233–237 deal with subordinate courts.
  2. Article 233 concerns appointment, posting and promotion of district judges.
  3. Article 234 deals with recruitment of persons other than district judges to the judicial service.
  4. Article 235 gives control over subordinate courts to the High Court.
  5. A person appointed from the Bar as a district judge must have at least seven years' experience as an advocate or pleader and must be recommended by the High Court.
  6. Article 237 concerns the application of the chapter to certain classes of magistrates.
  7. Article 50 is a Directive Principle concerning separation of the judiciary from the executive.
