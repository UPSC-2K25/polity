Independence of Judiciary

Independence of the judiciary is an essential feature of constitutional democracy. It ensures that courts can decide disputes impartially, protect Fundamental Rights and review governmental action without improper influence from the legislature, executive or powerful private interests.

1. Meaning of Independence of Judiciary

Independence of the judiciary means that judges must be able to discharge their judicial functions fairly, impartially and without external pressure, interference or improper influence.

It requires judges to decide cases according to the Constitution, laws and evidence, rather than political considerations, personal interests or pressure from powerful individuals and institutions.

Judicial independence does not mean that judges are above the law or free from accountability. It means that accountability must operate through constitutionally established procedures and must not become a means of controlling judicial decisions.

Core principle: An independent judiciary is necessary to uphold constitutional supremacy, protect individual liberty, enforce the rule of law and maintain checks and balances among the organs of government.

2. Constitutional Basis

The Constitution contains several provisions designed to protect the independence of the Supreme Court, High Courts and subordinate judiciary.

Article Provision Importance
Article 50 Separation of judiciary from executive Directs the State to take steps to separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State.
Article 124 Establishment and constitution of Supreme Court Provides the constitutional framework for the Supreme Court, including appointment and conditions of service of judges.
Article 125 Salaries of Supreme Court judges Provides constitutional protection for judicial remuneration.
Article 129 Supreme Court as a Court of Record Recognises its status and power to punish for contempt of itself.
Article 121 Restriction on discussion in Parliament Restricts discussion of the conduct of Supreme Court and High Court judges in Parliament except upon a motion for their removal.
Article 211 Restriction on discussion in State Legislatures Restricts discussion of the conduct of Supreme Court and High Court judges in State Legislatures.
Article 217 Appointment and conditions of office of High Court judges Provides constitutional safeguards concerning High Court judges.
Article 218 Application of certain provisions to High Courts Applies specified provisions relating to removal of Supreme Court judges to High Court judges.
Article 222 Transfer of High Court judges Provides the constitutional mechanism for transfer of judges from one High Court to another.
Article 235 Control over subordinate courts Places control over district courts and courts subordinate thereto in the High Court.
Article 311 Protection of civil servants Provides safeguards for civil servants, relevant to understanding institutional separation but not a direct judicial-independence provision.

3. Why Is Judicial Independence Necessary?

3.1 Protection of Fundamental Rights

Citizens must be able to approach courts when their rights are violated by the State or other parties. Judicial independence ensures that courts can decide such cases without fear of political or administrative consequences.

3.2 Constitutional supremacy

The Constitution is binding on all organs of government. Independent courts ensure that legislative and executive authorities do not exceed their constitutional powers.

3.3 Rule of law

The rule of law requires that public authorities and individuals remain subject to law. Independent courts help prevent arbitrary government action and ensure impartial adjudication.

3.4 Federal balance

In a federal system, disputes may arise between the Union and States or among States. An independent judiciary provides an impartial forum for resolving constitutional disputes.

3.5 Free and fair justice

Judicial independence helps ensure that cases are decided on legal merit rather than social status, wealth, political influence or personal relationships.

4. Safeguards Ensuring Independence of the Supreme Court

4.1 Security of tenure

A Supreme Court judge holds office until attaining the age of 65 years, unless the judge resigns or is removed through the constitutionally prescribed process.

4.2 Difficult removal procedure

A Supreme Court judge can be removed only by an order of the President after an address by each House of Parliament, supported by a majority of the total membership of that House and a majority of not less than two-thirds of members present and voting, on the ground of proved misbehaviour or incapacity.

4.3 Security of salaries and allowances

Salaries and allowances of Supreme Court judges are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India. They cannot be varied to a judge's disadvantage after appointment, except during a Financial Emergency under Article 360.

4.4 Restriction on legislative discussion

Article 121 prohibits discussion in Parliament about the conduct of Supreme Court or High Court judges in the discharge of their duties, except upon a motion for their removal.

4.5 Power to punish for contempt

Under Article 129, the Supreme Court is a Court of Record and has the power to punish for contempt of itself. High Courts have a corresponding constitutional position under Article 215.

4.6 Separation from the executive

The Constitution establishes an institutional separation between the judiciary and executive. Article 50 specifically directs the State to separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State.

4.7 Freedom to administer internal affairs

The Supreme Court has authority over its own administrative functioning, subject to the Constitution and applicable laws. This supports institutional autonomy.

5. Safeguards Ensuring Independence of High Courts

The Constitution provides safeguards for High Court judges broadly comparable to those applicable to Supreme Court judges, with certain differences.

  • High Court judges hold office until attaining the age of 62 years.
  • They can be removed only through the same constitutional process applicable to Supreme Court judges.
  • Their salaries and allowances are protected under Article 221.
  • Article 211 restricts discussion in State Legislatures about their conduct in the discharge of duties, except as constitutionally permitted.
  • High Courts are Courts of Record under Article 215.
  • Article 227 gives High Courts supervisory jurisdiction over subordinate courts and tribunals within their territorial jurisdiction, subject to constitutional exceptions.
  • Article 235 vests control over district courts and subordinate courts in the High Court.

6. Separation of Judiciary from Executive

The separation of judiciary from executive is an important constitutional safeguard against concentration of governmental power.

Article 50 is part of the Directive Principles of State Policy. It directs the State to take steps to separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State.

In a system where executive officials exercise judicial functions without adequate institutional safeguards, there is a risk that adjudication may be influenced by administrative or political considerations.

Separation helps ensure that disputes are decided by judicial officers who are institutionally independent and subject to the control and supervision of the judicial hierarchy.

Remember: Article 50 is a Directive Principle, not a Fundamental Right. However, it expresses an important constitutional objective for the independence of the judiciary.

7. Independence of Subordinate Judiciary

Judicial independence is not confined to the Supreme Court and High Courts. It must also extend to district courts and other subordinate courts, where a large proportion of ordinary citizens seek justice.

Important constitutional provisions

  • Article 233: Appointment of district judges by the Governor in consultation with the High Court.
  • Article 234: Recruitment of persons other than district judges to the judicial service of a State.
  • Article 235: Control over district courts and courts subordinate thereto is vested in the High Court.
  • Article 236: Defines certain expressions relating to the State judicial service.
  • Article 237: Enables the Governor to apply the provisions of the relevant chapter to certain classes of magistrates, subject to the constitutional conditions.

Article 235 is particularly significant because it gives High Courts control over the subordinate judiciary in matters such as postings, promotions and disciplinary control, subject to constitutional requirements and applicable service rules.

8. Appointment of Judges and Judicial Independence

The method of appointment of judges is closely connected with judicial independence. A system that allows improper political influence over appointments may affect public confidence in impartial adjudication.

The Constitution provides for the appointment of Supreme Court and High Court judges by the President. Through judicial interpretation, the collegium system developed as the present mechanism for recommending appointments and transfers of higher judiciary judges.

Evolution of the collegium system

  • First Judges Case (1981): S.P. Gupta v. Union of India gave primacy to the executive in the appointment process as then interpreted.
  • Second Judges Case (1993): Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India established judicial primacy and the collegium arrangement.
  • Third Judges Case (1998): A Presidential Reference clarified the composition and functioning of the collegium.

The collegium system is a product of judicial interpretation. It is not expressly set out in the text of the Constitution.

The National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC), created through the 99th Constitutional Amendment, was declared unconstitutional by the Supreme Court in 2015. The Court held that the arrangement violated the basic structure, particularly judicial independence.

Important distinction: The President formally appoints judges under the Constitution. The collegium makes recommendations through the judicially developed appointment process.

9. Transfer of High Court Judges

Article 222 empowers the President to transfer a judge from one High Court to another after consultation with the Chief Justice of India.

Transfers are intended to serve legitimate institutional purposes, including improving the administration of justice and addressing administrative requirements.

However, the power must not be used as a means of punishment, intimidation or improper interference with judicial independence.

Judicial decisions have emphasised the importance of consultation and procedural safeguards in the transfer process.

10. Financial Independence

Financial security is an important element of judicial independence. Judges should not be exposed to financial pressure that could influence their decisions.

The Constitution provides safeguards concerning the salaries and allowances of Supreme Court and High Court judges.

  • Supreme Court judges' salaries and allowances are dealt with under Article 125.
  • High Court judges' salaries and allowances are dealt with under Article 221.
  • Relevant expenditure is charged on the Consolidated Fund of India or the State, as constitutionally provided.
  • Conditions of service are protected against disadvantageous variation, subject to constitutional exceptions.

11. Post-Retirement Restrictions

Restrictions on certain post-retirement legal practice are intended to reduce possible conflicts of interest and preserve public confidence in judicial impartiality.

Supreme Court judges

Article 124(7) provides that a person who has held office as a judge of the Supreme Court shall not plead or act in any court or before any authority within the territory of India.

High Court judges

Article 220 restricts a permanent judge of a High Court from pleading or acting in any court or before any authority in India, except the Supreme Court and other High Courts.

UPSC point: Article 124(7) applies to former Supreme Court judges, while Article 220 deals with restrictions on practice by permanent High Court judges.

12. Threats and Challenges to Judicial Independence

12.1 Executive interference

Improper pressure from the executive in appointments, transfers, administrative matters or individual cases can undermine judicial independence.

12.2 Political influence

Political pressure or public campaigns targeting judges may threaten impartial adjudication and institutional confidence.

12.3 Judicial vacancies and delays

Vacancies, inadequate infrastructure and a large case backlog weaken the effective functioning of courts and can reduce access to timely justice.

12.4 Financial and administrative dependence

Inadequate control over resources, infrastructure and court administration may affect the ability of the judiciary to function independently.

12.5 Post-retirement appointments

Appointments of retired judges to public offices can raise questions about possible conflicts of interest or perceptions of influence, even where an appointment is legally permissible.

12.6 Lack of transparency

Limited transparency in appointments, transfers and certain administrative processes may affect public trust.

12.7 Allegations of misconduct

Misconduct by individual members of the judiciary can damage public confidence. Institutional accountability must therefore coexist with safeguards against improper interference.

13. Judicial Accountability and Independence

Judicial independence and judicial accountability are complementary constitutional values. Independence protects judges from improper influence, while accountability ensures that judicial authority is exercised responsibly.

Accountability may involve constitutional removal procedures, judicial ethics, transparent institutional practices, reasoned judgments and scrutiny through appellate processes.

Removal of judges

The Constitution provides a difficult removal procedure for Supreme Court and High Court judges. Removal is possible only on the grounds of proved misbehaviour or incapacity and through the special parliamentary process prescribed by the Constitution.

In-house procedures

The judiciary has also developed internal mechanisms for addressing complaints and allegations concerning judges. These mechanisms operate separately from the constitutional parliamentary removal process.

Balance: Accountability should not become a mechanism for pressuring judges over decisions made in good faith. Equally, judicial independence should not be used to avoid legitimate scrutiny of misconduct.

14. Important Judicial Decisions

Case Year Significance
S.P. Gupta v. Union of India 1981 First Judges Case; interpreted the appointment process with primacy to the executive as then understood.
Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India 1993 Second Judges Case; established judicial primacy in appointments and the collegium system.
In re Special Reference No. 1 of 1998 1998 Third Judges Case; clarified the collegium's composition and consultation process.
Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India 2015 Struck down the 99th Constitutional Amendment and NJAC Act for violating judicial independence as part of the basic structure.
Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala 1973 Established the basic structure doctrine, within which judicial independence is recognised as an essential feature.
Minerva Mills v. Union of India 1980 Reaffirmed limited amending power and the constitutional importance of judicial review.

15. Measures to Strengthen Judicial Independence

  • Ensure timely appointment of judges and reduce vacancies.
  • Improve court infrastructure, staffing and administrative resources.
  • Maintain transparent and principled procedures for appointments and transfers.
  • Strengthen judicial ethics and appropriate internal accountability mechanisms.
  • Protect judges from political pressure and threats.
  • Improve access to legal aid and affordable justice.
  • Ensure that post-retirement appointments do not create avoidable conflicts of interest.
  • Promote technology and effective case management to reduce delays.
  • Maintain a clear distinction between judicial adjudication and executive administration.

16. Important Distinctions for UPSC

Concept Meaning

Collegium system Judicially developed system for recommending appointments and transfers of higher judiciary judges.
Separation of powers Distribution of governmental functions among legislature, executive and judiciary, with constitutional checks and balances.

18. Self-Assessment Questions

Question 1

Which Directive Principle directs the State to separate the judiciary from the executive?

Answer: Article 50.

Question 2

What is the retirement age of a Supreme Court judge?

Answer: 65 years.

Question 3

What is the retirement age of a High Court judge?

Answer: 62 years.

Question 4

Which article gives the High Court control over subordinate courts?

Answer: Article 235.

Question 5

Which constitutional amendment created the NJAC framework?

Answer: The 99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014.

Question 6

Which judgment struck down the NJAC framework?

Answer: Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015).
