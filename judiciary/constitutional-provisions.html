Constitutional Provisions of the Indian Judiciary

The Constitution of India establishes an independent and integrated judicial system. It defines the structure, jurisdiction and powers of the Supreme Court, High Courts and subordinate courts, while protecting judicial independence and providing remedies for the enforcement of constitutional rights.

1. Constitutional Framework of the Judiciary

The Indian Constitution establishes a single integrated judicial system with the Supreme Court at the apex, High Courts at the State level and subordinate courts below them.

Unlike a system with completely separate federal and State court hierarchies, India's judicial structure operates as an integrated system. The Supreme Court is the highest judicial authority, and the High Courts supervise the subordinate judiciary within their respective jurisdictions.

The principal constitutional provisions relating to the judiciary are found in:

  • Part V, Chapter IV: The Union Judiciary.
  • Part VI, Chapter V: The High Courts in the States.
  • Part VI, Chapter VI: Subordinate Courts.
  • Part XIV-A: Tribunals.
  • Part III: Fundamental Rights and constitutional remedies.
  • Part IV: Directive Principles of State Policy.

5. Fundamental Rights and the Judiciary

Part III of the Constitution guarantees Fundamental Rights. The judiciary plays a central role in protecting these rights against unconstitutional State action.

Article 13: Laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights

Article 13 declares that laws inconsistent with or in derogation of Fundamental Rights are void to the extent of their inconsistency. It provides an important constitutional basis for judicial review of legislation.

Article 32: Constitutional Remedies

Article 32 guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights. The Supreme Court may issue directions, orders or writs, including habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari.

Dr. B. R. Ambedkar described Article 32 as the heart and soul of the Constitution.

Article 226: Writ Jurisdiction of High Courts

Article 226 empowers High Courts to issue writs for enforcement of Fundamental Rights and for any other purpose. Its scope is therefore wider than Article 32 in terms of the purposes for which writs may be issued.

6. Directive Principle: Article 50

Article 50 directs the State to take steps to separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State.

It is a Directive Principle of State Policy and is not directly enforceable by courts. However, it expresses an important constitutional objective: an independent judiciary separated from executive influence.

Remember: Article 50 is a Directive Principle, while judicial independence is also supported by several enforceable constitutional provisions and has been recognised as part of the basic structure.

8. Constitutional Safeguards for Judicial Independence

Judicial independence is essential for constitutional government, the rule of law and protection of Fundamental Rights. The Constitution provides several safeguards.

  • Security of tenure: Judges can be removed only through the constitutionally prescribed process.
  • Protected service conditions: Salaries and allowances are constitutionally regulated.
  • Charged expenditure: Specified judicial expenditure is charged on the Consolidated Fund, subject to constitutional provisions.
  • Restrictions after retirement: Certain restrictions apply to judicial practice after retirement.
  • Judicial control over subordinate courts: Article 235 vests control in the High Court.
  • Contempt powers: The Supreme Court and High Courts are Courts of Record with constitutional contempt powers.
  • Separation of judiciary and executive: Article 50 expresses this constitutional objective.
  • Judicial review: Courts can examine the constitutional validity of State action.

9. Important Constitutional Doctrines and Judicial Decisions

9.1 Basic Structure Doctrine: Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)

The Supreme Court held that Parliament's power to amend the Constitution under Article 368 does not extend to destroying its basic structure.

Judicial review and judicial independence are recognised as important elements of the basic structure.

9.2 Judicial Review: Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)

The Supreme Court reaffirmed the importance of limited amending power and the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. It also reinforced the constitutional significance of judicial review.

9.3 Judicial Appointments: Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (1993)

The Second Judges Case established the collegium system by giving primacy to the opinion of the Chief Justice of India in judicial appointments, formed through consultation with senior judges.

9.4 Third Judges Case (1998)

In its advisory opinion, the Supreme Court clarified the composition and consultation process of the collegium system for judicial appointments.

9.5 National Judicial Appointments Commission Case (2015)

In Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India, the Supreme Court invalidated the 99th Constitutional Amendment and the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, holding that the arrangement violated the basic structure principle of judicial independence.

9.6 L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997)

The Supreme Court held that judicial review under Articles 226 and 227 of the High Courts and Article 32 of the Supreme Court forms part of the basic structure.

11. Quick Revision

  • Articles 124–147 deal with the Supreme Court.
  • Articles 214–231 deal with High Courts.
  • Articles 233–237 deal with subordinate courts.
  • Articles 323A and 323B deal with tribunals.
  • Article 32 provides the right to constitutional remedies before the Supreme Court.
  • Article 226 gives High Courts wider writ purposes than Article 32.
  • Article 141 makes the law declared by the Supreme Court binding on all courts in India.
  • Article 142 empowers the Supreme Court to do complete justice.
  • Article 143 provides for advisory jurisdiction.
  • Article 50 directs separation of the judiciary from the executive.
  • Article 235 vests control over subordinate courts in the High Court.
  • Judicial review and judicial independence are elements of the basic structure.
