Appointment and Removal of Judges

The appointment and removal of judges are central to the independence and accountability of the Indian judiciary. The Constitution establishes the formal appointment process and a special removal procedure, while judicial interpretation has shaped the collegium system for appointments to the higher judiciary.

1. Constitutional Framework

The Constitution provides separate provisions for the appointment of judges to the Supreme Court, High Courts and subordinate judiciary. The President is the formal appointing authority for judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, while appointments to the subordinate judicial service involve the Governor and the High Court under the relevant constitutional provisions.

2. Appointment of Supreme Court Judges

Article 124 provides for the establishment and constitution of the Supreme Court. The President appoints the Chief Justice of India and other judges of the Supreme Court in accordance with the constitutional framework as interpreted by the Supreme Court.

Constitutional qualifications

A person is qualified for appointment as a Supreme Court judge if they:

  • Are a citizen of India; and
  • Have been a judge of a High Court or of two or more such courts in succession for at least five years; or
  • Have been an advocate of a High Court or of two or more such courts in succession for at least ten years; or
  • Are, in the opinion of the President, a distinguished jurist.

Retirement age

A Supreme Court judge holds office until attaining the age of 65 years, unless the judge resigns or is removed through the constitutional process.

Appointment of the Chief Justice of India

Conventionally, the senior-most judge of the Supreme Court considered fit to hold the office is appointed as the Chief Justice of India. The appointment is made by the President.

Seniority convention is an established practice, rather than an express qualification stated in Article 124.

3. Appointment of High Court Judges

Article 217 provides for the appointment of High Court judges. The President appoints the Chief Justice and other judges of a High Court through the constitutionally prescribed consultation process, as interpreted by judicial decisions.

Qualifications

A person must:

  • Be a citizen of India; and
  • Have held a judicial office in the territory of India for at least ten years; or
  • Have been an advocate of a High Court or of two or more such courts in succession for at least ten years.

Retirement age

A High Court judge holds office until attaining the age of 62 years, unless the judge resigns or is removed through the constitutional process.

Consultation

Article 217 refers to consultation with the Chief Justice of India, the Governor of the State and, in the case of appointment of a judge other than the Chief Justice, the Chief Justice of the concerned High Court. The meaning and relative weight of these consultations have been shaped by the Judges Cases.

4. Evolution of the Collegium System

The collegium system is the judicially developed mechanism through which recommendations are made for appointments and transfers of judges in the higher judiciary. It is not expressly mentioned in the text of the Constitution.

4.1 First Judges Case — S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981)

The Supreme Court interpreted the word "consultation" in the appointment provisions in a manner that gave primacy to the executive. The opinion of the Chief Justice of India was not treated as binding on the President.

4.2 Second Judges Case — Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (1993)

The Supreme Court overruled the earlier approach and established judicial primacy in appointments. The opinion of the Chief Justice of India was to be formed collectively through consultation with senior Supreme Court judges.

This judgment established the collegium arrangement for recommendations relating to appointments and transfers in the higher judiciary.

4.3 Third Judges Case — In re Special Reference No. 1 of 1998

In response to a Presidential Reference, the Supreme Court clarified the composition and functioning of the collegium. For appointments to the Supreme Court, the collegium consists of the Chief Justice of India and four senior-most Supreme Court judges.

For appointments to High Courts, the collegium at the Supreme Court level consists of the Chief Justice of India and two senior-most Supreme Court judges. The High Court collegium also plays an important role in initiating recommendations.

Remember: The collegium is a judicially evolved appointment mechanism. The President remains the formal appointing authority under the Constitution.

5. Composition and Working of the Collegium

Supreme Court appointments

The Supreme Court collegium consists of:

  • The Chief Justice of India.
  • Four senior-most judges of the Supreme Court.

High Court appointments

The Supreme Court collegium for High Court appointments consists of:

  • The Chief Justice of India.
  • Two senior-most judges of the Supreme Court.

Recommendations for High Court appointments originate through the High Court collegium, which includes the Chief Justice of that High Court and its two senior-most judges. The recommendations pass through the prescribed consultation and government process before final appointment.

Role of the executive

The Union Government processes recommendations and may seek reconsideration in accordance with the established procedure. If the collegium reiterates a recommendation, the constitutional convention and judicial framework give significant weight to that reiterated recommendation.

The process involves consultation and institutional checks; it is not a unilateral appointment by either the judiciary or the executive.

6. National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC)

The National Judicial Appointments Commission was proposed as an alternative to the collegium system. The 99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014 inserted provisions for the NJAC, while the NJAC Act, 2014 provided its statutory framework.

Composition of the NJAC

  • The Chief Justice of India as Chairperson.
  • Two senior-most Supreme Court judges after the Chief Justice of India.
  • The Union Minister in charge of Law and Justice.
  • Two eminent persons nominated by a committee consisting of the Prime Minister, the Chief Justice of India and the Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha, or the leader of the single largest opposition party where applicable.

Supreme Court judgment of 2015

In Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015), the Supreme Court struck down the 99th Constitutional Amendment and the NJAC Act.

The majority held that the framework violated the basic structure of the Constitution because it compromised judicial independence. The collegium system therefore continued.

UPSC point: The NJAC was not rejected merely because it included executive participation. The Court's concern was that the composition and veto arrangement could undermine judicial primacy and independence.

7. Appointment of District Judges and Other Judicial Officers

Article 233: District judges

Appointments, postings and promotions of district judges in a State are made by the Governor in consultation with the High Court exercising jurisdiction in relation to that State.

A person not already in the service of the Union or State must have been an advocate or pleader for at least seven years and must be recommended by the High Court for appointment as a district judge.

Article 234: Other judicial officers

Appointments of persons other than district judges to the judicial service of a State are made by the Governor in accordance with rules made after consultation with the State Public Service Commission and the High Court.

Article 235: Control by High Courts

Control over district courts and courts subordinate thereto, including matters such as posting, promotion and disciplinary control, is vested in the High Court, subject to the Constitution and applicable service rules.

8. Removal of Supreme Court Judges

The Constitution provides a special and difficult procedure for removing a Supreme Court judge. This safeguard is intended to protect judges from arbitrary removal by the executive or legislature.

Constitutional grounds

Under Article 124(4), a Supreme Court judge can be removed only on the grounds of:

  • Proved misbehaviour; or
  • Incapacity.

Removal procedure

  1. A motion seeking removal may be introduced in either House of Parliament.
  2. It must be signed by at least 100 members of the Lok Sabha or 50 members of the Rajya Sabha.
  3. The Speaker of the Lok Sabha or the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha may admit or refuse to admit the motion.
  4. If admitted, an inquiry committee is constituted under the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968.
  5. The committee investigates the charges and submits its findings.
  6. If the committee finds the judge guilty of misbehaviour or suffering from incapacity, the motion is taken up for consideration by Parliament.
  7. Each House must adopt the address by a majority of its total membership and by a majority of not less than two-thirds of members present and voting.
  8. After the required parliamentary addresses, the President issues the order of removal.
Important: The required special majority must be achieved separately in each House. The two Houses do not vote together as a joint sitting.

9. Removal of High Court Judges

A High Court judge is removed through the same constitutional process applicable to a Supreme Court judge.

Article 217(1)(b) provides that a High Court judge may be removed from office in the manner provided in Article 124(4). Article 218 applies the relevant provisions of Article 124 to High Courts.

Therefore, the grounds are proved misbehaviour or incapacity, and the parliamentary special-majority procedure is the same.

10. Resignation of Judges

Resignation is different from removal. A Supreme Court judge may resign by writing under the judge's hand addressed to the President.

A High Court judge may also resign by writing under the judge's hand addressed to the President.

Resignation does not require the parliamentary inquiry and special-majority procedure applicable to removal.

11. Impeachment: Correct Constitutional Usage

The term "impeachment of judges" is commonly used in public discussion and examination material. However, the Constitution does not expressly use the word impeachment for the removal of Supreme Court or High Court judges.

The Constitution uses the term "removal" and prescribes the procedure under Article 124(4), read with Article 124(5), Article 217 and Article 218.

The distinction is important because the process is a constitutionally prescribed removal procedure, not the impeachment process applicable to the President under Article 61.

12. Historical Attempts to Remove Judges

The parliamentary removal procedure has been invoked in a small number of prominent cases. These episodes illustrate the difficulty of securing the special majority required by the Constitution.

Justice V. Ramaswami

In 1993, a motion seeking the removal of Justice V. Ramaswami was considered in the Lok Sabha after an inquiry process. The motion failed to secure the required majority because many members abstained.

Justice Soumitra Sen

In 2011, the Rajya Sabha passed a motion seeking the removal of Justice Soumitra Sen. He resigned before the Lok Sabha completed consideration of the motion.

Justice P.D. Dinakaran

Removal proceedings were initiated against Justice P.D. Dinakaran, but he resigned before the parliamentary process was completed.

Justice C.S. Karnan

Justice C.S. Karnan was a subject of significant judicial proceedings and was convicted of contempt of court by the Supreme Court in 2017. His case did not result in removal through the parliamentary process under Article 124(4).

13. Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968

Article 124(5) empowers Parliament to regulate by law the procedure for presentation of an address and for investigation and proof of misbehaviour or incapacity of a judge.

The Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968 provides the statutory framework for investigation of allegations against Supreme Court and High Court judges in the context of removal proceedings.

The inquiry committee under the Act consists of:

  • The Chief Justice of India or a judge of the Supreme Court.
  • The Chief Justice of a High Court.
  • A distinguished jurist.

The committee examines the charges and reports its findings. The parliamentary process proceeds according to the statutory and constitutional requirements.

14. Comparison: Appointment and Removal

Basis Appointment Removal
Supreme Court judges President under Article 124, following the established consultation and collegium process. President after the constitutionally prescribed parliamentary address under Article 124(4).
High Court judges President under Article 217, following the established consultation and collegium process. Same removal procedure as Supreme Court judges, under Articles 217 and 218 read with Article 124(4).
District judges Governor in consultation with the High Court under Article 233. Subject to applicable service rules and constitutional control of the High Court under Article 235.
Other judicial officers Governor under Article 234, following consultation with the State Public Service Commission and High Court. Subject to applicable service rules and constitutional safeguards governing the State judicial service.

15. Issues and Challenges in the Appointment System

15.1 Lack of transparency

Critics argue that the collegium system does not always provide sufficient public explanation of the criteria and reasoning behind individual recommendations.

15.2 Executive-judiciary disagreements

Differences between the collegium and the Union Government over recommendations can delay appointments and create institutional friction.

15.3 Representation and diversity

Questions continue about the representation of women, Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, Other Backward Classes and other underrepresented groups in the higher judiciary.

15.4 Judicial vacancies

Delays in recommendations, reconsiderations and final appointments may contribute to vacancies and increase the burden on serving judges.

15.5 Accountability and independence

The appointment process must maintain judicial independence while ensuring transparency, integrity and institutional accountability.

16. Reforms and Way Forward

  • Develop clearer and publicly understandable criteria for judicial appointments.
  • Improve transparency while protecting the confidentiality of legitimate consultations.
  • Ensure timely processing of recommendations and reduce delays in appointments.
  • Encourage diversity and wider representation in the higher judiciary.
  • Strengthen judicial infrastructure and administrative support.
  • Improve the functioning of internal accountability mechanisms.
  • Maintain institutional dialogue between the judiciary and executive within constitutional limits.
  • Ensure that any proposed reform protects judicial independence as a basic feature of the Constitution.

18. Quick Revision

  • Article 124 deals with appointment of Supreme Court judges.
  • Article 217 deals with appointment of High Court judges.
  • Article 233 deals with appointment of district judges.
  • Article 234 deals with recruitment of other judicial officers.
  • Article 235 gives High Courts control over subordinate courts.
  • Supreme Court judges retire at 65 years; High Court judges retire at 62 years.
  • The collegium system developed through the Second and Third Judges Cases.
  • The NJAC framework was struck down in 2015.
  • Removal of Supreme Court and High Court judges is based on proved misbehaviour or incapacity.
  • Each House of Parliament must pass the removal address by a majority of its total membership and a two-thirds majority of members present and voting.
  • The President issues the final order of removal.
