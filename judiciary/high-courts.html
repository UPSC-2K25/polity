STATE JUDICIARY · ARTICLES 214–231

High Courts in India The principal constitutional courts at the State level.

High Courts occupy the highest position in the judicial hierarchy of a State or a group of States. They exercise original, appellate, writ and supervisory jurisdiction under the Constitution and other applicable laws.

High Courts play a central role in protecting fundamental rights, supervising subordinate courts, interpreting laws and ensuring that public authorities act within their legal and constitutional powers.

CONSTITUTIONAL FRAMEWORK

Articles 214–231: The High Courts

Part VI, Chapter V of the Constitution deals with High Courts in the States. Article 214 provides that there shall be a High Court for each State, subject to the constitutional provisions permitting a common High Court for two or more States or for States and Union Territories.

The constitutional framework covers the establishment, composition, appointment and service conditions of judges, jurisdiction, powers, transfer of judges and other institutional matters.

01 · FOUNDATION

Establishment and constitutional position

Article 214 provides for a High Court for each State. However, the Constitution also permits Parliament to establish a common High Court for two or more States or for two or more States and one or more Union Territories under Article 231.

High Courts form an important part of India's integrated judicial system. They stand below the Supreme Court and above the subordinate courts within their respective territorial jurisdictions.

Historical background

Several High Courts in India trace their institutional origins to the High Courts established during British rule under the Indian High Courts Act, 1861. The High Courts at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras were established in 1862.

The Constitution continued the institution of High Courts while placing them within a democratic and constitutional framework. Their powers and independence are now protected by constitutional provisions.

High Courts and the integrated judiciary

High Courts form the principal judicial institutions at the State level. They exercise jurisdiction over subordinate courts and tribunals within their constitutionally and legally defined authority.

Important distinction

A High Court is not an institution subordinate to the State executive. It is a constitutional court with independent judicial authority. Its supervisory relationship with subordinate courts is judicial, not executive.

02 · ARTICLE 216

Composition of High Courts

Article 216 provides that every High Court shall consist of a Chief Justice and such other judges as the President may from time to time deem necessary to appoint.

Unlike the Supreme Court, the Constitution does not prescribe a fixed numerical strength for each High Court. The strength may vary depending on factors such as the volume of cases, workload and administrative requirements.

Chief Justice of a High Court

The Chief Justice heads the High Court. The office carries judicial and administrative responsibilities, including the allocation of judicial work and constitution of benches in accordance with applicable rules and established practice.

Other judges

Other judges hear cases individually or in benches. The size and composition of benches depend on the nature of the case, the rules of the High Court and applicable legal requirements.

Additional and acting judges

Article 224 provides for the appointment of additional and acting judges in specified circumstances.

  • Additional judges may be appointed when there is a temporary increase in the business of a High Court or arrears of work.
  • An acting judge may be appointed when a judge, other than the Chief Justice, is unable to perform the duties of office or is appointed temporarily as Chief Justice.

An additional or acting judge cannot hold office after attaining the age of 62 years.

03 · ARTICLES 217 AND 222

Appointment of High Court judges

Under Article 217, every judge of a High Court is appointed by the President of India by warrant under the President's hand and seal.

The constitutional provision requires consultation with the Chief Justice of India, the Governor of the State and, in the case of appointment of a judge other than the Chief Justice, the Chief Justice of the concerned High Court.

The present appointment process is governed by the judicially evolved collegium system, which gives primacy to the judiciary in recommending appointments to the higher judiciary.

Role of the collegium

For appointments to a High Court, the process begins with recommendations from the Chief Justice of the concerned High Court in consultation with the two senior-most judges of that Court.

The recommendations are considered through the prescribed institutional process involving the State authorities and the Supreme Court collegium before appointment by the President.

Transfer of High Court judges — Article 222

Article 222 empowers the President to transfer a judge from one High Court to another after consultation with the Chief Justice of India.

The transfer process has been shaped by judicial decisions and the collegium framework. The power is exercised within constitutional requirements and established safeguards.

UPSC distinction

High Court judges are formally appointed by the President. Their retirement age is 62 years, whereas the retirement age of Supreme Court judges is 65 years.

04 · ARTICLE 217

Qualifications of High Court judges

Article 217, read with Article 217(2), specifies the qualifications required for appointment as a judge of a High Court. A person must be a citizen of India and must satisfy at least one of the following:

  1. The person must have held a judicial office in the territory of India for at least ten years.
  2. The person must have been an advocate of a High Court, or of two or more such Courts in succession, for at least ten years.

Important points

  • Citizenship of India is essential.
  • The minimum qualifying period is ten years.
  • The Constitution recognises both judicial service and legal practice as qualifying routes.
  • There is no separate constitutional qualification of distinguished jurist for High Court judges.
Comparison with the Supreme Court

Article 124(3) recognises a distinguished jurist as one of the qualifying categories for appointment to the Supreme Court. Article 217 does not provide the same category for High Court judges.

05 · ARTICLES 217 AND 218

Tenure, resignation and removal

Retirement age

A High Court judge holds office until attaining the age of 62 years.

Resignation

A High Court judge may resign by writing under the judge's hand addressed to the President of India.

Removal of High Court judges

Article 218 applies the removal procedure prescribed for Supreme Court judges under Article 124(4) and Article 124(5) to High Court judges.

A High Court judge can be removed only by the President after each House of Parliament passes an address supported by the prescribed special majority. The grounds are proved misbehaviour or incapacity.

Parliamentary majority

  • A majority of the total membership of the House; and
  • A majority of not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting.

The addresses must be passed by both Houses in the same session. The President then issues the removal order.

Remember

High Court judges do not have a separate, easier removal procedure. The constitutional removal process is substantially the same as that applicable to Supreme Court judges.

06 · JUDICIAL POWERS

Jurisdiction of High Courts

High Courts exercise several forms of jurisdiction under the Constitution and other laws. Their powers are particularly important in protecting rights and supervising the administration of justice within their territorial areas.

A. Writ jurisdiction — Article 226

Article 226 empowers every High Court to issue directions, orders or writs, including habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto.

High Courts may issue writs not only for the enforcement of fundamental rights but also for any other purpose. This makes Article 226 wider in scope than Article 32, which concerns the enforcement of fundamental rights.

Territorial limits under Article 226

A High Court may exercise writ jurisdiction throughout the territories in relation to which it exercises jurisdiction. It may also exercise jurisdiction where the cause of action, wholly or in part, arises within its territory, even if the seat of the authority or person is outside that territory, subject to the constitutional conditions.

B. Original jurisdiction

Original jurisdiction means that proceedings may be instituted directly before a High Court rather than being brought first before a subordinate court.

The extent of original jurisdiction differs among High Courts and depends on constitutional provisions, statutes and applicable procedural laws.

C. Appellate jurisdiction

High Courts hear appeals from subordinate courts in civil and criminal matters. The nature and scope of these appeals are determined by the relevant laws.

They may examine questions of law, questions of fact or both, depending on the applicable appellate provisions.

D. Supervisory jurisdiction — Article 227

Article 227 gives every High Court superintendence over all courts and tribunals throughout the territories in relation to which it exercises jurisdiction, subject to the constitutional exceptions.

This supervisory power enables High Courts to ensure that subordinate judicial institutions function within the limits of their authority and follow the law.

E. Control over subordinate courts — Article 235

Article 235 vests control over district courts and courts subordinate to them in the High Court. This includes important matters relating to posting, promotion and leave of judicial officers, subject to applicable service rules.

Article 226 and Article 32

Article 32 is itself a fundamental right and concerns enforcement of fundamental rights. Article 226 is a constitutional power of High Courts and extends to fundamental rights as well as other legal rights.

07 · ARTICLE 215

High Court as a Court of Record

Article 215 declares every High Court to be a Court of Record. Its records have evidentiary value and its judicial acts are preserved as authoritative records.

A High Court, as a Court of Record, also has the power to punish for contempt of itself. This power helps protect the authority of the Court and the administration of justice.

Contempt of court

Contempt jurisdiction is intended to protect the proper functioning of courts and the administration of justice. It is not intended to shield judges from fair criticism.

08 · INSTITUTIONAL SAFEGUARDS

Independence of High Courts

The independence of High Courts is essential to constitutional governance. They must be able to examine State action, protect legal rights and supervise subordinate courts without improper executive interference.

Important constitutional safeguards

  • Security of tenure: Removal is possible only through the special constitutional process.
  • Appointment framework: The collegium system provides judicial participation and primacy in higher judicial appointments.
  • Protected service conditions: Article 221 deals with salaries and allowances of High Court judges.
  • Restrictions after retirement: Article 220 restricts practice by a permanent High Court judge after retirement, subject to the constitutional exception for the Supreme Court and other High Courts.
  • Control over subordinate judiciary: Article 235 places control over subordinate courts in the High Court.
  • Administrative autonomy: High Courts exercise institutional administrative functions within the constitutional framework.

09 · FEDERAL ARRANGEMENT

Common High Courts

Article 231 empowers Parliament to establish a common High Court for two or more States or for two or more States and one or more Union Territories.

A common High Court serves the territories specified by law. It does not mean that the States lose their separate constitutional identities or legislative powers.

The arrangement is intended to provide a shared higher judicial institution for the territories concerned.

Examples

  • The Punjab and Haryana High Court serves Punjab, Haryana and the Union Territory of Chandigarh.
  • The Gauhati High Court has jurisdiction over Assam and certain other North-Eastern States under the applicable constitutional and statutory arrangements.

The jurisdiction and territorial arrangement of each High Court must be understood with reference to the relevant constitutional provisions and laws.

What you should remember

  1. Article 214 provides for a High Court for each State, subject to the common High Court arrangement.
  2. Articles 214–231 deal with High Courts.
  3. The President appoints High Court judges under Article 217.
  4. The retirement age of a High Court judge is 62 years.
  5. Article 226 provides writ jurisdiction for fundamental rights and other purposes.
  6. Article 227 provides supervisory jurisdiction over subordinate courts and tribunals.
  7. Article 235 gives the High Court control over subordinate courts.
  8. Article 215 declares every High Court to be a Court of Record.
  9. Article 218 applies the relevant removal provisions for Supreme Court judges to High Court judges.
  10. Article 231 permits Parliament to establish a common High Court.
