Statutory Bodies

Reserve Bank of India

The Reserve Bank of India is India's central bank. It regulates monetary policy, issues currency, manages foreign exchange reserves and contributes to the stability of the financial system.

1. Introduction and Evolution

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) is the central banking institution of India. It was established under the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934, following the recommendations of the Hilton Young Commission, also known as the Royal Commission on Indian Currency and Finance.

  • Established: 1 April 1935.
  • Initial ownership: Privately owned shareholders' bank.
  • Nationalisation: 1 January 1949.
  • Headquarters: Mumbai.
  • Legal basis: Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934.

After nationalisation, the RBI became wholly owned by the Government of India. It performs central banking functions for the country and acts as the banker to the government and banks.

2. Objectives of the RBI

The principal objectives of the RBI include:

  • Maintaining monetary stability and price stability.
  • Managing the currency and credit system of the country.
  • Maintaining financial stability and public confidence in the banking system.
  • Regulating and supervising banks and specified financial institutions.
  • Managing foreign exchange reserves and promoting orderly development of the foreign exchange market.
  • Supporting economic growth while keeping price stability in view.
  • Promoting an efficient and secure payment and settlement system.

3. Organisation and Management

The RBI is managed by a Central Board of Directors constituted under the RBI Act, 1934.

Central Board of Directors

Under Section 8 of the RBI Act, the Central Board includes:

  • A Governor.
  • Not more than four Deputy Governors.
  • Four directors nominated by the Central Government, one from each of the four Local Boards.
  • Ten directors nominated by the Central Government from various fields, including industry, commerce, finance and cooperation.
  • One government official nominated by the Central Government.

The Governor and Deputy Governors are appointed by the Central Government. The Central Board exercises general superintendence and direction over the affairs of the RBI, subject to the provisions of the Act.

Local Boards

The RBI Act provides for four Local Boards representing the four regional areas of the country:

  • Western Area.
  • Eastern Area.
  • Northern Area.
  • Southern Area.

Local Boards advise the Central Board on matters referred to them and represent regional economic and financial interests.

4. Monetary Policy

Monetary policy refers to the actions taken by the RBI to regulate money supply, credit conditions and interest rates in the economy. Its principal objective under the statutory framework is to maintain price stability while keeping in mind the objective of growth.

Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)

The Monetary Policy Committee was introduced through amendments to the RBI Act in 2016. It determines the policy interest rate required to achieve the inflation target.

The MPC consists of six members:

  • The RBI Governor, who is the ex-officio Chairperson.
  • The Deputy Governor of the RBI in charge of monetary policy.
  • One officer of the RBI nominated by the Central Board.
  • Three members appointed by the Central Government, based on the recommendations of the search-cum-selection committee specified in the Act.

The external members are appointed for a term of four years and are not eligible for reappointment. The RBI Governor has a casting vote in case of a tie.

Inflation Targeting

Under the amended RBI Act, the Central Government determines the inflation target in consultation with the RBI once every five years. The notified target for the current framework is 4 per cent consumer price inflation, with a tolerance band of 2 per cent to 6 per cent.

The RBI is required to publish a report explaining the reasons and proposed remedial actions if it fails to meet the target for three consecutive quarters.

Major Monetary Policy Instruments

Instrument Meaning and Purpose
Repo Rate The rate at which the RBI provides short-term liquidity to eligible banks against eligible collateral under the repo framework.
Standing Deposit Facility (SDF) A facility through which banks can deposit surplus funds with the RBI without providing collateral. It serves as the floor of the liquidity adjustment corridor.
Marginal Standing Facility (MSF) A facility that allows eligible scheduled commercial banks to obtain overnight liquidity from the RBI under specified conditions.
Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) The proportion of a bank's net demand and time liabilities that must be maintained as cash balance with the RBI.
Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) The proportion of a bank's net demand and time liabilities that must be maintained in specified liquid assets under the Banking Regulation Act.
Open Market Operations Purchase and sale of government securities by the RBI to manage liquidity in the economy.
Bank Rate The rate at which the RBI is prepared to buy or rediscount eligible bills of exchange or other commercial paper under the RBI Act. It also has statutory implications for certain penal rates.

5. Currency Issuance and Management

The RBI has the sole right to issue banknotes in India, except the one-rupee note and coins, which are issued by the Government of India.

  • Section 22 of the RBI Act grants the RBI the sole right to issue banknotes in India.
  • Section 24 deals with the denominations of banknotes that may be issued.
  • Section 26 deals with the legal tender character of banknotes.
  • Section 25 provides for the design, form and material of banknotes, subject to the prescribed approval process.

Banknotes issued by the RBI are backed by assets held in the Issue Department in accordance with the statutory minimum reserve system.

The Government of India is responsible for issuing coins and the one-rupee note. The RBI distributes coins and notes and manages currency circulation through its offices and banking network.

6. Banker, Agent and Adviser to the Government

The RBI performs the role of banker to the Central Government and, by agreement, to State Governments.

  • Maintains government accounts and receives government deposits.
  • Processes government receipts and payments.
  • Manages public debt and conducts government borrowing programmes.
  • Provides financial advice to the government on monetary and economic matters.
  • Facilitates the management of temporary mismatches in government cash flows through Ways and Means Advances, subject to applicable arrangements.

The RBI acts as banker to banks and maintains accounts of scheduled banks. It also provides liquidity support to eligible institutions under the applicable legal and policy framework.

7. Regulation and Supervision of Banks

The RBI regulates and supervises the banking system to promote financial stability, depositor confidence and sound banking practices.

Its regulatory responsibilities include:

  • Licensing and regulation of banks under applicable laws.
  • Prescribing prudential norms relating to capital adequacy, income recognition, asset classification and provisioning.
  • Supervising banks through inspections, reporting requirements and off-site surveillance.
  • Issuing directions to banks in the public interest and in the interest of banking policy.
  • Taking corrective and enforcement action where required by law.
  • Promoting financial inclusion and responsible banking practices.

The RBI's powers over banking companies are primarily exercised under the Banking Regulation Act, 1949, along with relevant provisions of the RBI Act and other laws.

8. Foreign Exchange Management

The RBI is responsible for managing foreign exchange reserves and administering important provisions of the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 (FEMA).

  • Maintains and manages India's foreign exchange reserves.
  • Promotes orderly development and functioning of the foreign exchange market.
  • Regulates authorised persons dealing in foreign exchange under FEMA.
  • Facilitates external payments and cross-border transactions in accordance with the law.
  • Monitors external sector developments and manages exchange market volatility through appropriate measures.

India follows a managed floating exchange rate system. The RBI may intervene in the foreign exchange market to address excessive volatility and maintain orderly market conditions.

9. Financial Inclusion and Developmental Functions

Along with its regulatory and monetary responsibilities, the RBI undertakes developmental initiatives to improve access to formal financial services.

  • Promotes banking access in rural and underserved areas.
  • Encourages financial literacy and consumer awareness.
  • Supports priority sector lending through regulatory guidelines.
  • Promotes responsible digital payments and financial technology.
  • Encourages customer protection and fair practices in financial services.
  • Supports the development of financial markets and institutions.

10. Payment and Settlement Systems

The RBI regulates and supervises payment systems under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007.

Its responsibilities include authorising payment system operators, prescribing standards, supervising system safety and promoting efficient and secure payment infrastructure.

Important payment systems and infrastructure in India include:

  • Real Time Gross Settlement (RTGS).
  • National Electronic Funds Transfer (NEFT).
  • Unified Payments Interface (UPI), operated by the National Payments Corporation of India under the applicable regulatory framework.
  • National Automated Clearing House (NACH).
  • Other authorised electronic payment and settlement systems.

11. Regulation of Non-Banking Financial Companies

The RBI regulates specified categories of Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs) under Chapter III-B of the RBI Act, 1934.

  • Section 45-IA provides for registration requirements for NBFCs covered by the provision.
  • The RBI prescribes prudential and governance requirements for regulated NBFCs.
  • It supervises their activities and may issue directions under the Act.
  • It may take regulatory action where an NBFC violates applicable requirements.

NBFCs provide financial services such as lending, investment and asset financing. They are not identical to banks and cannot accept demand deposits in the manner of banks.

12. Autonomy and Accountability of the RBI

The RBI is a statutory institution with operational responsibilities in monetary policy and financial regulation. Its institutional framework combines professional decision-making with statutory accountability and government oversight.

  • The RBI is established by an Act of Parliament.
  • The Central Government appoints the Governor and other members of the Central Board as provided by law.
  • Section 7 of the RBI Act empowers the Central Government to issue directions to the RBI in the public interest after consultation with the Governor.
  • The Monetary Policy Committee operates under the statutory framework and is accountable for its policy decisions.
  • The RBI submits reports and information to the Central Government as required under law.

Thus, the RBI has functional and operational responsibilities, but it is not constitutionally independent of the government.

14. Challenges and Contemporary Issues

  • Inflation management: Balancing price stability with economic growth.
  • Financial stability: Addressing risks arising from credit cycles, asset quality and interconnected financial institutions.
  • Digital finance: Managing cybersecurity, fraud, data protection and operational risks in digital payments.
  • Financial inclusion: Extending affordable and reliable financial services to underserved communities.
  • Global economic shocks: Managing exchange rate pressures and external financial volatility.
  • Regulatory coordination: Maintaining effective coordination with other financial regulators and government institutions.

15. Significance for Indian Polity and Governance

The RBI is an important institution in India's economic governance framework. Although it is not a constitutional body, its statutory powers influence monetary stability, banking regulation, public finance and the functioning of financial markets.

Its role illustrates the importance of specialised statutory institutions in implementing public policy and maintaining institutional stability in a modern economy.

16. Prelims Facts

  • The RBI was established on 1 April 1935.
  • The RBI was nationalised on 1 January 1949.
  • The RBI Act was enacted in 1934.
  • The RBI has its headquarters in Mumbai.
  • The RBI has the sole right to issue banknotes, except the one-rupee note.
  • The Monetary Policy Committee has six members.
  • The RBI regulates payment systems under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007.
  • The RBI administers important provisions of FEMA, 1999.
  • The RBI regulates specified NBFCs under the RBI Act, 1934.
  • The RBI is a statutory body, not a constitutional body.
