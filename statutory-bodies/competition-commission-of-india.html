Statutory Bodies

Competition Commission of India (CCI)

The Competition Commission of India is the statutory authority responsible for enforcing competition law, preventing anti-competitive practices and promoting fair competition in Indian markets.

1. Introduction

The Competition Commission of India (CCI) is a statutory body established under the Competition Act, 2002. It seeks to protect the process of competition in markets and safeguard consumer interests.

  • Legal basis: Competition Act, 2002.
  • Established: 2003 under the Act; became operational in 2009.
  • Headquarters: New Delhi.
  • Nature: Statutory quasi-judicial and regulatory authority.
  • Administrative ministry: Ministry of Corporate Affairs.

The Competition Act replaced the earlier Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices (MRTP) Act, 1969. The new framework shifted the focus from controlling monopolies as such to preventing conduct that adversely affects competition.

2. Objectives of CCI

The Competition Act, 2002, provides three central objectives:

  • Prevent practices having an adverse effect on competition.
  • Promote and sustain competition in markets.
  • Protect the interests of consumers.
  • Ensure freedom of trade carried on by other participants in Indian markets.

CCI seeks to create a competitive environment in which businesses compete on merit, consumers have meaningful choices and market power is not misused.

3. Composition of CCI

Section 8 of the Competition Act, 2002, provides for the composition of the Commission.

  • CCI consists of a Chairperson and not less than two and not more than six other members.
  • The Chairperson and members are appointed by the Central Government.
  • They are selected from persons of ability, integrity and standing who have special knowledge and professional experience in specified fields such as economics, law, finance, commerce, management, industry and public affairs.
  • The Commission functions as a collective statutory authority.

The Act provides for the appointment, term, service conditions and removal of the Chairperson and members.

4. Regulation of Anti-Competitive Agreements

Section 3 of the Competition Act prohibits agreements that cause or are likely to cause an appreciable adverse effect on competition (AAEC) in India.

Anti-competitive agreements may include:

A. Horizontal Agreements

These are agreements between enterprises or persons engaged in similar or identical trade. Examples include:

  • Price fixing.
  • Limiting or controlling production, supply or technical development.
  • Market or customer allocation.
  • Bid rigging and collusive bidding.

Such arrangements are commonly associated with cartels.

B. Vertical Agreements

These are agreements between enterprises operating at different stages or levels of the production or distribution chain. Examples include:

  • Tie-in arrangements.
  • Exclusive supply agreements.
  • Exclusive distribution agreements.
  • Refusal to deal.
  • Resale price maintenance.

Vertical agreements are assessed under the statutory test of appreciable adverse effect on competition.

5. Abuse of Dominant Position

Section 4 prohibits the abuse of a dominant position by an enterprise or group.

Dominance itself is not prohibited. The law prohibits its abuse.

Abusive conduct may include:

  • Imposing unfair or discriminatory conditions in the purchase or sale of goods or services.
  • Imposing unfair or discriminatory prices, including predatory pricing.
  • Limiting or restricting production, markets or technical development.
  • Denial of market access.
  • Making contracts subject to unrelated supplementary obligations.
  • Using dominance in one relevant market to enter into or protect another relevant market.

Dominance is assessed with reference to factors such as market share, size and resources, economic power, entry barriers, dependence of consumers and market structure.

6. Regulation of Combinations

Section 5 defines combinations, which broadly include certain acquisitions, mergers and amalgamations that cross prescribed asset or turnover thresholds.

Section 6 regulates combinations that cause or are likely to cause an appreciable adverse effect on competition in the relevant market in India.

CCI examines combinations to determine whether they are likely to:

  • Create or strengthen market power.
  • Reduce competition significantly.
  • Raise barriers to entry.
  • Harm consumer welfare through reduced choice or higher prices.
  • Facilitate coordinated conduct among market participants.

The Commission may approve a combination, approve it subject to modifications or prohibit it, in accordance with the Act.

The Competition (Amendment) Act, 2023 introduced significant changes to the combination-control framework, including a deal-value threshold for certain transactions and a shorter statutory review timeline.

7. Functions of CCI

  • Eliminates practices having an adverse effect on competition.
  • Promotes and sustains competition in markets.
  • Protects consumer interests.
  • Ensures freedom of trade in Indian markets.
  • Inquires into anti-competitive agreements and abuse of dominant position.
  • Reviews combinations such as mergers and acquisitions.
  • Undertakes competition advocacy.
  • Advises the Central Government or State Governments on competition-related matters when required.

8. Powers of CCI

CCI has investigative, regulatory and adjudicatory powers under the Competition Act.

Investigative Powers

  • Directs the Director General to investigate alleged contraventions.
  • Calls for information and documents.
  • Examines relevant persons and records through the statutory process.

Adjudicatory and Regulatory Powers

  • Passes orders in cases involving anti-competitive agreements and abuse of dominance.
  • Imposes penalties as authorised by the Act.
  • Orders modification or discontinuance of anti-competitive conduct.
  • Reviews combinations and may impose appropriate conditions or modifications.
  • Issues interim orders in circumstances provided by the Act.

CCI's orders are subject to appellate review under the statutory framework.

9. Director General

The Director General (DG) is the investigative arm associated with CCI.

  • CCI may direct the DG to investigate an alleged contravention of the Competition Act.
  • The DG conducts investigations and gathers relevant evidence.
  • The DG submits an investigation report to the Commission.
  • The report assists CCI in deciding the matter; the final decision is made by the Commission.

The DG's investigative role should be distinguished from CCI's adjudicatory role.

10. Initiation of Inquiry

Under Section 19, CCI may inquire into alleged contraventions relating to anti-competitive agreements and abuse of dominant position.

An inquiry may be initiated:

  • On its own motion (suo motu).
  • On receipt of information from a person, consumer or their association or trade association.
  • On a reference made by the Central Government, a State Government or a statutory authority.

CCI applies the legal tests and procedures prescribed under the Act before passing an order.

11. Competition Advocacy

Competition advocacy refers to activities aimed at promoting a competitive culture and encouraging policies that support fair competition.

CCI undertakes advocacy through:

  • Awareness programmes for businesses, consumers and public authorities.
  • Training and capacity-building activities.
  • Research and publications on competition issues.
  • Advice to governments on the competition implications of policies and laws.
  • Engagement with stakeholders and market participants.

Section 49 provides for competition advocacy and references from governments and statutory authorities.

12. Appellate Mechanism

Orders passed by CCI may be appealed to the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT), subject to the conditions and limitations prescribed by the Competition Act.

Further appeals from NCLAT lie to the Supreme Court of India under Section 53T of the Act, subject to the statutory requirements.

The appellate structure provides judicial oversight over decisions of the Commission.

13. Competition (Amendment) Act, 2023

The Competition (Amendment) Act, 2023 introduced several changes to modernise competition regulation and address evolving market practices.

Important changes include:

  • Introduction of a deal-value threshold for certain combinations.
  • Changes to the timelines and procedures for combination review.
  • Introduction of a framework for settlement and commitment in specified cases.
  • Recognition of certain forms of anti-competitive conduct involving facilitators of cartels.
  • Changes to penalty determination and enforcement provisions.
  • Provision for a leniency-plus framework in cartel investigations.

The amendment seeks to improve enforcement while responding to developments in digital and technology-driven markets.

14. Competition in Digital Markets

Digital markets have created new competition challenges because of network effects, data advantages, platform dependence and economies of scale.

Issues that may arise include:

  • Preferential treatment of a platform's own products or services.
  • Restrictions on business users and competing platforms.
  • Data-related barriers to entry.
  • Predatory pricing and exclusionary strategies.
  • Concentration of market power in platform-based ecosystems.

CCI applies the Competition Act to conduct within its jurisdiction. The Competition (Amendment) Act, 2023 also strengthened aspects of the enforcement framework. Proposals for additional ex-ante regulation of large digital enterprises should be distinguished from the existing provisions of the Competition Act.

15. Importance of CCI

  • Consumer welfare: Promotes competitive prices, quality and choice.
  • Economic efficiency: Encourages efficient allocation of resources.
  • Business environment: Supports fair opportunities for enterprises, including new entrants.
  • Innovation: Encourages firms to innovate and improve products and services.
  • Market integrity: Discourages cartels and abusive market conduct.
  • Investment: Supports a predictable and competitive market environment.

16. Challenges Before CCI

  • Digital concentration: Addressing competition concerns in platform-based markets.
  • Complex investigations: Investigating sophisticated commercial arrangements and data-driven practices.
  • Regulatory capacity: Building expertise in economics, technology and digital business models.
  • Timely enforcement: Ensuring that investigations and decisions are completed efficiently.
  • Regulatory coordination: Coordinating with sectoral regulators where jurisdictions overlap.
  • Balancing interests: Protecting competition without discouraging legitimate business growth and innovation.

18. CCI and Sectoral Regulators

CCI is a general competition regulator. Sector-specific regulators oversee particular industries under their respective laws.

Authority Primary Area
CCI Competition across markets and sectors.
TRAI Telecommunications and broadcasting regulation.
RBI Monetary policy, banking and financial regulation within its statutory mandate.
SEBI Securities markets and investor protection.
IRDAI Insurance sector regulation.

Where competition issues arise in a regulated sector, the relevant statutory mandates and coordination mechanisms become important.

19. Prelims Facts

  • CCI is established under the Competition Act, 2002.
  • It became operational in 2009.
  • It replaced the competition-control framework of the MRTP Act, 1969.
  • Section 3 deals with anti-competitive agreements.
  • Section 4 deals with abuse of dominant position.
  • Sections 5 and 6 deal with combinations.
  • The Director General investigates matters when directed by CCI.
  • NCLAT is the appellate forum for CCI orders under the Act.
  • Further appeals lie to the Supreme Court under Section 53T.
  • CCI is a statutory body, not a constitutional body.

20. Mains Perspective

Possible Question

Examine the role of the Competition Commission of India in promoting fair competition and protecting consumer interests in the Indian economy. Discuss the challenges posed by digital markets.

Answer Framework

  1. Introduction: Explain the statutory basis and objectives of CCI.
  2. Regulatory role: Discuss action against anti-competitive agreements, abuse of dominance and harmful combinations.
  3. Consumer welfare: Explain the importance of choice, quality, fair prices and market access.
  4. Digital markets: Examine network effects, data concentration and platform power.
  5. Challenges: Discuss investigative capacity, coordination and timely enforcement.
  6. Conclusion: Emphasise effective, transparent and innovation-sensitive competition regulation.
