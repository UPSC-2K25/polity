Non-Governmental Organisations and Self-Help Groups

Non-Governmental Organisations (NGOs) and Self-Help Groups (SHGs) are important institutions of civil society. They promote community participation, social development, empowerment and delivery of services, particularly among disadvantaged sections.

1. Non-Governmental Organisations (NGOs)

NGOs are non-state, non-profit organisations that work independently of government structures to pursue social, developmental, humanitarian, environmental or rights-based objectives.

They may operate at village, district, State, national or international levels. Their activities include service delivery, awareness generation, research, advocacy, capacity building and community mobilisation.

2. Characteristics of NGOs

  • They are organisationally separate from government.
  • They generally do not distribute profits to owners or members.
  • They pursue defined social, developmental or public-interest objectives.
  • They may depend on donations, grants, membership contributions or project funding.
  • They often work closely with local communities.
  • They may collaborate with government agencies, businesses and international organisations.
  • They vary considerably in size, structure, capacity and area of work.

3. Types and Areas of NGO Activity

  • Development organisations: Work on poverty reduction, livelihoods, rural development and community infrastructure.
  • Humanitarian organisations: Provide relief and rehabilitation during disasters and emergencies.
  • Rights-based organisations: Promote human rights, legal awareness, social justice and access to entitlements.
  • Environmental organisations: Work on conservation, pollution control, biodiversity and sustainable development.
  • Health and education organisations: Support public health, nutrition, literacy and access to education.
  • Research and advocacy organisations: Undertake studies, monitor public programmes and recommend policy changes.

4. Legal Forms and Regulation of NGOs in India

NGOs in India may be organised under different legal frameworks, depending on their objectives, structure and area of operation.

Legal framework Key features
Societies Registration Act, 1860 Provides for registration of societies formed for charitable, literary, scientific and other specified purposes. State amendments and State-specific laws may apply.
Indian Trusts Act, 1882 and State public trust laws Trust structures may be used for charitable and public-benefit activities. Public charitable trusts are also governed by applicable State laws.
Companies Act, 2013 — Section 8 Permits incorporation of companies for specified charitable and other not-for-profit objects, subject to statutory conditions.

Organisations receiving foreign contributions are regulated under the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, 2010 (FCRA), as amended. Registration or prior permission is required where applicable before receiving foreign contributions under the Act.

NGOs may also be subject to tax laws, labour laws, financial reporting requirements and other applicable regulatory provisions.

5. Role of NGOs in Governance

  1. Service delivery: Supplement public efforts in health, education, sanitation, nutrition and livelihood support.
  2. Community mobilisation: Organise communities and encourage participation in local development.
  3. Awareness generation: Inform citizens about rights, entitlements and government programmes.
  4. Social accountability: Facilitate social audits, community monitoring and public hearings.
  5. Policy advocacy: Represent community concerns and provide policy inputs.
  6. Innovation: Develop and test locally appropriate solutions to social problems.
  7. Disaster response: Assist with relief, rehabilitation and community recovery.
  8. Inclusion: Support groups that face barriers in accessing public services and opportunities.

6. Challenges Faced by NGOs

  • Dependence on uncertain or short-term funding.
  • Limited professional capacity and trained personnel.
  • Weak internal governance and accountability in some organisations.
  • Concerns about transparency in the use of funds.
  • Regulatory complexity and compliance burdens.
  • Difficulty reaching remote and marginalised communities.
  • Risk of donor-driven priorities replacing local needs.
  • Coordination gaps with government agencies and other organisations.
  • Possibility of misuse of charitable status or funds.

7. Measures to Strengthen NGOs

  • Promote transparent accounting and timely statutory disclosures.
  • Strengthen boards, internal controls and organisational governance.
  • Build professional capacity in project management and evaluation.
  • Encourage community participation in planning and monitoring.
  • Improve coordination with local governments and public institutions.
  • Promote diversified and sustainable funding sources.
  • Ensure that regulation is fair, predictable and proportionate.
  • Evaluate outcomes and social impact rather than relying only on expenditure.

8. Self-Help Groups (SHGs)

A Self-Help Group is a small, voluntary association of people, usually from a similar socio-economic background, who come together to save regularly, pool resources and support one another through collective financial and social action.

In India, SHGs are widely associated with women's empowerment, financial inclusion, livelihood promotion and community development. Many SHGs consist of around 10–20 members, although membership and operating arrangements may vary across programmes and contexts.

9. Principles and Features of SHGs

  • Voluntary membership and collective participation.
  • Regular savings by members.
  • Collective decision-making and mutual trust.
  • Internal lending from pooled savings.
  • Repayment discipline and transparent record-keeping.
  • Peer support and shared responsibility.
  • Focus on financial inclusion and improved livelihoods.
  • Development of leadership and collective bargaining capacity.

10. Working of an SHG

  1. Formation: Members organise voluntarily around shared interests and establish group rules.
  2. Savings: Members contribute a fixed amount at regular intervals.
  3. Internal lending: The pooled savings are lent to members according to agreed rules and priorities.
  4. Repayment: Members repay loans according to agreed schedules.
  5. Bank linkage: Eligible groups may access formal credit and banking services.
  6. Livelihood activities: Members may undertake individual or collective income-generating activities.
  7. Federation and support: Groups may join village-level or higher federations for training, support and collective action.

11. SHG-Bank Linkage Programme

The SHG-Bank Linkage Programme was initiated by NABARD in 1992 to connect informal savings groups with the formal banking system. It has become an important mechanism for expanding financial inclusion, particularly among rural women.

Under the programme, eligible SHGs can open savings accounts and obtain credit from banks based on group performance and applicable lending norms. The programme reduces dependence on informal moneylenders and supports access to institutional finance.

12. National Rural Livelihoods Mission

The Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana–National Rural Livelihoods Mission (DAY-NRLM) is a major government programme for organising rural poor households, particularly women, into SHGs and their federations.

Its major objectives include:

  • Mobilising rural poor households into community institutions.
  • Promoting access to formal financial services.
  • Supporting sustainable livelihoods and self-employment.
  • Building skills, capacity and community leadership.
  • Strengthening federations and community-based support systems.
  • Improving access to public services and social protection.

13. Role of SHGs in Women's Empowerment

  • Economic empowerment: Savings, credit and livelihood activities improve financial independence.
  • Social empowerment: Collective action can challenge discriminatory practices and social isolation.
  • Political participation: Members gain confidence and may participate more actively in Gram Sabhas and local governance.
  • Leadership: Group activities create opportunities to develop organisational and decision-making skills.
  • Access to services: Groups can help members access health, nutrition, education and welfare programmes.
  • Collective bargaining: Members can negotiate more effectively with banks, markets and public institutions.

14. Other Important Initiatives Supporting SHGs

  • DAY-NRLM: Promotes rural livelihoods through SHGs and their federations.
  • DAY-NULM: Supports urban livelihoods and the organisation of urban poor communities, including SHGs.
  • Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana and livelihood programmes: Provide institutional support for skill development, financial inclusion and self-employment.
  • Bank linkage: Connects eligible groups with formal savings and credit facilities.
  • Producer groups and collectives: Help members aggregate products, improve market access and strengthen value chains.

15. Challenges Faced by SHGs

  • Limited access to adequate and timely institutional credit.
  • Insufficient financial literacy and business skills.
  • Weak bookkeeping and record management.
  • Unequal participation and dominance by a few members.
  • Limited access to markets, technology and infrastructure.
  • Inadequate training and continuing institutional support.
  • Risk of over-indebtedness when credit is poorly managed.
  • Social barriers that restrict women's mobility and decision-making.
  • Difficulty sustaining collective enterprises and expanding beyond small activities.

16. Measures to Strengthen SHGs

  1. Improve access to affordable and timely bank credit.
  2. Provide regular training in financial literacy, bookkeeping and enterprise management.
  3. Strengthen community resource persons and SHG federations.
  4. Improve market linkages, branding, packaging and digital access.
  5. Promote value addition and diversified livelihood activities.
  6. Encourage transparent group records and democratic decision-making.
  7. Provide access to technology, infrastructure and business support.
  8. Integrate SHGs with local planning and public service delivery.
  9. Prevent over-indebtedness through responsible lending and financial counselling.

17. NGOs and SHGs: Comparison

Basis NGOs SHGs
Nature Formal or informal non-governmental organisations working for social or public-interest objectives. Small voluntary groups formed around mutual support, savings and collective action.
Membership May include professionals, volunteers, members and community representatives. Usually people from a similar socio-economic background or community.
Primary focus Development, service delivery, advocacy, research or rights protection. Savings, credit, livelihoods and member empowerment.
Scale May operate at local, national or international levels. Generally operates at the community level, often linked through federations.
Funding May receive donations, grants, project funds and other lawful resources. Primarily based on member savings, internal lending and institutional credit.
Accountability To governing bodies, members, donors, regulators and beneficiaries, as applicable. Primarily to group members through collective decision-making and agreed rules.

18. Role in Inclusive and Participatory Governance

NGOs and SHGs can strengthen the relationship between citizens and public institutions. NGOs contribute through expertise, advocacy, service delivery and independent monitoring. SHGs encourage collective self-reliance, financial inclusion and participation in local development.

Their contribution is strongest when they are transparent, accountable to communities and coordinated with public institutions without compromising their independence.

19. Key Takeaways

  • NGOs are non-state organisations working for social, developmental and public-interest objectives.
  • In India, NGOs may be organised as societies, trusts or Section 8 companies.
  • FCRA regulates the receipt and use of foreign contributions by covered organisations.
  • SHGs are small voluntary groups built around savings, internal lending and mutual support.
  • The SHG-Bank Linkage Programme, initiated by NABARD in 1992, connects groups with formal finance.
  • DAY-NRLM promotes rural women's SHGs, federations and livelihoods.
  • Both NGOs and SHGs can advance inclusion, participation and accountability.
