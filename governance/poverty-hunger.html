Poverty and Hunger

Poverty and hunger are multidimensional development challenges that affect human dignity, equality and social justice. Their eradication requires livelihood opportunities, food and nutrition security, access to essential services and effective social protection.

1. Meaning of Poverty

Poverty refers to a condition in which individuals or households lack sufficient resources and capabilities to attain an acceptable standard of living. It includes not only inadequate income but also deprivation in health, education, housing, sanitation and access to essential services.

Types of Poverty

  • Absolute poverty: Inability to meet minimum basic needs such as food, shelter and clothing.
  • Relative poverty: Deprivation measured in relation to the living standards and income distribution of a society.
  • Multidimensional poverty: Deprivation across several dimensions, including health, education and living standards.
  • Rural poverty: Poverty associated with agrarian distress, limited non-farm employment and inadequate rural infrastructure.
  • Urban poverty: Poverty associated with insecure employment, inadequate housing, high living costs and limited access to services.

2. Meaning and Dimensions of Hunger

Hunger refers to the physical condition resulting from inadequate access to food. Food insecurity occurs when people do not have reliable access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food.

  • Undernourishment: Insufficient habitual dietary energy intake.
  • Undernutrition: Inadequate nutrition that may manifest as wasting, stunting or underweight.
  • Micronutrient deficiency: Lack of essential vitamins and minerals, sometimes described as hidden hunger.
  • Food insecurity: Uncertain or inadequate access to food because of economic, physical or social constraints.
  • Malnutrition: A broader condition that includes undernutrition, micronutrient deficiencies and overnutrition.

3. Causes of Poverty and Hunger

Economic Causes

  • Unemployment, underemployment and low wages.
  • Unequal distribution of income and productive assets.
  • Low agricultural productivity and insecure farm incomes.
  • Limited access to credit, markets and productive resources.
  • Economic shocks, inflation and loss of livelihoods.

Social Causes

  • Caste-based exclusion and social discrimination.
  • Gender inequality and unequal access to resources.
  • Low levels of education and skills.
  • Population vulnerability and lack of social protection.
  • Inadequate access to healthcare and sanitation.

Structural and Environmental Causes

  • Unequal land ownership and landlessness.
  • Regional imbalance and inadequate infrastructure.
  • Droughts, floods, climate change and crop failure.
  • Displacement, conflict and migration-related insecurity.
  • Weak implementation of welfare programmes.

4. Poverty Measurement in India

Poverty measurement helps identify deprivation, estimate its extent and guide public policy. Different methods capture different aspects of poverty.

A. Consumption Expenditure Approach

Traditional poverty estimation in India has relied significantly on household consumption expenditure. A poverty line represents a minimum expenditure level used to identify people unable to meet specified basic needs.

B. Committees on Poverty Estimation

Committee / Group Major contribution
Alagh Task Force (1979) Developed poverty lines based on calorie requirements and consumption expenditure.
Lakdawala Expert Group (1993) Recommended state-specific poverty lines and use of consumer price indices for updating them.
Tendulkar Committee (2009) Recommended a revised methodology that moved away from a strict calorie-based poverty line and incorporated broader consumption requirements.
Rangarajan Committee (2014) Suggested a different poverty-line methodology based on normative food and non-food expenditure requirements.

These committees used different assumptions and methodologies. Their poverty estimates are therefore not directly interchangeable.

C. Multidimensional Poverty Index

The Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) identifies overlapping deprivations experienced by households. The global MPI developed by UNDP and OPHI considers health, education and living standards.

India's National Multidimensional Poverty Index uses a broader set of indicators across these dimensions to assess deprivation and inform policy.

  • Health: Indicators relating to nutrition, child and adolescent mortality and maternal health.
  • Education: Indicators relating to years of schooling and school attendance.
  • Living standards: Indicators covering housing, sanitation, drinking water, electricity, cooking fuel, assets and banking access.

MPI complements income or consumption measures; it does not replace every poverty measure or capture all forms of deprivation.

5. Food Security in India

Food security exists when all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food that meets their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life.

Four Dimensions of Food Security

  • Availability: Adequate food production, stocks and supply.
  • Access: Physical and economic ability to obtain food.
  • Utilisation: Proper nutrition, food safety, clean water and healthcare to enable the body to use food effectively.
  • Stability: Consistent availability and access over time, including during shocks and emergencies.

6. Constitutional and Legal Framework

Constitutional Provisions

  • Article 21: Protection of life and personal liberty, interpreted by the Supreme Court to include aspects of dignified living.
  • Article 38: Promotion of welfare and reduction of inequalities.
  • Article 39(a): Adequate means of livelihood for men and women equally.
  • Article 39(b): Distribution of material resources to serve the common good.
  • Article 41: Public assistance in specified circumstances within the State's economic capacity.
  • Article 43: Living wage and decent conditions of life for workers.
  • Article 47: Improvement of nutrition and standard of living and public health.

National Food Security Act, 2013

The National Food Security Act provides a rights-based framework for food and nutritional security. It covers eligible households under the Targeted Public Distribution System and provides nutritional entitlements for specified groups.

  • Coverage of up to 75% of the rural population and 50% of the urban population, subject to applicable identification and coverage rules.
  • Priority households receive 5 kg of foodgrains per person per month under the statutory entitlement.
  • Antyodaya Anna Yojana households are entitled to 35 kg of foodgrains per household per month.
  • Provides nutritional support for eligible pregnant women and lactating mothers, subject to statutory provisions and applicable scheme arrangements.
  • Provides nutritional support for children through prescribed programmes.
  • Includes provisions relating to grievance redressal, transparency and accountability.

The Act also provides for food security allowance in specified circumstances when entitled foodgrains or meals are not supplied.

8. Public Distribution System

The Public Distribution System is a major instrument of food security in India. It distributes essential foodgrains to eligible households through a network of fair price shops.

Key Features

  • Central and State Governments share responsibilities for procurement, allocation, distribution and implementation.
  • The Food Corporation of India plays a major role in procurement, storage and movement of foodgrains.
  • State Governments identify eligible households and oversee fair price shop operations.
  • Digitisation and portability initiatives seek to improve access and transparency.

One Nation One Ration Card

The One Nation One Ration Card system enables eligible ration-card holders to access their foodgrain entitlements from participating fair price shops across States and Union Territories. It is particularly relevant for migrant workers and mobile households.

Challenges in PDS

  • Inclusion and exclusion errors in beneficiary identification.
  • Leakages, diversion and irregularities in distribution.
  • Storage losses and supply-chain inefficiencies.
  • Uneven quality and availability of foodgrains.
  • Authentication and connectivity problems in technology-enabled delivery.
  • Limited dietary diversity in foodgrain-based entitlements.

9. Poverty Alleviation Strategies

A. Employment and Livelihoods

  • Promote labour-intensive and inclusive economic growth.
  • Expand rural non-farm and urban employment opportunities.
  • Improve agricultural productivity and farmer incomes.
  • Strengthen skills, apprenticeships and vocational education.
  • Support micro-enterprises and self-help groups.

B. Human Capital Development

  • Improve access to quality education and healthcare.
  • Strengthen maternal and child nutrition.
  • Expand sanitation, safe drinking water and housing.
  • Reduce barriers faced by disadvantaged groups.

C. Social Protection

  • Improve coverage and adequacy of pensions and social assistance.
  • Strengthen food security and nutrition programmes.
  • Provide protection against livelihood and health-related shocks.
  • Ensure timely and accessible benefit delivery.

10. Challenges in Eradicating Poverty and Hunger

  • Jobless or low-quality growth: Economic growth may not generate sufficient secure employment.
  • Informal employment: Many workers lack stable incomes and social security.
  • Agrarian distress: Small landholdings, climate risks and price uncertainty affect rural livelihoods.
  • Regional inequality: Poverty and service access vary significantly across regions.
  • Gender inequality: Unequal control over assets, income and opportunities increases vulnerability.
  • Urban deprivation: Informal settlements, insecure work and high living costs affect urban households.
  • Climate change: Extreme weather can disrupt food production and livelihoods.
  • Programme fragmentation: Poor coordination can weaken the impact of welfare interventions.
  • Nutrition quality: Food availability alone does not ensure a balanced and nutritious diet.

11. Way Forward

  1. Promote broad-based growth that creates productive and decent employment.
  2. Strengthen public health, education and nutrition systems.
  3. Improve agricultural productivity, irrigation, storage and market access.
  4. Ensure effective implementation of food security legislation.
  5. Improve targeting while preventing exclusion of eligible beneficiaries.
  6. Strengthen social security for informal and vulnerable workers.
  7. Promote women's economic participation and control over resources.
  8. Improve urban housing, basic services and livelihood support.
  9. Build climate-resilient agriculture and social protection systems.
  10. Use reliable data, independent evaluation and social audits to improve outcomes.

12. Key Takeaways

  • Poverty is multidimensional and includes income, health, education and living-standard deprivation.
  • Hunger and malnutrition are related but distinct concepts.
  • India has used different poverty-estimation methodologies, including consumption-based and multidimensional approaches.
  • The Constitution provides a foundation for livelihood, welfare, nutrition and social justice.
  • The National Food Security Act, 2013 establishes statutory food and nutrition entitlements.
  • PDS, employment programmes and social protection measures are important instruments of poverty reduction.
  • Long-term progress requires employment, human development, inclusion and accountable institutions.
