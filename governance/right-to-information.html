Right to Information (RTI)

The Right to Information enables citizens to access information held by public authorities. It promotes transparency, strengthens accountability, reduces secrecy in administration and allows citizens to participate more effectively in democratic governance.

1. Meaning and Significance

The Right to Information is the legal right of citizens to obtain information from public authorities, subject to specified exemptions and procedural requirements. It recognises that citizens need information to understand government decisions, monitor public expenditure and hold institutions accountable.

Access to information is particularly important in a representative democracy because public authority is exercised in the name of the people. The RTI framework helps reduce the gap between government institutions and citizens.

2. Constitutional Basis

The Constitution does not expressly use the phrase "Right to Information". However, the Supreme Court has recognised the right to know as an aspect of freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a), subject to constitutional limitations.

  • Article 19(1)(a): Protects freedom of speech and expression. Access to information about public affairs supports informed expression and democratic debate.
  • Article 21: The right to life and personal liberty has been interpreted broadly, including access to information in certain contexts affecting life and liberty.
  • Article 14: Equality before law and protection against arbitrary state action reinforce the demand for transparent administration.

The RTI Act, 2005 gives statutory effect to the right of citizens to obtain information from public authorities.

3. Evolution of the RTI Movement in India

  • Grassroots campaigns: People's movements in Rajasthan, particularly the Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan (MKSS), demanded access to muster rolls, bills and public expenditure records.
  • Public hearings: Jan Sunwais helped expose discrepancies in development expenditure and demonstrated the importance of community access to official records.
  • State-level laws: Several States enacted right-to-information laws before the national legislation.
  • Freedom of Information Act, 2002: Parliament enacted an earlier law, but it was not brought into operation.
  • Right to Information Act, 2005: The comprehensive national law replaced the earlier framework and came into force on 12 October 2005.

4. Objectives of the RTI Act, 2005

  1. Promote transparency and accountability in public authorities.
  2. Enable citizens to access information held by government institutions.
  3. Reduce corruption and misuse of public resources.
  4. Improve administrative responsiveness and public service delivery.
  5. Strengthen informed participation in democratic processes.
  6. Establish an independent statutory appeal and complaint mechanism.

5. Important Definitions and Scope

Information

Under Section 2(f), information includes material in any form, such as records, documents, memos, emails, opinions, advice, press releases, circulars, orders, logbooks, contracts, reports, papers, samples, models and data held in electronic form.

Right to Information

Section 2(j) defines the right to information to include inspection of work, documents and records; taking notes, extracts or certified copies; taking certified samples of material; and obtaining information in electronic form or through printouts where it is stored in a computer or other device.

Public Authority

Section 2(h) covers authorities, bodies and institutions of self-government established or constituted by the Constitution, by legislation, or by government notification or order. It also covers bodies owned, controlled or substantially financed by government and qualifying non-government organisations substantially financed by government.

Who Can Apply?

Under Section 3, all citizens have the right to information. The statutory right is available to citizens of India; it is not a general application right for foreign nationals.

7. Proactive Disclosure: Section 4

Section 4 requires every public authority to maintain records properly and publish specified categories of information on its own initiative. This is known as suo motu or proactive disclosure.

Important categories include:

  • Particulars of the organisation, functions and duties.
  • Powers and duties of officers and employees.
  • Decision-making procedures and channels of supervision.
  • Rules, regulations, instructions and manuals used by employees.
  • Categories of documents held by the authority.
  • Budget allocations and expenditure details.
  • Subsidy programmes and details of beneficiaries.
  • Names and particulars of Public Information Officers.

Effective implementation of Section 4 can reduce the need for individual applications and improve public access to routine government information.

8. Procedure for Seeking Information

  1. An applicant submits a written or electronic request in English, Hindi or the official language of the area to the concerned Public Information Officer or Assistant Public Information Officer.
  2. The applicant is not required to provide reasons for seeking the information.
  3. If the request concerns another public authority, it should be transferred under Section 6(3), normally within five days of receipt.
  4. The Public Information Officer processes the request and provides the information or communicates the applicable grounds for rejection.
  5. Where the applicant is dissatisfied or receives no response within the prescribed period, an appeal may be filed under Section 19.

If the public authority fails to provide information within the time prescribed under Section 7(1), the information is to be provided free of charge under Section 7(6).

10. Exemptions from Disclosure: Section 8

The right to information is not absolute. Section 8 protects specified categories of information where disclosure may affect important public interests or protected interests of individuals and institutions.

  • Information whose disclosure would prejudicially affect the sovereignty and integrity of India, security, strategic, scientific or economic interests of the State, relations with foreign States or lead to incitement of an offence.
  • Information expressly forbidden to be published by a court or tribunal, or disclosure of which may constitute contempt of court.
  • Information whose disclosure would cause a breach of privilege of Parliament or a State Legislature.
  • Commercial confidence, trade secrets or intellectual property where disclosure would harm the competitive position of a third party, unless larger public interest warrants disclosure.
  • Information available to a person in their fiduciary relationship, unless larger public interest warrants disclosure.
  • Information received in confidence from a foreign government.
  • Information whose disclosure would endanger the life or physical safety of a person or identify a confidential source of information or assistance.
  • Information that would impede the process of investigation or apprehension or prosecution of offenders.
  • Cabinet papers, including records of deliberations of the Council of Ministers, Secretaries and other officers, subject to the statutory conditions for disclosure after a decision is taken and the matter is complete or over.
  • Personal information protected under the applicable statutory framework.

Section 8(2) provides for disclosure where public interest in disclosure outweighs the harm to protected interests, subject to the statutory framework. The Act also contains a provision concerning information relating to events or matters that occurred twenty years before the request, subject to specified exceptions.

11. Third-Party Information: Section 11

Where a Public Information Officer intends to disclose information relating to or supplied by a third party and treated as confidential by that third party, the prescribed consultation procedure may apply.

  • The third party is given an opportunity to make a submission.
  • The submission is considered while deciding the request.
  • The Public Information Officer makes the disclosure decision according to the Act.
  • The third party may exercise the appeal rights provided by the Act.

Consultation does not give the third party an automatic veto over disclosure. The decision must be made in accordance with the applicable legal provisions.

12. Central and State Information Commissions

The RTI Act provides for the Central Information Commission (CIC) and State Information Commissions (SICs). These are statutory bodies responsible for exercising the functions assigned to them under the Act.

Central Information Commission

  • Established by the Central Government under Section 12.
  • Consists of the Chief Information Commissioner and such number of Information Commissioners as may be prescribed, subject to the statutory maximum.
  • Exercises jurisdiction over central public authorities.
  • Hears complaints and second appeals under the Act.

State Information Commissions

  • Constituted by State Governments under Section 15.
  • Consist of a State Chief Information Commissioner and such number of State Information Commissioners as may be prescribed, subject to the statutory maximum.
  • Exercise jurisdiction over public authorities within the relevant State framework.
  • Hear complaints and second appeals under the Act.

13. Complaint and Appeal Mechanism

Complaint: Section 18

A person may complain to the appropriate Information Commission in circumstances specified by the Act, including where:

  • No Public Information Officer has been appointed.
  • An application is refused or not accepted.
  • Access to information is denied.
  • No response is received within the prescribed period.
  • Unreasonable fees are demanded.
  • Incomplete, misleading or false information is provided.

First Appeal: Section 19(1)

A person who does not receive a decision within the prescribed period or is aggrieved by a decision of the Public Information Officer may file a first appeal with an officer senior in rank to the Public Information Officer in the same public authority. It is ordinarily to be filed within thirty days, subject to condonation for sufficient cause.

Second Appeal: Section 19(3)

A second appeal lies to the Central or State Information Commission, as applicable, within ninety days from the date on which the decision should have been made or was actually received, subject to condonation for sufficient cause.

14. Penalties and Compensation

Section 20 empowers the Information Commission to impose a penalty on a Public Information Officer in specified circumstances, such as:

  • Refusing to receive an application without reasonable cause.
  • Not furnishing information within the prescribed time without reasonable cause.
  • Malafidely denying a request.
  • Knowingly providing incorrect, incomplete or misleading information.
  • Destroying requested information.
  • Obstructing the furnishing of information in any manner.

The penalty is ₹250 per day of delay, subject to a maximum of ₹25,000. Before imposing a penalty, the officer must be given a reasonable opportunity of being heard. The burden of proving that the officer acted reasonably and diligently lies on the officer.

The Commission may also recommend disciplinary action under the applicable service rules where the statutory conditions are met. Under Section 19(8), it may require the public authority to compensate the complainant for loss or other detriment suffered.

15. Special Exemptions: Section 24

Section 24 excludes specified intelligence and security organisations listed in the Second Schedule from the general application of the Act, subject to statutory exceptions.

Information pertaining to allegations of corruption and human rights violations is not excluded in the same way. For human rights violation allegations, information is provided only after approval of the Information Commission and within the special statutory time limit of forty-five days.

16. RTI Amendment Act, 2019

The Right to Information (Amendment) Act, 2019 amended provisions relating to the tenure and service conditions of the Chief Information Commissioner, Information Commissioners and State Information Commissioners.

The amendment replaced the earlier statutory specification of fixed tenure and service conditions with a framework under which these matters are prescribed by the Central Government through rules.

The amendment generated debate about the independence and autonomy of Information Commissions, particularly because their institutional effectiveness depends on secure tenure, impartiality and adequate resources.

17. Importance of RTI in Indian Democracy

  1. Strengthens the citizen's ability to scrutinise public decisions.
  2. Helps expose corruption, irregularities and misuse of public funds.
  3. Promotes transparency in recruitment, procurement and welfare delivery.
  4. Supports informed participation in democratic processes.
  5. Improves the responsiveness of public institutions.
  6. Enables social audits and community monitoring.
  7. Strengthens the relationship between citizens and public authorities.

18. Challenges in Implementation

  • Vacancies and pendency: Delays in appointing Information Commissioners and a large number of pending appeals weaken timely access to information.
  • Weak record management: Poor maintenance, digitisation and indexing of records make information difficult to retrieve.
  • Low awareness: Many citizens, especially in rural and marginalised communities, may not know how to use the RTI mechanism.
  • Threats to applicants: Citizens and activists seeking sensitive information may face intimidation or violence.
  • Inadequate proactive disclosure: Failure to comply effectively with Section 4 increases the burden on individual applicants.
  • Misuse and administrative burden: Frivolous, repetitive or excessively broad requests can place pressure on public authorities and divert resources.
  • Balancing competing interests: Public access must be reconciled with privacy, national security, commercial confidentiality and other lawful exemptions.
  • Digital divide: Online systems may exclude people without internet access, digital literacy or accessible interfaces.

19. Measures to Strengthen the RTI Framework

  1. Ensure timely appointments and adequate staffing of Information Commissions.
  2. Improve record management and digitisation across public authorities.
  3. Strengthen proactive disclosure under Section 4.
  4. Provide accessible RTI facilities in local languages and through offline channels.
  5. Improve awareness and training for citizens and public officials.
  6. Ensure effective protection and support for applicants facing threats.
  7. Dispose of appeals and complaints within reasonable periods.
  8. Balance transparency with privacy and legitimate security interests through lawful and proportionate measures.
  9. Promote a culture of openness and public service accountability.

20. RTI and Good Governance

RTI is an important instrument of good governance because it makes government functioning more visible and enables citizens to question public authorities. It complements social audits, citizens' charters, public hearings, grievance redressal and institutional audits.

However, information access alone cannot ensure accountability. Effective governance also requires independent oversight, corrective action, fair investigation and institutional responsiveness.

21. Key Takeaways

  • The RTI Act, 2005 provides citizens with a statutory right to access information held by public authorities.
  • The constitutional foundation is closely connected with Article 19(1)(a) and the right to know.
  • Section 4 requires proactive disclosure by public authorities.
  • Sections 8 and 9 provide specified grounds for withholding information.
  • Information Commissions hear complaints and second appeals and can impose statutory penalties.
  • Effective implementation depends on strong institutions, proper records, citizen awareness and protection of applicants.
