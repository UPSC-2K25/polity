Financial Relations between the Union and States

Financial federalism concerns the constitutional distribution of taxation powers, sharing of revenues, transfer of resources and borrowing responsibilities between different levels of government. India's Constitution combines a relatively strong Union with mechanisms intended to ensure that States have resources to discharge their constitutional and developmental responsibilities.

1. Constitutional Framework

Financial relations are primarily governed by Part XII of the Constitution, particularly Articles 264 to 293. The Seventh Schedule distributes legislative competence over taxation, while Part XII establishes the framework for tax assignment, tax devolution, grants and borrowing.

2. Principles of Fiscal Federalism

Fiscal federalism seeks to align the assignment of public functions with the resources required to perform them. Its central concern is how financial powers and responsibilities should be distributed to achieve efficiency, equity and accountability.

Major principles

  • Vertical fiscal balance: The distribution of revenue resources between the Union and all States should broadly correspond to their expenditure responsibilities.
  • Horizontal fiscal balance: Differences in revenue capacity and expenditure needs among States should be addressed through appropriate transfers.
  • Equity: Citizens should have access to reasonably comparable public services irrespective of the State in which they reside.
  • Efficiency: Taxation and public expenditure should minimise economic distortions and encourage productive use of resources.
  • Autonomy: Each level of government should have meaningful discretion over its constitutionally assigned responsibilities.
  • Accountability: Governments should be answerable for revenue collection, expenditure and delivery of public services.
  • Predictability: Transfers and fiscal rules should provide governments with a reasonable basis for medium-term planning.

3. Distribution of Taxation Powers

The Constitution distributes legislative authority over taxation through the Union List, State List and relevant constitutional provisions governing concurrent and special tax arrangements. Unlike ordinary subjects, taxation requires specific constitutional authority.

Union taxation powers

Parliament has exclusive authority over taxes assigned to the Union List, subject to the Constitution. Major Union taxes include:

  • Corporation tax.
  • Taxes on income other than agricultural income.
  • Customs duties.
  • Union excise duties on specified goods.
  • Taxes on capital value of assets of individuals and companies, subject to constitutional provisions.

State taxation powers

State Legislatures have authority over taxes assigned to the State List, subject to constitutional limitations. Important examples include:

  • Land revenue.
  • Agricultural income tax.
  • Taxes on land and buildings.
  • Taxes on mineral rights, subject to constitutional provisions.
  • Taxes on consumption or sale of electricity.
  • Taxes on vehicles.
  • Taxes on professions, trades, callings and employments, subject to Article 276.
  • State excise duties on specified alcoholic liquor and other constitutionally assigned products.

Article 265: Rule of law in taxation

Article 265 provides that no tax shall be levied or collected except by authority of law. It protects individuals and businesses against arbitrary taxation and requires a valid legal basis for both the imposition and collection of taxes.

Impact of the GST constitutional amendment

The Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, 2016 introduced a major restructuring of indirect taxation powers through the Goods and Services Tax framework.

Article 246A provides concurrent legislative power to Parliament and State Legislatures to make laws with respect to GST, subject to the special arrangement for inter-State supplies. Parliament has exclusive power over GST where the supply takes place in the course of inter-State trade or commerce.

4. Distribution of Tax Revenues

The Constitution distinguishes between the authority to levy a tax and the manner in which its proceeds are assigned or distributed. A tax may be levied by the Union but assigned to States or shared between the Union and States under constitutional provisions.

Article 268 — Duties levied by the Union but collected and appropriated by States

Article 268 deals with specified stamp duties and duties of excise on medicinal and toilet preparations containing alcohol or narcotic substances. These duties are levied by the Union but collected and appropriated by the States in accordance with the constitutional arrangement.

Article 269 — Taxes levied and collected by the Union but assigned to States

Article 269 provides for specified taxes on inter-State sales and purchases to be levied and collected by the Union but assigned to the States, subject to the constitutional and statutory framework. The GST amendments altered the treatment of inter-State supplies through Article 269A.

Article 269A — GST on inter-State supplies

GST on supplies in the course of inter-State trade or commerce is levied and collected by the Government of India and apportioned between the Union and States in the manner provided by Parliament on the recommendations of the GST Council.

Article 270 — Taxes distributed between Union and States

Article 270 provides for the distribution of specified Union taxes between the Union and States. The divisible pool forms the basis for tax devolution recommended by the Finance Commission.

Certain taxes and proceeds are excluded from the divisible pool under the constitutional framework. These include surcharges under Article 271 and specified cesses levied for particular purposes.

Article 271 — Surcharge

Parliament may at any time increase specified duties or taxes for Union purposes by imposing a surcharge. The proceeds of such surcharges form part of Union revenues and are not shared with States through the ordinary divisible-pool mechanism.

Why the divisible pool matters

  • It provides a constitutionally grounded basis for sharing central tax revenues.
  • It helps States finance their expenditure responsibilities.
  • It reduces disparities in fiscal capacity among States.
  • Its composition affects the effective fiscal space available to States.

5. Goods and Services Tax and Cooperative Federalism

GST is a destination-based indirect tax on the supply of goods and services. It replaced a range of central and State indirect taxes and created a common framework for taxing supplies across the country.

Constitutional provisions

  • Article 246A: Confers legislative power on Parliament and State Legislatures to make laws on GST, with Parliament having exclusive power over inter-State supplies.
  • Article 269A: Provides for levy and apportionment of GST on inter-State supplies.
  • Article 279A: Provides for the GST Council.
  • Article 286: Restricts the imposition of taxes on supplies outside the State or in the course of import into or export out of India, subject to the constitutional framework.

GST Council — Article 279A

The President constitutes the GST Council. It is a constitutional body designed to facilitate joint decision-making between the Union and States on important aspects of GST.

Its composition includes:

  • The Union Finance Minister as Chairperson.
  • The Union Minister of State in charge of Revenue or Finance.
  • The Minister in charge of Finance or Taxation, or another nominated Minister, from each State government.

Functions of the GST Council

  • Recommend taxes, cesses and surcharges to be subsumed into GST.
  • Recommend goods and services that may be subject to or exempt from GST.
  • Recommend model GST laws, principles of levy and apportionment of GST on inter-State supplies.
  • Recommend rates, including floor rates with bands where appropriate.
  • Recommend special provisions for specified States.
  • Address other matters relating to GST as provided under Article 279A.

Decision-making

Every decision of the GST Council requires a majority of not less than three-fourths of the weighted votes of members present and voting. The Union Government has one-third of the total weighted votes, while the States together have two-thirds.

Importance for federalism

  • Institutionalises consultation between the Union and States.
  • Promotes harmonisation of indirect taxation.
  • Facilitates a common national market.
  • Creates a forum for negotiation over tax rates and administrative issues.

Challenges and debates

  • States have concerns about reduced independent discretion over important indirect taxes.
  • Dependence on shared revenues can affect fiscal autonomy.
  • Compliance complexity and technological issues can burden smaller businesses.
  • Revenue uncertainty can complicate State budgeting.
  • Differences in fiscal priorities may create disagreement in Council deliberations.

6. Grants-in-Aid

Grants are transfers from one level of government to another. They help address differences between revenue capacity and expenditure requirements and can support nationally important objectives.

Article 275 — Statutory grants

Parliament may provide grants-in-aid from the Consolidated Fund of India to States that are in need of assistance. Different sums may be fixed for different States.

Article 275 also provides for grants for promoting the welfare of Scheduled Tribes and raising the level of administration in Scheduled Areas, as constitutionally specified.

Article 282 — Discretionary grants

The Union or a State may make grants for any public purpose, even if the purpose is outside its legislative field, subject to constitutional requirements.

Article 282 provides the constitutional basis for many forms of discretionary grants and centrally supported programmes.

Centrally Sponsored Schemes

Centrally Sponsored Schemes are programmes supported financially by the Union and implemented by States, usually with specified sharing arrangements. They operate within the wider framework of fiscal transfers and cooperative policy implementation.

Their effectiveness depends on appropriate design, predictable fund flows, flexibility for regional requirements and clear accountability for outcomes.

7. Finance Commission

The Finance Commission is a constitutional body established under Article 280. It recommends the distribution of financial resources between the Union and States and among the States.

Constitution and composition

  • The President constitutes a Finance Commission every five years or earlier if necessary.
  • It consists of a Chairperson and four other members.
  • Parliament may determine by law the qualifications of members and the manner of their selection.

Functions under Article 280(3)

  1. Recommend the distribution between the Union and States of the net proceeds of taxes that are to be divided between them, and the allocation among the States of their respective shares.
  2. Recommend the principles governing grants-in-aid of the revenues of States out of the Consolidated Fund of India.
  3. Recommend measures needed to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of Panchayats, based on recommendations of the State Finance Commission.
  4. Recommend measures needed to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of Municipalities, based on recommendations of the State Finance Commission.
  5. Consider any other matter referred to it by the President in the interests of sound finance.

Vertical devolution

Vertical devolution refers to the distribution of the divisible pool of Union taxes between the Union and all States collectively. It addresses the imbalance between the revenue-raising capacity and expenditure responsibilities of the two levels of government.

Horizontal devolution

Horizontal devolution refers to the distribution of the States' collective share among individual States. It seeks to account for differences in population, income levels, geography, demographic performance and other relevant factors.

Common criteria used in devolution

Finance Commissions may use criteria such as:

  • Income distance.
  • Population.
  • Area.
  • Forest and ecological considerations.
  • Demographic performance.
  • Tax and fiscal effort.

The weight assigned to each criterion is determined by the particular Finance Commission. Criteria and weights should therefore be studied with reference to the relevant Commission's report rather than assumed to remain constant.

Nature of Finance Commission recommendations

The Finance Commission is an expert constitutional advisory body. Its recommendations are placed before Parliament along with an explanatory memorandum on action taken. The Union Government considers and implements recommendations through the constitutional and financial process.

8. State Finance Commissions

State Finance Commissions are provided for under Articles 243-I and 243-Y. They examine the financial position of Panchayats and Municipalities and recommend measures to improve their financial resources.

Constitutional provisions

  • Article 243-I: The Governor constitutes a Finance Commission to review the financial position of Panchayats.
  • Article 243-Y: The Finance Commission constituted under Article 243-I also reviews the financial position of Municipalities and makes recommendations concerning their finances.

Major functions

  • Recommend principles governing the distribution of State revenues between the State and local bodies.
  • Determine the taxes, duties, tolls and fees that may be assigned to or appropriated by local bodies.
  • Recommend grants-in-aid to local bodies from the Consolidated Fund of the State.
  • Suggest measures to improve the financial position of Panchayats and Municipalities.

Challenges

  • Delays in constituting State Finance Commissions.
  • Delayed acceptance or implementation of recommendations.
  • Weak local revenue administration.
  • Limited availability of reliable financial data.
  • Inadequate transfer of functions, funds and functionaries to local governments.

9. Public Funds and Financial Accountability

Article 266 — Consolidated Funds and Public Accounts

Article 266 provides for the Consolidated Fund of India and the Consolidated Fund of each State, as well as their respective Public Accounts.

  • Consolidated Fund: Receives revenues, loans raised and loan repayments by the government. Withdrawals require constitutional and legislative authorisation.
  • Public Account: Includes money held by the government in a fiduciary or banking capacity, such as provident funds and small savings. It is distinct from the Consolidated Fund.

Article 267 — Contingency Fund

Article 267 provides for a Contingency Fund of India and Contingency Funds of States. These funds enable advances for unforeseen expenditure pending legislative authorisation through the normal budgetary process.

Role of the Comptroller and Auditor General

The Comptroller and Auditor General of India audits public expenditure and receipts in accordance with constitutional and statutory provisions. Audit supports legislative oversight, transparency and accountability in the use of public resources.

The constitutional position, functions and institutional safeguards of the CAG are covered in the dedicated chapter on Constitutional Bodies.

10. Borrowing Powers of the Union and States

Article 292 — Borrowing by the Union

The Union Government may borrow upon the security of the Consolidated Fund of India within limits fixed by Parliament by law. It may also give guarantees within such limits as Parliament determines.

Article 293 — Borrowing by States

A State may borrow within India upon the security of its Consolidated Fund, subject to constitutional conditions and limits imposed by its Legislature.

A State that has outstanding loans or guarantees for which the Union Government is responsible may require the consent of the Government of India before raising further loans. Such consent may be subject to conditions.

Why State borrowing matters

  • States require resources for infrastructure, social services and development expenditure.
  • Borrowing can help finance long-term capital investment.
  • Excessive debt can create interest burdens and reduce future fiscal flexibility.
  • Borrowing restrictions can create tension between fiscal discipline and State autonomy.

11. Fiscal Responsibility and Debt Sustainability

Fiscal responsibility involves managing public expenditure, borrowing and deficits in a sustainable manner. It seeks to preserve macroeconomic stability while ensuring that governments retain adequate resources for development and public services.

Fiscal indicators

  • Revenue deficit: The excess of revenue expenditure over revenue receipts.
  • Fiscal deficit: The excess of total expenditure over total receipts excluding borrowings.
  • Primary deficit: Fiscal deficit minus interest payments.
  • Debt-to-GSDP ratio: A measure of State government debt relative to the size of its economy.

Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management framework

The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act, 2003 provides a framework for fiscal discipline at the Union level. States have also enacted their own fiscal responsibility legislation.

Fiscal rules can improve transparency and discipline, but they must be designed with sufficient flexibility to respond to economic shocks, disasters and exceptional public needs.

Off-budget borrowings and contingent liabilities

Governments may incur liabilities through guarantees, public enterprises or borrowing arrangements that do not immediately appear as ordinary budgetary borrowing. Inadequate disclosure can obscure the true fiscal position.

Transparent reporting of guarantees, public enterprise liabilities and other contingent obligations is therefore important for sound fiscal management.

12. Major Issues in India's Fiscal Federalism

  • Vertical imbalance: The Union has access to major and buoyant sources of taxation, while States carry significant responsibilities for public services and development.
  • Horizontal disparities: States differ in income, tax capacity, geography, population and social development needs.
  • Cesses and surcharges: Their exclusion from the divisible pool can reduce the proportion of gross Union tax revenue shared with States.
  • Conditional transfers: Tied grants and scheme-based transfers may limit State flexibility in allocating resources.
  • GST-related concerns: Changes in the indirect tax system have altered the fiscal space available to States.
  • Borrowing constraints: States may face restrictions in accessing additional resources for development.
  • Local government finances: Panchayats and Municipalities often lack adequate own-source revenue and predictable transfers.
  • Expenditure pressures: Welfare commitments, salaries, pensions, subsidies and debt servicing constrain development expenditure.
  • Regional disparities: Less-developed States may have greater expenditure needs but weaker revenue capacity.
  • Weak fiscal transparency: Incomplete reporting of guarantees and off-budget liabilities can undermine accountability.

13. Strengthening Fiscal Federalism

  1. Strengthen predictable tax devolution: Ensure timely and transparent implementation of Finance Commission recommendations.
  2. Improve the quality of grants: Balance national priorities with flexibility for State-specific requirements.
  3. Enhance GST Council cooperation: Promote regular consultation, evidence-based decisions and transparent resolution of disagreements.
  4. Strengthen State revenue capacity: Improve tax administration, compliance, property taxation and non-tax revenue mobilisation.
  5. Reinforce local government finances: Ensure timely State Finance Commissions and meaningful devolution of the three Fs: functions, funds and functionaries.
  6. Improve fiscal transparency: Disclose guarantees, contingent liabilities and off-budget borrowings comprehensively.
  7. Promote responsible borrowing: Link borrowing to sustainable debt management and productive capital investment.
  8. Address regional inequalities: Use equalisation transfers to improve access to essential public services across States.
  9. Improve public expenditure quality: Emphasise outcomes, efficiency, monitoring and social accountability rather than expenditure alone.

15. Key Takeaways

  • Part XII of the Constitution provides the principal framework for Union-State financial relations.
  • Article 265 establishes the requirement that taxation must have the authority of law.
  • Articles 268–271 govern important aspects of tax assignment and distribution.
  • Article 280 provides for the Finance Commission, a central institution of fiscal federalism.
  • Articles 243-I and 243-Y provide for State Finance Commissions to strengthen local government finances.
  • Article 279A establishes the GST Council as a constitutional forum for cooperative tax governance.
  • Articles 292 and 293 govern borrowing by the Union and States.
  • Effective fiscal federalism requires adequate resources, predictable transfers, responsible borrowing and meaningful State autonomy.
