Administrative Relations between the Union and States

Administrative relations concern the distribution and coordination of executive authority between the Union and the States. The Constitution generally allows each level of government to administer matters within its legislative competence, while providing mechanisms for coordination, Union directions, All India Services and the resolution of intergovernmental difficulties.

1. Constitutional Framework

Administrative relations are primarily governed by Part XI of the Constitution, particularly Articles 256 to 263. These provisions establish the relationship between Union and State executive powers and provide mechanisms for cooperation and coordination.

2. Distribution of Executive Powers

Executive power of the Union — Article 73

The executive power of the Union extends:

  • To matters with respect to which Parliament has power to make laws.
  • To the exercise of rights, authority and jurisdiction conferred on the Union by a treaty or agreement.

The Constitution places an important qualification on the Union's executive power in relation to matters in the Concurrent List. Unless the Constitution or a parliamentary law expressly provides otherwise, Union executive power does not ordinarily extend to a matter on which the State Legislature also has power to make laws.

Executive power of the States — Article 162

The executive power of a State extends to matters with respect to which its Legislature has power to make laws.

In relation to Concurrent List matters, State executive power is subject to the Constitution and to the executive power expressly conferred on the Union or its authorities by the Constitution or parliamentary law.

Relationship between legislative and executive powers

Legislative and executive powers are closely connected, but they are not identical. The legislative lists provide the principal basis for determining the ordinary fields of executive responsibility. Constitutional provisions and valid laws may create additional arrangements for implementation and coordination.

3. Union Directions to State Governments

The Constitution authorises the Union to issue directions to States in specified situations. These provisions seek to ensure that State executive action remains consistent with constitutional obligations and does not obstruct the exercise of Union executive power.

Article 256 — Compliance with laws made by Parliament

The executive power of every State must be exercised to ensure compliance with laws made by Parliament and with existing laws applicable in that State.

The Union may give directions to a State as may appear necessary to the Government of India for this purpose.

Article 257 — Control of the Union over States in certain cases

The executive power of a State must be exercised so as not to impede or prejudice the exercise of the executive power of the Union. The Union may issue directions to a State for this purpose.

Article 257 also provides for specific directions concerning:

  • Construction and maintenance of means of communication declared to be of national or military importance.
  • Measures to be taken for the protection of railways within a State.

The Constitution provides for compensation arrangements in specified circumstances where a State incurs additional costs in complying with directions under Article 257.

Limits and constitutional significance

  • Union directions must be grounded in the relevant constitutional provisions.
  • They are not a general authority to administer every State subject.
  • Their exercise should respect the constitutional distribution of powers.
  • Excessive or arbitrary use can create friction and weaken cooperative federalism.

4. Delegation and Entrustment of Executive Functions

Article 258 — Union functions entrusted to States

The President may, with the consent of the Government of a State, entrust to that State or its officers functions relating to matters within the executive power of the Union.

Parliament may also confer powers and impose duties on a State or its officers in relation to matters within the Union's legislative competence.

Article 258A — State functions entrusted to the Union

The Governor of a State may, with the consent of the Government of India, entrust functions relating to matters within the executive power of the State to the Union or its officers.

Why these provisions matter

  • They allow administrative flexibility without changing the constitutional distribution of legislative powers.
  • They can reduce duplication of administrative machinery.
  • They enable governments to use existing institutional capacity at another level.
  • They support coordinated implementation of laws and public programmes.

Entrustment of functions should be distinguished from a permanent transfer of legislative competence. The constitutional authority to make laws remains governed by the relevant legislative provisions.

5. All India Services

All India Services are an important institutional link between the Union and the States. Their officers serve in both Union and State administrations, contributing to administrative continuity, coordination and national standards.

Constitutional basis — Article 312

Article 312 empowers Parliament to create one or more All India Services common to the Union and the States if the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution supported by not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting, declaring that it is necessary or expedient in the national interest to do so.

Parliament may then provide by law for the creation of the service and regulate recruitment and conditions of service, subject to the Constitution.

Existing All India Services

  • Indian Administrative Service (IAS) — general administration, policy implementation and senior administrative responsibilities.
  • Indian Police Service (IPS) — police leadership, public order administration and internal security responsibilities.
  • Indian Forest Service (IFoS) — forest administration, conservation and environmental management.

Role in Indian federalism

  • Provides an administrative link between the Union and State governments.
  • Supports continuity in public administration across changes in elected governments.
  • Facilitates the implementation of national laws and programmes at the State and district levels.
  • Enables officers to gain experience in different levels of government.
  • Can support coordination in areas involving national and regional responsibilities.

Challenges and concerns

  • Tensions may arise between Union and State priorities in matters of cadre management and deputation.
  • Frequent transfers and political interference can affect administrative stability.
  • Officers may face conflicting expectations from different levels of government.
  • Administrative uniformity must be balanced with sensitivity to local conditions and State priorities.

Way forward

  • Strengthen professional autonomy and impartiality.
  • Ensure transparent and predictable cadre-management arrangements.
  • Promote stability of tenure, subject to legitimate administrative needs.
  • Improve training in constitutional values, cooperative federalism and public service ethics.
  • Encourage constructive coordination between cadre-controlling authorities and State governments.

6. Inter-State Coordination and Cooperation

Article 261 — Public acts, records and judicial proceedings

Article 261 provides that full faith and credit must be given throughout India to public acts, records and judicial proceedings of the Union and every State.

Parliament may determine the manner in which, and the conditions under which, such acts, records and proceedings are to be proved and their effect determined.

This provision supports legal continuity and recognition of public records and judicial proceedings across State boundaries.

Article 263 — Inter-State Council

The President may establish an Inter-State Council if it appears that public interests would be served by such a body.

The Council may be assigned duties relating to:

  • Inquiring into and advising upon disputes that may have arisen between States.
  • Investigating and discussing subjects in which some or all States, or the Union and one or more States, have a common interest.
  • Making recommendations for better coordination of policy and action on such subjects.

Inter-State Council in practice

The Inter-State Council was established by Presidential Order in 1990 following the recommendations of the Sarkaria Commission. It is a constitutional mechanism for dialogue rather than a court or an authority with general power to impose binding decisions.

Its value lies in facilitating consultation, identifying common concerns and building consensus on issues affecting more than one government.

Zonal Councils

Zonal Councils are statutory bodies established under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956. They are designed to promote cooperation among States within geographical zones and between States and the Union.

Their discussions commonly concern inter-State transport, border issues, economic and social planning, linguistic matters, security and regional development.

Zonal Councils are distinct from the Inter-State Council: the former are statutory regional institutions, while the latter is provided for by Article 263 of the Constitution.

7. Inter-State River Water Disputes

Rivers frequently cross State boundaries. Differences in water availability, irrigation requirements, drinking-water needs, hydropower and ecological priorities can create disputes between States.

Article 262

Article 262 empowers Parliament to provide by law for the adjudication of disputes relating to the use, distribution or control of waters of any inter-State river or river valley.

Parliament may also provide that neither the Supreme Court nor any other court shall exercise jurisdiction over such disputes as specified by law.

Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956

Parliament enacted the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 to provide a statutory mechanism for adjudication of specified inter-State water disputes.

The Act provides for the constitution of tribunals when the statutory conditions are met. Tribunal proceedings and awards operate within the framework of the Act and the relevant constitutional provisions.

Challenges in river-water governance

  • Competing demands for drinking water, irrigation, industry and power generation.
  • Variations in rainfall and river flows across seasons and years.
  • Differences in historical water-use patterns and agreements.
  • Delays in adjudication and difficulties in implementing decisions.
  • Political mobilisation around regional water interests.
  • Climate change, groundwater depletion and environmental degradation.

Measures for better management

  • Strengthen reliable river-basin data and transparent sharing of information.
  • Promote basin-level planning and ecological sustainability.
  • Improve institutional coordination among concerned States.
  • Ensure timely adjudication and effective implementation of lawful decisions.
  • Encourage negotiation and technical dialogue alongside formal dispute-resolution mechanisms.

8. Governor and Administrative Federalism

The Governor is the constitutional head of a State. The office forms part of the State executive, but the Governor is appointed by the President and holds office during the pleasure of the President under the constitutional framework.

The Governor's role can become important in Union-State relations, particularly in situations involving government formation, legislative processes, reservation of Bills for the President's consideration and constitutional breakdown.

Federal concerns

  • Perceived political partisanship can undermine confidence in the office.
  • Delays in decisions concerning Bills may affect the legislative process and State autonomy.
  • Discretionary action in government-formation situations can generate constitutional controversy.
  • Differences between the Governor and the elected State government can affect administrative coordination.

The constitutional position, powers, discretionary functions and relevant judicial decisions concerning the Governor are examined in the dedicated chapter on the Governor under Executive and Administration.

9. Emergency Provisions and Administrative Relations

Emergency provisions can substantially change the ordinary distribution of executive authority. They are exceptional constitutional arrangements and should not be treated as the normal basis of Union-State administration.

National Emergency — Article 352

During a Proclamation of Emergency, the Union's executive authority may extend to giving directions to a State concerning the manner in which its executive power is to be exercised.

President's Rule — Article 356

Where the constitutional machinery of a State cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution, a Proclamation under Article 356 may be issued, subject to constitutional requirements and parliamentary approval.

The President may assume specified State executive functions and declare that the powers of the State Legislature shall be exercisable by or under the authority of Parliament.

Financial Emergency — Article 360

During a Financial Emergency, the Union may issue directions to States to observe specified financial propriety and other measures within the scope of Article 360.

These provisions are subject to constitutional safeguards. In particular, the use of Article 356 is subject to judicial review as recognised in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India.

10. Administrative Relations: Major Challenges

  • Over-centralisation: Excessive reliance on Union directions may reduce the space available for State-level decision-making.
  • Political differences: Different political alignments at the Union and State levels can complicate administrative cooperation.
  • Governor-related disputes: Controversies concerning the exercise of gubernatorial functions can strain federal relations.
  • Administrative capacity: States differ in staffing, technical expertise, institutional resources and implementation capacity.
  • All India Services tensions: Differences over deputation, transfers and cadre management may create friction.
  • Inter-State disputes: River waters, boundaries and shared infrastructure can require prolonged coordination.
  • Weak consultation: Insufficient use of intergovernmental forums may leave disputes unresolved or deepen mistrust.
  • Implementation gaps: National policies may not adequately account for differences in local conditions and State capacity.

11. Strengthening Cooperative Administrative Federalism

  1. Institutionalise consultation: Use the Inter-State Council and Zonal Councils regularly for policy dialogue and dispute prevention.
  2. Respect constitutional boundaries: Ensure that Union directions are exercised within their constitutional scope.
  3. Improve administrative coordination: Establish clear responsibilities, timelines and communication channels between Union and State departments.
  4. Strengthen All India Services: Promote impartiality, professional autonomy, stable tenure and transparent cadre management.
  5. Improve dispute resolution: Combine negotiation, technical assessment and timely adjudication where necessary.
  6. Build State capacity: Strengthen local administrative institutions, technical expertise and public-service delivery systems.
  7. Encourage data sharing: Promote common information systems and reliable data for issues involving more than one government.
  8. Promote cooperative implementation: Design national programmes with meaningful State participation and flexibility for regional needs.
  9. Maintain constitutional morality: Public authorities should exercise their powers with restraint, impartiality and respect for representative institutions.

12. Important Committees and Commissions

Commission or committee Period Relevance to administrative federalism
Administrative Reforms Commission First ARC, 1966; Second ARC, 2005 Examined administrative reform, public-service delivery, accountability and institutional coordination.
Sarkaria Commission 1983–1988 Examined Centre-State relations and recommended stronger consultation, restraint in the use of central powers and institutional coordination.
Punchhi Commission 2007–2010 Re-examined Centre-State relations, including the Governor's role, emergency provisions and intergovernmental cooperation.
Rajamannar Committee 1969–1971 Examined Centre-State relations from the perspective of State autonomy and decentralisation of powers.

These reports and recommendations should be studied as inputs into constitutional and administrative reform. Their recommendations are not automatically binding constitutional rules.

13. Important Judicial Decisions

Case Year Significance
State of Rajasthan v. Union of India 1977 Examined the scope of judicial review in relation to Article 356 and Union-State constitutional relations.
S.R. Bommai v. Union of India 1994 Established important limits on the use of Article 356 and recognised federalism as part of the Basic Structure.
Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India 2006 Examined constitutional limits on the dissolution of a State Legislative Assembly and the exercise of gubernatorial powers.
Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker 2016 Examined the constitutional role of the Governor and limits on gubernatorial discretion in the State legislative process.
Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India 2018 Emphasised constitutional governance, representative government and the need for institutional cooperation within Delhi's special constitutional framework.

14. Key Takeaways

  • Articles 256 to 263 form the principal constitutional framework for administrative relations between the Union and States.
  • Articles 73 and 162 define the general extent of Union and State executive powers.
  • Articles 256 and 257 provide for Union directions in specified circumstances.
  • Articles 258 and 258A enable reciprocal entrustment of executive functions.
  • Article 312 provides the constitutional basis for creating All India Services.
  • Article 263 provides for the Inter-State Council, while Zonal Councils are statutory bodies.
  • Article 262 provides the constitutional basis for parliamentary legislation on inter-State river-water dispute adjudication.
  • Cooperative administrative federalism requires consultation, constitutional restraint and institutional coordination.
