Inter-State Council and Zonal Councils

India's federal structure requires continuous consultation between the Union and States and among States themselves. The Inter-State Council, Zonal Councils and other coordination mechanisms provide institutional platforms for discussing common concerns, resolving differences and strengthening cooperative federalism.

1. Need for Intergovernmental Institutions

The constitutional distribution of powers does not eliminate the need for cooperation. Many public policy problems extend beyond the jurisdiction of a single government. Intergovernmental institutions help governments coordinate their decisions while retaining their respective constitutional responsibilities.

Such institutions are important for:

  • Discussing matters of common interest between the Union and States.
  • Reducing administrative and political friction.
  • Facilitating coordinated implementation of national programmes.
  • Addressing inter-State disputes through consultation.
  • Sharing information, experience and administrative practices.
  • Promoting balanced regional development and national integration.
  • Building trust in a diverse and politically competitive federation.

These institutions generally facilitate consultation and coordination. They do not replace the constitutional division of legislative, executive and financial powers.

2. Inter-State Council: Constitutional Basis

Article 263

Article 263 empowers the President to establish an Inter-State Council if it appears that public interests would be served by its establishment.

The President may define the nature of the Council's duties and its organisation and procedure.

Functions contemplated by Article 263

The Article identifies three broad functions:

  1. Inquiry and advice: Inquiring into and advising upon disputes that may have arisen between States.
  2. Discussion: Investigating and discussing subjects in which some or all States, or the Union and one or more States, have a common interest.
  3. Recommendations: Making recommendations upon such subjects, particularly recommendations for better coordination of policy and action.

Article 263 is enabling in nature. It does not itself create a permanently functioning Council with a fixed composition. The President may establish the Council and determine its arrangements through an order.

3. Establishment and Composition of the Inter-State Council

The Inter-State Council was established by a Presidential Order dated 28 May 1990, following the recommendations of the Sarkaria Commission.

Composition

The Council comprises:

  • The Prime Minister as Chairperson.
  • Chief Ministers of all States.
  • Chief Ministers of Union Territories having Legislative Assemblies.
  • Administrators of Union Territories without Legislative Assemblies.
  • Governors of States under President's Rule.
  • Six Union Cabinet Ministers nominated by the Prime Minister, including the Union Home Minister.

Other Union Ministers and Ministers of State with independent charge may be invited as permanent invitees or participants in accordance with the Council's arrangements.

The Union Home Minister has traditionally served as the Vice-Chairperson of the Council.

Secretariat

The Inter-State Council Secretariat was established in 1991 to provide administrative and technical support. It assists with meetings, agenda preparation, coordination and follow-up of decisions and recommendations.

4. Functions and Significance of the Inter-State Council

Policy coordination

The Council provides a forum for discussing policies that require coordination between the Union and States. It can help identify differences before they become serious disputes.

Dispute discussion

It provides an institutional setting for discussion of inter-State issues. Its recommendations can facilitate negotiated settlements, although the Council is not a court or a compulsory adjudicatory tribunal.

National integration

Regular interaction between political and administrative leadership can strengthen mutual understanding and reduce the perception that national decisions are imposed without consultation.

Sharing of best practices

States can exchange experience in public administration, service delivery, welfare implementation and institutional reform.

Significance for cooperative federalism

  • Creates a constitutional platform for regular dialogue.
  • Encourages consultation before major policy differences escalate.
  • Recognises States as important participants in national governance.
  • Helps coordinate responses to issues affecting multiple jurisdictions.
  • Provides a forum for discussing federal concerns beyond formal litigation.

5. Standing Committee of the Inter-State Council

A Standing Committee was constituted in 1996 to facilitate continuous consultation and process matters for consideration by the full Council.

Composition

The Standing Committee is chaired by the Union Home Minister and includes selected Union Ministers and Chief Ministers representing different regions and States. Its composition is determined under the applicable arrangements.

Functions

  • Process matters for consideration by the full Council.
  • Examine recommendations and issues referred to it.
  • Consider matters requiring continuous consultation.
  • Coordinate follow-up on the Council's discussions and recommendations.

The Standing Committee supports the functioning of the Council; it does not replace the Council's constitutional role under Article 263.

6. Sarkaria Commission and Inter-State Council

The Commission on Centre-State Relations, chaired by Justice R.S. Sarkaria, examined the functioning of Indian federalism and recommended institutional improvements.

Major recommendations concerning the Council

  • A permanent Inter-State Council should be established under Article 263.
  • The Council should serve as a meaningful forum for consultation on issues affecting the Union and States.
  • Its meetings should be held regularly rather than only when a major dispute arises.
  • The Council should promote discussion and coordination before differences become difficult to resolve.

The establishment of the Inter-State Council in 1990 followed a major recommendation of the Commission.

7. Punchhi Commission and Intergovernmental Relations

The Commission on Centre-State Relations chaired by Justice Madan Mohan Punchhi examined contemporary challenges in India's federal arrangements.

Relevant recommendations and concerns

  • Strengthen institutional consultation between the Union and States.
  • Improve the effectiveness of existing federal coordination mechanisms.
  • Promote regular and structured dialogue on common policy concerns.
  • Clarify constitutional practices relating to the Governor, emergency provisions and Union-State relations.

The Commission's broader work is relevant to understanding the need for predictable constitutional conventions and institutional restraint in federal governance.

8. Zonal Councils: Statutory Basis

Zonal Councils were established under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956. They are statutory advisory bodies intended to promote cooperation and coordination among States within specified geographical regions.

Unlike the Inter-State Council, which derives its basis from Article 263, Zonal Councils derive their statutory basis from an Act of Parliament.

Objectives

  • Promote national integration.
  • Reduce regional tensions and misunderstandings.
  • Encourage cooperation on economic and social planning.
  • Discuss matters involving two or more States in a region.
  • Facilitate coordination on border, transport, linguistic and developmental issues.
  • Encourage balanced regional development.

9. Five Zonal Councils

The States Reorganisation Act, 1956 provides for five Zonal Councils.

Zonal Council States included
Northern Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Punjab, Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Delhi and Chandigarh.
Central Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Uttarakhand and Uttar Pradesh.
Eastern Bihar, Jharkhand, Odisha and West Bengal.
Western Goa, Gujarat, Maharashtra and the Union Territories of Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu.
Southern Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Telangana and the Union Territory of Puducherry.

The statutory membership and representation of Union Territories should be understood with reference to the applicable provisions and subsequent administrative changes.

North Eastern Council

The North Eastern Council is a separate statutory body established under the North-Eastern Council Act, 1971. It is not one of the five Zonal Councils constituted under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956.

It provides a regional planning and coordination platform for the North-Eastern region. Its membership and functions have evolved through statutory amendments.

10. Composition of Zonal Councils

The composition of each Zonal Council is prescribed by the States Reorganisation Act, 1956.

Chairperson

The Union Home Minister is the common Chairperson of the five Zonal Councils.

Vice-Chairperson

The Chief Ministers of the States included in a particular Zonal Council act as Vice-Chairpersons by rotation, each holding office for a prescribed period.

Members

  • The Chief Minister and two other Ministers nominated by each member State.
  • Two members from each State, nominated by the Governor.
  • Representation of the Union Territories included in the Council as provided by the Act.
  • Union Ministers and other persons may participate or be invited in accordance with statutory and procedural arrangements.

The Chief Secretaries of the member States generally function as important administrative participants in the Council's deliberations.

11. Functions of Zonal Councils

Zonal Councils are advisory bodies. They discuss matters of common interest among member States and make recommendations to promote cooperation.

Major areas of discussion

  • Economic and social planning.
  • Border disputes and regional tensions.
  • Inter-State transport and communication.
  • Language-related and minority concerns.
  • Public health and epidemic response.
  • Regional infrastructure and connectivity.
  • Water, power and other shared resources.
  • Internal security and coordinated law-enforcement concerns.
  • Implementation of development programmes.

Importance

  • Provides a regional rather than exclusively national forum for consultation.
  • Allows neighbouring States to address practical administrative concerns.
  • Encourages regular interaction between political and administrative leadership.
  • Can help prevent local disagreements from escalating into larger political disputes.
  • Supports regional planning and balanced development.

12. Standing Committees and Regional Coordination

Zonal Councils are supported by Standing Committees comprising senior officials from member States and Union Territories.

These committees examine matters referred to them, prepare proposals and facilitate administrative follow-up.

The administrative machinery is important because many issues discussed by the Councils require sustained coordination between departments rather than a single high-level political decision.

14. Other Mechanisms of Intergovernmental Coordination

North Eastern Council

The North Eastern Council provides a regional platform for coordinated planning and development in the North-Eastern region. It is particularly relevant to connectivity, infrastructure, regional development and coordination among the States of the region.

GST Council

Article 279A provides for the GST Council, which brings together the Union and State governments to make recommendations on important Goods and Services Tax matters.

It is an important example of cooperative fiscal federalism. Its constitutional structure and functions are covered in the chapter on the GST Council.

National Development Council

The National Development Council was established in 1952 as a forum for cooperative planning involving the Union and States. Its historical role is relevant to understanding India's planning institutions.

The institutional framework for national planning changed substantially with the establishment of NITI Aayog in 2015. The National Development Council is not a substitute for the constitutional Inter-State Council.

Joint ministerial and administrative forums

Governments may also establish joint committees, working groups and administrative arrangements for particular projects or policy areas. Such mechanisms can be useful for transport, infrastructure, public health, environmental management and disaster response.

15. Limitations and Challenges

  • Irregular meetings: Infrequent meetings weaken the ability of institutions to provide continuous consultation.
  • Advisory character: Recommendations do not automatically resolve disputes or ensure implementation.
  • Political differences: Differences between ruling parties at the Union and State levels may affect participation and consensus.
  • Weak follow-up: Decisions and recommendations may not receive systematic monitoring.
  • Overlapping forums: Multiple institutions may discuss related issues without clear coordination.
  • Limited institutional capacity: Technical support, research and administrative follow-up may be inadequate.
  • Centralisation concerns: States may perceive consultation as inadequate where decisions with significant State-level implications are taken without meaningful participation.
  • Implementation gaps: Recommendations may remain unimplemented when responsibility is dispersed across departments and governments.

16. Reforms for Stronger Cooperative Federalism

  1. Regular meetings: Ensure predictable and adequately frequent meetings of the Inter-State Council and Zonal Councils.
  2. Better agenda-setting: Allow States to place important issues on the agenda and circulate background material well in advance.
  3. Time-bound follow-up: Establish clear responsibility for implementing recommendations and reviewing progress.
  4. Stronger secretariats: Improve research, technical expertise and administrative capacity of coordination institutions.
  5. Evidence-based deliberation: Use common data, independent assessments and transparent technical reports to inform decisions.
  6. Early dispute prevention: Use consultation and mediation before disputes become prolonged legal or political conflicts.
  7. Coordination across institutions: Clarify the relationship between the Inter-State Council, Zonal Councils and sector-specific forums.
  8. Respect for State autonomy: Ensure that coordination strengthens constitutional cooperation without undermining the legitimate powers of States.

17. Key Takeaways

  • Article 263 provides the constitutional basis for establishing an Inter-State Council.
  • The Inter-State Council was established by Presidential Order in 1990 following the Sarkaria Commission's recommendation.
  • The Council is chaired by the Prime Minister and provides a national forum for consultation and coordination.
  • The five Zonal Councils are statutory bodies created under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956.
  • The Union Home Minister chairs the five Zonal Councils.
  • The North Eastern Council is a separate statutory institution, not one of the five Zonal Councils.
  • These institutions are primarily consultative and advisory; their effectiveness depends on regular meetings, consensus and implementation.
