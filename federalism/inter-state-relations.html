Inter-State Relations

Inter-State relations concern the constitutional, administrative, economic and political relationships among India's States. The Constitution provides mechanisms for cooperation, recognition of legal acts, coordination of public policy and resolution of disputes. Their effective functioning is essential for national integration, balanced regional development and cooperative federalism.

1. Why Inter-State Relations Matter

India's States share rivers, transport networks, markets, natural resources, ecological systems and social connections. Decisions taken by one State can therefore have consequences for neighbouring States and for the country as a whole.

Inter-State relations are important for:

  • Maintaining national unity while respecting the constitutional autonomy of States.
  • Managing shared natural resources such as rivers, forests and groundwater.
  • Facilitating movement of people, goods, services and capital.
  • Coordinating infrastructure, transport and regional development.
  • Addressing disputes through constitutional and institutional mechanisms rather than unilateral action.
  • Managing cross-border public concerns such as pollution, public health, security and disaster response.

3. Inter-State Disputes and the Supreme Court

Article 131 — Original jurisdiction

Article 131 gives the Supreme Court original jurisdiction over specified disputes involving:

  • The Government of India and one or more States.
  • The Government of India and one or more States on one side and one or more other States on the other.
  • Two or more States, where the dispute involves a question of law or fact on which the existence or extent of a legal right depends.

This jurisdiction is subject to the constitutional requirements and exclusions applicable to Article 131.

Nature of the jurisdiction

  • It is an original jurisdiction, meaning the dispute may be brought directly before the Supreme Court.
  • The dispute must involve the existence or extent of a legal right.
  • It is not a general forum for every political disagreement between governments.
  • It is distinct from the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction.

Limitations and exclusions

Article 131 does not apply to every category of intergovernmental dispute. For example, Parliament may provide a separate adjudicatory framework for inter-State river-water disputes under Article 262. Certain disputes arising from pre-Constitution treaties, agreements and similar instruments are also subject to constitutional qualifications.

The Supreme Court's role under Article 131 must therefore be distinguished from the specialised statutory mechanisms created for particular disputes.

4. Inter-State River-Water Disputes

River-water disputes arise when States disagree over the use, distribution, control or management of water flowing across their boundaries. Such disputes often involve competing developmental, ecological and livelihood requirements.

Constitutional position

Entry 17 of the State List deals with water, including water supplies, irrigation, canals, drainage, embankments, water storage and water power, subject to Entry 56 of the Union List.

Entry 56 of the Union List empowers Parliament to regulate and develop inter-State rivers and river valleys to the extent that such regulation and development are declared by Parliament by law to be expedient in the public interest.

Article 262 empowers Parliament to provide for adjudication of disputes relating to the use, distribution or control of waters of inter-State rivers or river valleys.

Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956

Parliament enacted the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 to provide a statutory framework for adjudication of specified inter-State water disputes.

The Act provides for the constitution of a tribunal when the statutory requirements are met. The adjudicatory process is intended to address disputes that cannot be resolved through negotiation.

River Boards Act, 1956

The River Boards Act, 1956 provides for the establishment of River Boards to advise governments on the regulation and development of inter-State rivers and river valleys.

The Act represents an institutional approach to coordinated river-basin planning, although its practical use has remained limited.

Why river-water disputes are difficult

  • Rivers do not follow political boundaries, while administrative responsibility is organised by States.
  • Water availability varies across seasons and years.
  • Upstream and downstream States may have different priorities.
  • Irrigation, drinking water, hydropower, industry and environmental needs compete for limited resources.
  • Historical agreements may become contested as populations, economies and water requirements change.
  • Political mobilisation can make negotiated settlements more difficult.
  • Climate variability and groundwater depletion add uncertainty to water planning.

Reforms in river-water governance

  • Strengthen river-basin institutions and integrated water-resource planning.
  • Improve transparent, real-time sharing of hydrological data.
  • Encourage early negotiation and technical mediation before disputes escalate.
  • Ensure timely constitution and functioning of adjudicatory institutions.
  • Improve implementation and monitoring of tribunal decisions.
  • Incorporate ecological flows, groundwater conditions and climate resilience into allocation decisions.
  • Promote water conservation, efficient irrigation and demand management across the basin.

5. Inter-State Boundary Disputes

Boundary disputes arise when States disagree over the location or interpretation of their common boundaries. They may involve historical administrative boundaries, linguistic and cultural identities, local governance or access to resources.

Common causes

  • Reorganisation of States and differences over historical territorial claims.
  • Ambiguities in old administrative maps and boundary descriptions.
  • Linguistic, ethnic and cultural identities in border regions.
  • Disputes over land, minerals, forests, transport routes and public infrastructure.
  • Political competition and mobilisation of local populations.

Examples of boundary disputes

Dispute Broad context
Maharashtra–Karnataka Disagreement involving areas along the border, particularly the Belagavi region, and competing historical and linguistic claims.
Assam and neighbouring north-eastern States Multiple boundary disagreements involving historical administrative boundaries, local communities and territorial claims.
Haryana–Himachal Pradesh Disagreement concerning the Shivalik region around Parwanoo and related territorial claims.

Approaches to resolution

  • Bilateral negotiations between the concerned State governments.
  • Joint boundary surveys and verification of historical and administrative records.
  • Use of central facilitation where States agree to such assistance.
  • Joint committees and confidence-building arrangements.
  • Judicial resolution where the dispute involves a justiciable legal right and falls within the Court's jurisdiction.
  • Protection of local communities and prevention of violence during negotiations.

Boundary disputes should be approached through constitutional methods, historical evidence, consultation and respect for the rights and security of affected residents.

6. Inter-State Trade, Commerce and Intercourse

A common economic space is important for national integration and economic efficiency. Part XIII of the Constitution, comprising Articles 301 to 307, deals with trade, commerce and intercourse within the territory of India.

Article 301 — Freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse

Article 301 declares that trade, commerce and intercourse throughout the territory of India shall be free, subject to the other provisions of Part XIII.

The provision seeks to prevent unjustified barriers that fragment the national market and obstruct economic activity across State boundaries.

Article 302 — Parliament's power to impose restrictions

Parliament may impose restrictions on the freedom of trade, commerce or intercourse between one State and another or within any part of the territory of India in the public interest.

Article 303 — Restrictions on discriminatory legislation

Article 303 generally prohibits Parliament and State Legislatures from giving preference to one State over another or making discrimination between States in matters relating to trade and commerce.

Parliament may make an exception where it is necessary to deal with a situation arising from scarcity of goods in any part of India.

Article 304 — State restrictions

Article 304 allows States to impose certain taxes and reasonable restrictions on trade, commerce and intercourse, subject to constitutional conditions.

  • Article 304(a) permits a State to impose non-discriminatory taxes on goods imported from other States or Union Territories, provided similar goods produced within the State are subject to the same tax treatment.
  • Article 304(b) permits a State Legislature to impose reasonable restrictions in the public interest, subject to the requirement of previous sanction of the President for the introduction or moving of the relevant Bill.

Article 305 — Existing laws and State monopolies

Article 305 preserves specified existing laws and laws relating to State monopolies from the operation of the freedom provisions, subject to the constitutional qualifications.

Article 307 — Authority for carrying out Articles 301–304

Parliament may appoint an authority to carry out the purposes of Articles 301, 302, 303 and 304 and confer on it necessary powers and duties.

Significance of Part XIII

  • Promotes an integrated national market.
  • Discourages protectionist barriers between States.
  • Supports movement of goods and services across State boundaries.
  • Balances economic freedom with public-interest regulation.
  • Strengthens the economic dimension of Indian federalism.

7. Full Faith and Credit to Public Acts and Records

Article 261

Article 261 provides that full faith and credit shall be given throughout India to public acts, records and judicial proceedings of the Union and every State.

Parliament may determine the manner in which, and the conditions under which, such acts, records and proceedings are to be proved and their effect determined.

Final judgments and orders of civil courts in any part of India are capable of execution anywhere within India according to law.

Importance

  • Promotes legal continuity across State boundaries.
  • Reduces the need for repetitive legal proceedings merely because a person or record moves to another State.
  • Supports recognition of public records and judicial proceedings throughout the country.
  • Strengthens the idea of a unified constitutional and legal system.

8. Inter-State Migration and Coordination

Movement of people between States is an important feature of India's economy and society. Migration contributes to labour mobility, urbanisation, remittances and cultural exchange. It also creates administrative responsibilities that frequently cross State boundaries.

Major issues

  • Portability of welfare entitlements for migrant workers and their families.
  • Access to healthcare, education and housing in destination States.
  • Registration and protection of migrant workers.
  • Coordination between labour departments of source and destination States.
  • Language barriers, discrimination and social exclusion.
  • Timely assistance during disasters, epidemics and other emergencies.

Measures for better coordination

  • Improve portability of eligible welfare entitlements across States.
  • Develop interoperable administrative records with appropriate privacy safeguards.
  • Establish coordination mechanisms between source and destination governments.
  • Strengthen access to legal assistance and grievance redressal.
  • Ensure non-discriminatory access to public services consistent with the Constitution and law.

9. Environmental and Ecological Interdependence

Environmental systems often extend across State boundaries. Air pollution, river pollution, forest loss, wildlife movement and coastal degradation cannot be addressed effectively by isolated State action.

Major areas requiring cooperation

  • Control of pollution in inter-State river basins.
  • Coordinated management of forests and wildlife corridors.
  • Air-quality management across metropolitan and regional airsheds.
  • Disaster-risk reduction in shared river basins and coastal regions.
  • Joint planning for droughts, floods and climate-related risks.

Way forward

  • Promote regional environmental planning.
  • Share scientific data and monitoring systems.
  • Coordinate pollution control and enforcement across State boundaries.
  • Encourage joint conservation programmes and ecosystem-based planning.

11. Challenges to Effective Inter-State Relations

  • Political competition: Party differences and electoral considerations may obstruct cooperation.
  • Regionalism: Strong regional identities can sometimes intensify territorial or resource disputes.
  • Resource asymmetry: States have different natural-resource endowments and development requirements.
  • Institutional delays: Dispute-resolution mechanisms may take considerable time to produce outcomes.
  • Weak implementation: Agreements and adjudicatory decisions may face difficulties in execution.
  • Data gaps: Inadequate common data on rivers, resources and population can undermine evidence-based decisions.
  • Unilateral action: Decisions affecting neighbouring States without adequate consultation can increase mistrust.
  • Climate and environmental pressures: Changing ecological conditions can intensify competition over shared resources.

12. Strengthening Inter-State Relations

  1. Institutionalise dialogue: Hold regular consultations through constitutional and statutory forums.
  2. Promote negotiated settlements: Encourage early mediation and consultation before disputes become politically entrenched.
  3. Improve data transparency: Develop common databases and transparent technical assessments for shared resources.
  4. Ensure timely adjudication: Strengthen the capacity and effectiveness of specialised dispute-resolution mechanisms.
  5. Respect constitutional processes: Avoid unilateral measures that undermine the rights and legitimate interests of other States.
  6. Promote regional planning: Coordinate transport, energy, water, environmental and infrastructure projects.
  7. Protect affected communities: Ensure that residents of border regions and vulnerable groups are not exposed to violence, exclusion or disruption of essential services.
  8. Build a cooperative political culture: Treat inter-State cooperation as a constitutional responsibility rather than merely a political choice.

14. Key Takeaways

  • Inter-State relations are essential to the functioning of India's federal system.
  • Article 131 provides the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction in specified legal disputes involving the Union and States or States inter se.
  • Article 262 provides the constitutional basis for parliamentary legislation on inter-State river-water dispute adjudication.
  • Article 261 supports legal continuity by recognising public acts, records and judicial proceedings throughout India.
  • Part XIII protects freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse, subject to constitutional restrictions.
  • River-water and boundary disputes require institutional mechanisms, transparent data and negotiated cooperation.
  • Cooperative federalism depends on consultation, mutual respect and timely dispute resolution.
