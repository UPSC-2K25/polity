Legislative Relations between the Union and States

Legislative relations determine how law-making authority is distributed between Parliament and State Legislatures. The Constitution establishes separate and shared legislative fields, while also providing exceptional circumstances in which Parliament may legislate on matters ordinarily assigned to the States. This arrangement seeks to balance national coordination with regional autonomy.

1. Constitutional Framework

The principal provisions governing legislative relations are contained in Part XI of the Constitution, particularly Articles 245 to 255. The Seventh Schedule identifies the subjects over which the Union and State Legislatures may ordinarily make laws.

2. Distribution of Legislative Subjects: Seventh Schedule

Article 246 distributes legislative authority through three lists in the Seventh Schedule. The allocation reflects the nature of subjects, their geographical reach and the need for national uniformity or regional flexibility.

Union List — List I

Parliament ordinarily has exclusive power to make laws on subjects in the Union List, subject to other constitutional provisions. These subjects generally involve national importance, common standards or matters requiring a unified legal framework.

Important examples include:

  • Defence of India and armed forces.
  • Foreign affairs and diplomatic relations.
  • War and peace.
  • Citizenship, naturalisation and aliens.
  • Railways.
  • Posts, telegraphs and telecommunications, subject to the current constitutional entries.
  • Currency, coinage and legal tender.
  • Banking and insurance.
  • Atomic energy.
  • Inter-State trade and commerce.
  • Union taxation subjects assigned to this List.

State List — List II

State Legislatures ordinarily have exclusive power to make laws on subjects in the State List, subject to constitutional exceptions. These matters generally require regional administration and sensitivity to local conditions.

Important examples include:

  • Public order.
  • Police.
  • Public health and sanitation, subject to other constitutional entries.
  • Agriculture.
  • Land and land rights, subject to constitutional limitations.
  • Markets and fairs.
  • Local government.
  • Betting and gambling.
  • State public services and State public service commissions.
  • State taxation subjects assigned to this List.

Concurrent List — List III

Both Parliament and State Legislatures may legislate on Concurrent List subjects. This allows national principles to coexist with State-level implementation and adaptation.

Important examples include:

  • Criminal law and criminal procedure.
  • Marriage and divorce.
  • Transfer of property other than agricultural land.
  • Contracts.
  • Bankruptcy and insolvency.
  • Education.
  • Forests.
  • Protection of wild animals and birds.
  • Trade unions and industrial disputes.
  • Electricity.
  • Economic and social planning.
  • Population control and family planning.

The lists are not independent compartments in every practical situation. A single policy may involve subjects appearing in more than one list. Courts therefore examine the true nature and substance of legislation when legislative competence is challenged.

4. Residuary Legislative Powers

Residuary subjects are matters not enumerated in the State List or Concurrent List. Article 248 vests residuary legislative power in Parliament, including the power to impose a tax not mentioned in either of those lists.

Entry 97 of the Union List also covers matters not enumerated in List II or List III, including taxes not mentioned in either of those lists.

Significance

  • Provides a mechanism for dealing with new subjects that may emerge through scientific, technological or economic developments.
  • Gives Parliament authority over unenumerated subjects.
  • Reflects the relatively strong Union orientation of India's constitutional distribution of legislative powers.

Residuary power must be distinguished from the power to legislate on an enumerated subject. A subject does not become residuary merely because it is new or technologically advanced; its constitutional character and relationship with existing entries must be examined.

5. Parliamentary Power to Legislate on State List Matters

Although State Legislatures ordinarily have exclusive authority over State List subjects, the Constitution recognises specific circumstances in which Parliament may legislate on them.

Article 249 — National interest

If the Rajya Sabha declares by a resolution supported by not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting that it is necessary or expedient in the national interest for Parliament to legislate on a specified State List matter, Parliament may make laws on that matter.

  • The resolution is passed by the Rajya Sabha, reflecting its role as the Council of States.
  • It remains in force for a period not exceeding one year.
  • It may be renewed for further periods, each not exceeding one year.
  • A parliamentary law made under this Article ceases to have effect, to the extent specified in Article 249, six months after the resolution ceases to operate, except as regards things done or omitted to be done before that cessation.

Article 250 — During a National Emergency

While a Proclamation of Emergency is in operation, Parliament may legislate on any matter in the State List.

  • The power operates during the period of the Emergency.
  • A parliamentary law made under this Article ceases to have effect, to the extent specified in Article 250, six months after the Emergency ceases to operate, except in respect of things done or omitted to be done before that cessation.

Article 251 — Inconsistency with State laws

If a State law is inconsistent with a parliamentary law made under Article 249 or Article 250, the parliamentary law prevails while it remains operative. The State law is inoperative to the extent of the inconsistency for that period.

Article 252 — Legislation by consent of States

If the legislatures of two or more States pass resolutions requesting Parliament to legislate on a matter in the State List, Parliament may enact a law for those States.

  • Other States may adopt the parliamentary law by passing similar resolutions.
  • The law applies to the States that requested or subsequently adopted it.
  • The concerned State Legislatures cannot amend or repeal that parliamentary law.
  • Parliament may amend or repeal it through subsequent legislation.

This provision enables States to use parliamentary legislation where a common legal framework is considered desirable.

Article 253 — International agreements

Parliament has power to make laws for implementing treaties, agreements and conventions with other countries, or decisions made at international conferences, associations or other bodies. This power extends to matters that may otherwise fall within the State List.

Article 253 supports India's capacity to fulfil international obligations through domestic legislation. It does not mean that every international agreement automatically becomes enforceable as domestic law without the required legal framework.

6. Special Legislative Arrangement for GST

The Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, 2016 introduced a special framework for the goods and services tax.

Article 246A

  • Parliament and State Legislatures have power to make laws with respect to GST imposed by the Union or by a State.
  • Parliament has exclusive power to make laws with respect to GST where the supply of goods, services or both takes place in the course of inter-State trade or commerce.
  • Parliament's power under Article 246A operates subject to the special constitutional arrangement for GST.

Related provisions

  • Article 269A: Provides for levy and collection of GST on inter-State supplies and the apportionment of revenue.
  • Article 279A: Provides for the GST Council and its constitutional functions.
  • Article 286: Restricts the imposition of tax by States on supplies taking place outside the State or in the course of import into or export out of India, subject to constitutional provisions.

Article 246A is a special constitutional arrangement rather than a straightforward application of the ordinary three-list distribution. It creates a shared legislative framework while reserving exclusive parliamentary authority over inter-State supplies.

The GST Council is examined separately in the chapter on constitutional bodies. Its recommendations and institutional role should be understood alongside the legislative framework under Article 246A.

7. Territorial Extent of Legislative Powers

Article 245

Article 245 provides that:

  • Parliament may make laws for the whole or any part of the territory of India.
  • A State Legislature may make laws for the whole or any part of the State.

A parliamentary law is not invalid merely because it has extra-territorial operation. However, the connection between the law and India remains relevant when assessing its constitutional validity and application.

Territorial nexus

The doctrine of territorial nexus concerns the connection between the subject of a State law and the territory of that State.

A State law may, in appropriate circumstances, affect persons, property or transactions outside the State if there is a sufficient territorial connection and the connection is relevant to the object of the law.

The doctrine prevents a State Legislature from exercising unlimited authority over matters having no meaningful territorial connection with the State.

8. Repugnancy between Union and State Laws

Repugnancy arises when a valid Union law and a valid State law are inconsistent with each other in a field where both legislatures possess legislative competence.

Article 254

Article 254 principally deals with inconsistency between parliamentary legislation and State legislation on matters in the Concurrent List.

  • Under Article 254(1), if a State law is repugnant to a parliamentary law or an existing law on a Concurrent List matter, the parliamentary law generally prevails.
  • The State law is void to the extent of the repugnancy.
  • Under Article 254(2), a State law on a Concurrent List matter may prevail within that State if it has been reserved for the President's consideration and has received Presidential assent.
  • Parliament retains the power to enact a later law adding to, amending, varying or repealing the State law.

Conditions relevant to repugnancy

The doctrine is not triggered merely because two laws deal with related subjects. Courts examine whether the legislatures were competent, whether the laws operate in the same field and whether there is an actual constitutional inconsistency.

Repugnancy under Article 254 should be distinguished from a dispute over legislative competence under Article 246. The first concerns inconsistency between laws; the second concerns whether a legislature had authority to enact the law in the first place.

9. Judicial Doctrines Used to Interpret Legislative Competence

Doctrine of pith and substance

The doctrine identifies the true nature and character of a law. If, in its essential character, the law falls within the legislative competence of the enacting legislature, incidental encroachment on another legislature's field does not automatically invalidate it.

The doctrine helps courts resolve overlapping entries and prevents the distribution of legislative powers from being interpreted in an excessively rigid manner.

Doctrine of colourable legislation

The doctrine addresses situations in which a legislature appears to enact a law within its authority but, in substance, attempts to achieve something beyond its constitutional competence.

The central principle is that what cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly. The inquiry concerns legislative competence, not merely the motives or political wisdom behind a law.

Doctrine of harmonious construction

Where two or more legislative entries appear to overlap, courts attempt to interpret them in a manner that gives effect to both, as far as possible.

The objective is to avoid rendering any constitutional provision or legislative field meaningless.

Doctrine of occupied field

In certain situations, Parliament may have legislated comprehensively on a Concurrent List subject. A State law that enters the same field may be examined for repugnancy under Article 254.

The existence of a Union law does not, by itself, establish that every State law on a related subject is invalid. The statutory scheme, legislative entries and actual inconsistency must be examined.

11. Legislative Relations and Federal Balance

The distribution of legislative powers is central to the functioning of Indian federalism. It provides constitutional boundaries while allowing cooperation where policy problems cross State or national borders.

Importance of the arrangement

  • Clarity of responsibility: The lists identify the ordinary legislative fields of different levels of government.
  • Regional autonomy: State Legislatures can make laws responsive to local conditions on State List subjects.
  • National coordination: Parliament can legislate on matters requiring national uniformity or action in specified constitutional circumstances.
  • Shared policy-making: The Concurrent List allows both levels to address subjects of common concern.
  • Adaptability: Residuary powers and special provisions help the constitutional system respond to emerging issues.
  • Judicial oversight: Courts protect the constitutional allocation of legislative authority.

12. Challenges in Legislative Federalism

  • Expansion of Union legislation: Extensive use of parliamentary powers may generate concerns about the legislative autonomy of States.
  • Concurrent List conflicts: Overlapping responsibilities can produce disputes over policy design and implementation.
  • Centralisation through national laws: Common legal frameworks may not always account for regional diversity and local conditions.
  • Unclear legislative boundaries: Technological and economic developments may create difficult questions about the proper constitutional entry.
  • Repugnancy disputes: Conflicts between Union and State laws can create uncertainty for governments and citizens.
  • Limited consultation: Inadequate engagement with States during policy and law-making can weaken federal cooperation.
  • Implementation differences: Even where a common law applies, States may have different administrative capacities and regional requirements.

13. Measures to Strengthen Legislative Federalism

  1. Promote meaningful consultation with States before legislating on matters with substantial State-level implications.
  2. Use the Rajya Sabha effectively as a forum for representing State interests in national law-making.
  3. Strengthen parliamentary committee scrutiny of legislation affecting federal relations.
  4. Respect the constitutional division of legislative powers and avoid unnecessary encroachment on State subjects.
  5. Encourage evidence-based assessment of the regional effects of proposed laws.
  6. Improve coordination between Union and State law departments.
  7. Use intergovernmental institutions to resolve policy disagreements through consultation.
  8. Ensure that national legislation allows appropriate flexibility for regional conditions where the constitutional framework permits.

14. Key Takeaways

  • Articles 245 to 255 form the principal framework for legislative relations between the Union and the States.
  • Article 246 and the Seventh Schedule distribute subjects among three legislative lists.
  • Parliament has residuary legislative power under Article 248.
  • Articles 249 to 253 provide specific circumstances in which Parliament may legislate on State List matters.
  • Article 246A establishes a special shared legislative framework for GST.
  • Article 254 governs repugnancy between Union and State laws on Concurrent List matters.
  • Pith and substance, colourable legislation and harmonious construction are important doctrines used to interpret legislative competence.
  • Effective legislative federalism requires constitutional restraint, consultation and respect for regional autonomy.
