STATE EXECUTIVE · ARTICLES 153–162

Governor The constitutional head of a State and an important link between the Union and State constitutional systems.

The Governor is the constitutional head of a State. The executive power of the State is formally vested in the Governor, but it is ordinarily exercised on the aid and advice of the State Council of Ministers headed by the Chief Minister.

The Governor performs executive, legislative, financial and judicial functions under the Constitution. The office also has certain constitutionally recognised discretionary functions, which must be exercised within constitutional limits.

CONSTITUTIONAL FRAMEWORK

Part VI · Articles 153–167

Part VI of the Constitution deals with the States. Articles 153–162 contain the basic provisions relating to the Governor, including appointment, qualifications, tenure, oath, conditions of office and executive power.

Articles 163 and 164 deal with the State Council of Ministers, while Articles 174–200 contain important provisions relating to the Governor's legislative functions.

1. Constitutional position of the Governor

The Governor is the formal constitutional head of the State. The executive power of the State is vested in the Governor under Article 154.

However, India follows the parliamentary system at the State level as well as at the Union level. Therefore, the Governor ordinarily acts on the aid and advice of the State Council of Ministers headed by the Chief Minister.

The Governor is appointed by the President and holds office during the pleasure of the President. Unlike the President of India, the Governor is not elected directly or indirectly by an electoral college.

Governor as constitutional head

  • The Governor is the formal head of the State executive.
  • The Governor appoints the Chief Minister and, on the Chief Minister's advice, other ministers.
  • The Governor summons, prorogues and may dissolve the State Legislative Assembly under constitutional provisions.
  • The Governor gives assent to Bills, may withhold assent, return certain Bills for reconsideration or reserve Bills for the President.
  • The Governor performs certain functions in which constitutional discretion may be available.

2. Appointment of the Governor

Article 155

The Governor of a State is appointed by the President by warrant under the President's hand and seal.

The appointment is made by the President. There is no direct election of the Governor by the people or by the State Legislature.

Why is the Governor appointed and not elected?

The Constitution adopts an appointed Governor rather than an elected State head. This arrangement seeks to preserve the parliamentary system at the State level and maintain a constitutional connection between the Union and the States.

The Governor is expected to function as a constitutional head rather than as a parallel political executive competing with the elected State Government.

Appointment of the same person as Governor of multiple States

The Constitution permits the appointment of the same person as Governor of two or more States. This is expressly provided under Article 153.

3. Qualifications for appointment

Article 157

A person is qualified for appointment as Governor if the person:

  • Is a citizen of India; and
  • Has completed 35 years of age.

These are the constitutional qualifications expressly prescribed for the office.

Conditions of office: Article 158

Article 158 provides certain conditions relating to the office of Governor.

  • The Governor shall not be a member of either House of Parliament or of a House of the Legislature of any State.
  • If a member of Parliament or a State Legislature is appointed Governor, the person is deemed to have vacated the seat on entering upon the office.
  • The Governor shall not hold any other office of profit.
  • The Governor is entitled to official residence and emoluments, allowances and privileges as determined by law.

4. Oath or affirmation

Article 159

Before entering upon office, the Governor takes an oath or affirmation in the presence of the Chief Justice of the High Court exercising jurisdiction in relation to the State, or, in the Chief Justice's absence, the senior-most available judge of that High Court.

The Governor swears to faithfully execute the office, preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law, and devote himself or herself to the service and well-being of the people of the State.

5. Tenure and removal

Article 156

Article 156 provides that the Governor holds office during the pleasure of the President.

The Governor may resign by writing addressed to the President. Subject to the pleasure provision, the normal term of office is five years from the date on which the Governor enters upon office.

A Governor continues in office until a successor enters upon office, even after the expiry of the five-year term.

Important features

  • The Governor does not have security of tenure in the same manner as a High Court or Supreme Court judge.
  • The President may remove the Governor before completion of the five-year term.
  • The Governor may resign by addressing the resignation to the President.
  • The Governor may continue beyond five years until a successor enters office.
  • The Constitution does not prescribe a fixed procedure of impeachment for the Governor.

Can a Governor be removed arbitrarily?

The Supreme Court has clarified that although the Governor holds office during the pleasure of the President, removal cannot be based on arbitrary, capricious or unreasonable grounds. The constitutional power is subject to judicial scrutiny in appropriate cases.

6. Executive powers of the Governor

Article 154: Executive power of the State

The executive power of the State is vested in the Governor and is exercised either directly or through officers subordinate to the Governor in accordance with the Constitution.

In the parliamentary system, the Governor ordinarily exercises these powers on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers.

Major executive functions

  • Appoints the Chief Minister.
  • Appoints other ministers on the advice of the Chief Minister.
  • Appoints the Advocate-General of the State.
  • Appoints the State Election Commissioner, subject to the constitutional provisions governing the office.
  • Appoints the chairman and members of the State Public Service Commission, subject to constitutional provisions.
  • Exercises executive functions in accordance with the Constitution and applicable laws.

Appointments requiring special attention

The Governor appoints the Chief Minister under Article 164. The Governor also appoints the Advocate-General under Article 165 and the State Public Service Commission's chairman and members under Article 316.

The Governor appoints the State Election Commissioner under Article 243K for Panchayats and Article 243ZA for Municipalities.

7. Legislative powers of the Governor

The Governor is an integral part of the State Legislature under Article 168, where the State has a legislature. The Governor performs several legislative functions.

A. Summoning and proroguing the Legislature

Under Article 174, the Governor summons the House or Houses of the State Legislature and may prorogue either House. The Governor may also dissolve the Legislative Assembly.

The Constitution requires that six months shall not intervene between the last sitting of a session and the date appointed for the first sitting of the next session.

B. Addressing the Legislature

Under Article 175, the Governor may address the Legislative Assembly or, in a bicameral State, either House or both Houses assembled together. The Governor may also send messages to the House or Houses regarding Bills or other matters.

C. Special address

Under Article 176, the Governor addresses the Legislative Assembly at the commencement of the first session after each general election and at the commencement of the first session of each year.

D. Nomination to the Legislative Council

Under Article 171(3)(e), the Governor nominates members to the Legislative Council of a State having such a Council.

The nominated members are selected from persons having special knowledge or practical experience in literature, science, art, the cooperative movement and social service.

E. Assent to Bills

Article 200 deals with the Governor's options when a Bill passed by the State Legislature is presented for assent.

The Governor may:

  • Give assent to the Bill;
  • Withhold assent; or
  • Reserve the Bill for consideration of the President.

If the Bill is not a Money Bill, the Governor may return it to the Legislature with a message requesting reconsideration of the Bill or specified provisions.

If the Bill is passed again by the Legislature, with or without amendments, and presented again, the Governor shall not withhold assent. The Constitution also provides for reservation of certain Bills for the consideration of the President.

F. Ordinance-making power

Article 213 empowers the Governor to promulgate ordinances when the State Legislature is not in session and circumstances require immediate action, subject to constitutional conditions.

An ordinance has the same force and effect as an Act of the State Legislature, but it must be laid before the Legislature and ordinarily ceases to operate six weeks after reassembly, unless earlier withdrawn or disapproved.

8. Financial powers of the Governor

The Governor performs certain financial functions within the State's constitutional financial framework.

  • Causes the annual financial statement (State Budget) to be laid before the State Legislature under Article 202.
  • Recommends demands for grants under Article 203.
  • Recommends certain financial Bills in accordance with constitutional provisions.
  • Causes supplementary, additional or excess grants to be presented under Article 205.
  • Exercises powers relating to advances from the State Contingency Fund under applicable law and constitutional arrangements.

The Governor's financial functions operate within the parliamentary principle that public expenditure requires legislative authorisation.

9. Judicial powers of the Governor

Article 161: Power to grant pardons and other relief

Article 161 empowers the Governor to grant pardons, reprieves, respites or remissions of punishment, or to suspend, remit or commute sentences in cases relating to matters to which the executive power of the State extends.

This power is exercised in accordance with the constitutional framework and the aid and advice of the State Council of Ministers, subject to recognised constitutional limitations.

10. Discretionary powers of the Governor

The Governor ordinarily acts on the aid and advice of the State Council of Ministers. However, the Constitution recognises certain situations in which the Governor may exercise discretion.

Discretion is not a general personal power to disregard the elected government. It must be traced to the Constitution or a valid constitutional arrangement.

Important situations involving discretion

  • Appointment of a Chief Minister when no party or coalition has a clear majority in the Legislative Assembly.
  • Requiring a government to demonstrate its majority on the floor of the House when its legislative support is genuinely in doubt.
  • Reservation of certain Bills for the consideration of the President under Article 200.
  • Submitting a report under Article 356 when the Governor concludes that the constitutional machinery of the State has failed.
  • Performing functions in certain special constitutional arrangements where discretion is expressly provided.

Article 163: Council of Ministers to aid and advise

Article 163 provides that there shall be a Council of Ministers with the Chief Minister at the head to aid and advise the Governor in the exercise of functions, except where the Constitution requires the Governor to act in discretion.

Article 163(2) provides that if any question arises whether a matter is one in which the Governor is required by the Constitution to act in discretion, the decision of the Governor in his discretion shall be final, and the validity of anything done by the Governor shall not be called in question on the ground that the Governor ought or ought not to have acted in discretion.

This provision does not create unlimited discretionary power. The Supreme Court has interpreted the Governor's role within the broader framework of parliamentary democracy and constitutional government.

11. Governor and President's Rule

Article 356

Article 356 provides for President's Rule in a State when the President, on receipt of a report from the Governor or otherwise, is satisfied that the government of the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution.

The Governor may send a report to the President describing circumstances indicating failure of constitutional machinery in the State.

However, the Governor's report is not itself a proclamation of President's Rule. The constitutional decision under Article 356 is made by the President and is subject to parliamentary approval and judicial review.

Important principle

The Governor must not use the office to destabilise an elected State Government or recommend President's Rule merely because of political disagreement.

12. Immunities of the Governor

Article 361

Article 361 provides certain constitutional immunities to the President and Governors.

  • The President or Governor is not answerable to any court for the exercise and performance of the powers and duties of office or for acts done or purported to be done in the exercise of those powers and duties.
  • No criminal proceedings shall be instituted or continued against the President or Governor during the term of office.
  • No process for arrest or imprisonment shall issue against the President or Governor during the term of office.
  • Civil proceedings in respect of personal acts may be instituted subject to the constitutional requirement of prior notice.

Article 361 does not make every action of the government immune from judicial review. The validity of government action may still be examined in appropriate proceedings.

13. Governor and Chief Minister: Constitutional relationship

The Governor is the formal constitutional head of the State, while the Chief Minister heads the elected political executive.

Article 167 specifies the duties of the Chief Minister in relation to the Governor. These duties establish a channel of communication between the Governor and the State Council of Ministers.

  • The Chief Minister communicates decisions of the Council of Ministers relating to State administration and legislative proposals.
  • The Chief Minister furnishes information relating to State administration and legislative proposals when the Governor calls for it.
  • The Chief Minister submits for consideration of the Council of Ministers a matter decided by an individual minister if the Governor so requires.

The relationship is intended to facilitate constitutional cooperation, not to establish two competing political authorities within the State.

14. Special responsibilities of Governors

The Constitution provides special responsibilities for Governors in certain States and under particular constitutional arrangements.

These provisions must be distinguished from the general discretionary powers of the Governor. Special responsibilities are based on specific constitutional provisions and may concern particular subjects or regions.

Examples include special responsibilities concerning tribal areas, law and order in specified States, and certain development or administrative matters. The exact scope depends on the relevant constitutional provision.

15. Important Supreme Court judgments

Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974)

The Supreme Court held that the President and Governor are constitutional heads who ordinarily act on the aid and advice of their respective Councils of Ministers, except in constitutionally recognised situations.

S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)

The judgment established important principles concerning federalism, secularism and the use of Article 356. It emphasised that legislative majority should ordinarily be tested on the floor of the House rather than determined through the subjective assessment of the Governor.

Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006)

The Supreme Court examined the dissolution of the Bihar Legislative Assembly before it had met following the election. The Court held that the dissolution was unconstitutional and emphasised the limits on the Governor's role in such circumstances.

B.P. Singhal v. Union of India (2010)

The Supreme Court held that the President's power to remove a Governor is not wholly arbitrary. Removal cannot be based on capricious, unreasonable or mala fide grounds.

Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker (2016)

The Supreme Court examined the Governor's discretionary powers in the context of legislative proceedings in Arunachal Pradesh. It held that the Governor cannot ordinarily interfere with the functioning of the elected Legislature by acting beyond the constitutional limits of the office.

CORE UNDERSTANDING

The Governor is a constitutional head, not an independent parallel government.

The Governor occupies an important position in India's federal parliamentary system. The office connects the Union and State constitutional structures while preserving the principle that the elected State Government ordinarily exercises political executive authority through the Council of Ministers. Discretion must remain within constitutional boundaries.
