UNION EXECUTIVE · ARTICLES 52–62

President of India The constitutional head of the Union and the first authority in the Union Executive.

The President of India is the constitutional head of the Union. Article 52 provides that there shall be a President of India, while Article 53 vests the executive power of the Union in the President. The President exercises this power in accordance with the Constitution, including through officers subordinate to the President.

India follows a parliamentary form of government. Consequently, the President is ordinarily the formal or nominal executive, whereas the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers exercise the real political executive authority. The President generally acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister.

CONSTITUTIONAL POSITION

Articles 52 and 53: The office and executive power

Article 52 establishes the office of the President. Article 53 states that the executive power of the Union is vested in the President and is exercised either directly or through subordinate officers in accordance with the Constitution.

The President is also an integral part of Parliament under Article 79, although not a member of either House. A Bill passed by Parliament generally requires the President's assent to become law, subject to the constitutional provisions governing the legislative process.

1. Constitutional basis and nature of the office

The office of the President reflects India's decision to establish a republican and parliamentary system. Unlike a hereditary monarch, the President occupies an elected constitutional office. The President is not directly elected by citizens, but by an electoral college established under Article 54.

The President's constitutional position must be understood through the relationship between Articles 53, 74 and 75. Article 53 vests Union executive power in the President; Article 74 provides for a Council of Ministers to aid and advise the President; and Article 75 establishes the political responsibility of the Council of Ministers to the Lok Sabha.

Nominal executive and real executive

In a parliamentary system, the constitutional head and the political executive are institutionally distinct. The President is the nominal executive, while the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers constitute the real executive.

This does not mean that the President's office is merely ceremonial. The President performs important constitutional functions, ensures that constitutional procedures are followed and may exercise limited discretion where the Constitution or established parliamentary conventions permit it.

Article 74: Aid and advice of the Council of Ministers

Article 74 provides for a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at its head to aid and advise the President in the exercise of constitutional functions.

The Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976 made ministerial advice binding on the President. The Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978 added a provision allowing the President to require the Council of Ministers to reconsider its advice, either generally or otherwise. After reconsideration, the President is bound to act in accordance with the advice tendered.

2. Election of the President

The President is elected indirectly through an electoral college. The arrangement combines representation of Parliament with representation of the States and certain Union Territories. The system seeks to maintain a balance between the Union and the States.

Article 54: Electoral college

The electoral college for the election of the President consists of:

  • The elected members of both Houses of Parliament.
  • The elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of the States.
  • The elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of the Union Territories of Delhi and Puducherry.

Nominated members of Parliament and nominated members of State Legislative Assemblies do not participate in the presidential election. Members of State Legislative Councils also do not participate.

Why are elected representatives included?

The electoral college reflects the federal character of the Constitution. Parliament represents the Union, while elected members of State Legislative Assemblies represent the States. The inclusion of Delhi and Puducherry recognises their special constitutional arrangements.

Article 55: Manner of election

The election is conducted through proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote. Voting takes place by secret ballot.

The system gives different vote values to elected representatives. The value of an MLA's vote is calculated with reference to the population of the State and the number of elected members in its Legislative Assembly. The value of an MP's vote is calculated to maintain parity between the total value of votes of elected MPs and the total value of votes of elected MLAs.

Value of an MLA's vote

The constitutional formula is based on the population of the State and the number of elected members of its Legislative Assembly.

The value of each MLA's vote is determined by dividing the State's population by the total number of elected members of its Legislative Assembly, with the result expressed in units of one thousand. The constitutional rule for the remainder is applied while determining the final value.

Value of an MP's vote

The total value of votes assigned to all elected MLAs is divided by the total number of elected members of both Houses of Parliament. The resulting value determines the value of each elected MP's vote, subject to the constitutional method of calculation.

Single transferable vote

Each elector marks preferences among candidates. A candidate must obtain the prescribed quota of votes to be elected. If no candidate reaches the quota on the first count, the votes of the lowest candidate are transferred according to the next available preferences until a candidate is elected.

The system is intended to ensure that the elected President has support beyond a simple plurality of first-preference votes.

3. Qualifications for election

Article 58 lays down the qualifications required for a person to be elected President of India. A candidate must:

  1. Be a citizen of India.
  2. Have completed 35 years of age.
  3. Be qualified for election as a member of the Lok Sabha.
  4. Not hold any office of profit under the Government of India, the Government of any State, or any local or other authority subject to the control of these governments.

For this purpose, the offices of the President, Vice-President, Governor of a State and Minister of the Union or of a State are not treated as offices of profit.

Other nomination requirements

The Presidential and Vice-Presidential Elections Act, 1952, as amended, prescribes the nomination procedure. A presidential candidate must have at least 50 electors as proposers and at least 50 electors as seconders. A security deposit is also required under the law.

These nomination requirements are statutory conditions and should be distinguished from the constitutional qualifications in Article 58.

4. Oath or affirmation

Article 60 provides for the oath or affirmation of the President. Before entering office, the President makes and subscribes to the oath in the presence of the Chief Justice of India. In the Chief Justice's absence, the senior-most available Judge of the Supreme Court administers the oath.

Through the oath, the President undertakes to faithfully execute the office, preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law, and devote himself or herself to the service and well-being of the people of India.

5. Term of office and conditions

Article 56: Term of office

The President holds office for a term of five years from the date of entering office. The President may resign by writing addressed to the Vice-President. The President may also be removed from office through impeachment in accordance with Article 61.

The President continues in office until the successor enters office. This provision helps prevent a vacancy arising merely because the five-year term has expired.

Article 57: Eligibility for re-election

A person who holds or has held the office of President is eligible for re-election, subject to the constitutional qualifications. The Constitution does not prescribe a maximum number of presidential terms.

Article 59: Conditions of office

  • The President cannot be a member of either House of Parliament or of a House of the Legislature of any State.
  • If a member of such a House is elected President, the seat is deemed to have been vacated on the date the person enters office.
  • The President cannot hold any other office of profit.
  • The President is entitled to official residence, emoluments, allowances and privileges as determined by law.
  • The emoluments and allowances of the President cannot be diminished during the term of office.

Article 62: Timing of election

An election to fill a vacancy caused by the expiry of the term must be completed before the term expires. If a vacancy arises due to death, resignation, removal or otherwise, an election must be held as soon as possible and in no case later than six months from the date of occurrence of the vacancy.

A person elected to fill such a vacancy is entitled to hold office for the full term of five years from the date of entering office.

6. Impeachment of the President

Article 61 provides the procedure for impeachment of the President for violation of the Constitution. It is a special constitutional procedure involving both Houses of Parliament.

Important stages

  1. Either House of Parliament may initiate the charge.
  2. The proposal must be contained in a resolution moved after at least 14 days' written notice signed by not less than one-fourth of the total number of members of the House.
  3. The resolution must be passed by a majority of not less than two-thirds of the total membership of the House initiating the charge.
  4. The other House investigates the charge or causes it to be investigated.
  5. The President has the right to appear and be represented during the investigation.
  6. If the investigating House also passes the resolution by a majority of not less than two-thirds of its total membership, the President stands removed from office from the date the resolution is passed.

Important features

  • Either House may initiate impeachment proceedings.
  • The charge is for violation of the Constitution.
  • Both Houses participate in the process.
  • The required majority is two-thirds of the total membership of each House, not merely two-thirds of members present and voting.
  • Nominated members of either House of Parliament participate in the impeachment process.
  • Members of State Legislatures have no role in the impeachment of the President.

The Constitution does not exhaustively define the expression "violation of the Constitution". This leaves the expression to constitutional interpretation rather than providing a closed statutory list of grounds.

7. Powers and functions of the President

The President's powers are commonly classified into executive, legislative, financial, judicial, diplomatic, military and emergency powers. These classifications help organise the subject, but the exercise of the powers remains governed by the Constitution and the parliamentary system.

A. Executive powers

  • The President appoints the Prime Minister.
  • Other ministers are appointed by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister.
  • The President appoints the Attorney-General of India.
  • The President appoints Governors of States.
  • The President appoints the Comptroller and Auditor-General of India.
  • The President appoints the Chief Election Commissioner and other Election Commissioners, subject to the applicable constitutional and statutory framework.
  • The President appoints the Chairman and members of the Union Public Service Commission.
  • The President appoints the Chief Justice of India and other Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts in accordance with the constitutional process.
  • Union executive action is formally expressed in the name of the President under Article 77.

The President also has the power to seek information from the Prime Minister concerning the administration of Union affairs and proposals for legislation. Under Article 78, the Prime Minister has corresponding constitutional duties to communicate relevant information to the President.

B. Legislative powers

Article 79 makes the President a constituent part of Parliament. The President has several important legislative functions:

  • The President summons and prorogues the Houses of Parliament.
  • The President may dissolve the Lok Sabha.
  • The President addresses Parliament at the commencement of the first session after each general election and the first session of each year.
  • The President may send messages to either House of Parliament.
  • The President nominates 12 members to the Rajya Sabha from among persons having special knowledge or practical experience in literature, science, art and social service.
  • The President gives assent to Bills passed by Parliament, subject to constitutional provisions.
  • The President may promulgate ordinances when Parliament is not fully in session, subject to Article 123.

Assent to Bills

Under Article 111, when a Bill has been passed by both Houses of Parliament, it is presented to the President. The President may declare assent, withhold assent, or return a non-Money Bill for reconsideration.

If Parliament passes the returned Bill again, with or without amendments, and presents it to the President, the President cannot withhold assent.

A Money Bill cannot be returned for reconsideration under Article 111. The President may assent or withhold assent.

C. Ordinance-making power

Article 123 empowers the President to promulgate ordinances when both Houses of Parliament are not in session simultaneously and circumstances require immediate action.

An ordinance has the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament, but it is temporary in nature. It must be laid before both Houses of Parliament and ceases to operate six weeks after Parliament reassembles, unless it is approved earlier or withdrawn.

If the Houses reassemble on different dates, the six-week period is calculated from the later date. An ordinance may also cease earlier if both Houses pass resolutions disapproving it.

D. Financial powers

  • A Money Bill cannot be introduced in the Lok Sabha without the President's recommendation.
  • The annual financial statement, commonly called the Union Budget, is caused to be laid before Parliament by the President under Article 112.
  • Demands for grants require the President's recommendation under Article 113.
  • Certain financial Bills require the President's recommendation under Article 117.
  • The President constitutes the Finance Commission under Article 280 at the prescribed intervals or earlier when necessary.

E. Judicial powers

Article 72 gives the President the power to grant pardons, reprieves, respites or remissions of punishment, or to suspend, remit or commute sentences in specified cases.

The President's power under Article 72 applies to:

  • Cases where punishment or sentence is by a Court Martial.
  • Offences relating to matters within the executive power of the Union.
  • All cases where a sentence of death has been passed.

The President's clemency power is exercised in accordance with the constitutional framework and the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers. It is not an unrestricted personal power.

F. Diplomatic powers

  • The President represents India in formal diplomatic matters.
  • Ambassadors and High Commissioners are appointed in the name of the President.
  • Diplomatic representatives of foreign countries present their credentials to the President.
  • International treaties and agreements are concluded in the name of the Union, subject to constitutional and legal requirements.

G. Military powers

Article 53 provides that the supreme command of the Defence Forces of the Union is vested in the President. The exercise of this power is regulated by law and by the constitutional system of ministerial responsibility.

H. Emergency powers

The President has important constitutional responsibilities in relation to the three emergency provisions:

  • National Emergency under Article 352.
  • President's Rule in a State under Article 356.
  • Financial Emergency under Article 360.

These powers are subject to constitutional conditions, parliamentary approval and other safeguards. They are not independent powers exercised outside the constitutional framework.

8. Veto powers of the President

The President's veto power refers to the constitutional choices available when a Bill passed by Parliament is presented for assent. These are commonly described as absolute, suspensive and pocket vetoes.

Absolute veto

The President withholds assent to a Bill. The Bill does not become law. This is called an absolute veto.

Suspensive veto

The President returns a non-Money Bill to Parliament for reconsideration. If Parliament passes it again, with or without amendments, the President must give assent.

Pocket veto

The Constitution does not prescribe a specific time limit within which the President must act on an ordinary Bill presented for assent. The President may therefore keep the Bill pending without giving assent or returning it. This is commonly called the pocket veto.

Important limitation

The President cannot return a Money Bill for reconsideration. The President also cannot withhold assent after Parliament has passed a returned ordinary Bill again in accordance with Article 111.

9. Pardoning power: Article 72

The President's clemency power is an important constitutional safeguard. It allows the executive to provide relief in appropriate cases, taking account of circumstances that may not have been fully reflected in the judicial process.

Form Meaning
Pardon Completely removes the punishment and the legal consequences of the conviction.
Commutation Substitutes a lighter form of punishment for a heavier one.
Remission Reduces the period of the sentence without changing its character.
Respite Allows a lesser sentence in view of special circumstances, such as pregnancy or physical disability.
Reprieve Temporarily suspends the execution of a sentence, particularly a death sentence, to allow time for further consideration.

Article 72 and Article 161: An important distinction

Article 161 gives the Governor the power to grant pardons, reprieves, respites or remissions of punishment, or to suspend, remit or commute sentences in relation to matters within the State's executive power.

Unlike the President, the Governor does not have the constitutional power to pardon a death sentence. However, the Governor may suspend, remit or commute a death sentence. The President's power under Article 72 extends to all cases where a death sentence has been passed.

10. Constitutional position during a vacancy

Article 65 provides for the Vice-President to act as President or discharge the functions of the President in specified circumstances.

When a vacancy occurs in the office of President due to death, resignation, removal or otherwise, the Vice-President acts as President until a new President is elected and enters office. The election to fill the vacancy must be held within the period prescribed by Article 62.

When the President is unable to discharge functions because of absence, illness or another cause, the Vice-President discharges the President's functions until the President resumes duties.

11. Important constitutional amendments

Amendment Relevant change
11th Amendment Act, 1961 Changed the procedure for election of the Vice-President and clarified that a vacancy in the electoral college does not invalidate the presidential election.
42nd Amendment Act, 1976 Made the advice of the Council of Ministers binding on the President under Article 74.
44th Amendment Act, 1978 Allowed the President to require the Council of Ministers to reconsider its advice once; the reconsidered advice is binding.
70th Amendment Act, 1992 Included elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of Delhi and Puducherry in the presidential electoral college.

12. Important Supreme Court judgments

Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974)

The Supreme Court explained the nature of parliamentary government and held that the President and Governor are constitutional heads who ordinarily act on the aid and advice of their respective Councils of Ministers, except where the Constitution permits discretion.

U.N.R. Rao v. Indira Gandhi (1971)

The Supreme Court held that the Council of Ministers does not cease to exist merely because the Lok Sabha has been dissolved. The constitutional requirement of a Council of Ministers under Article 74 continues.

Maru Ram v. Union of India (1980)

The Supreme Court clarified that the President's clemency power under Article 72 is exercised on the advice of the Union Council of Ministers.

Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of Andhra Pradesh (2006)

The Supreme Court recognised that clemency decisions are subject to limited judicial review in cases involving arbitrariness, mala fides, irrelevant considerations or non-application of mind.

CORE UNDERSTANDING

The President is the constitutional head, not an independent political executive.

The President's office combines constitutional authority with parliamentary accountability. Executive power is formally vested in the President, but the government is politically responsible through the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers. Understanding this distinction is central to the study of the Indian parliamentary system.
