UNION EXECUTIVE · ARTICLES 74 AND 75

Union Council of Ministers The constitutional body responsible for advising the President and conducting the political executive of the Union.

The Union Council of Ministers is the central political executive institution under India's parliamentary system. It is headed by the Prime Minister and consists of ministers appointed by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister.

The Council of Ministers aids and advises the President in the exercise of constitutional functions. It is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha, which ensures that the political executive remains accountable to the elected House of Parliament.

CONSTITUTIONAL FRAMEWORK

Articles 74 and 75

Article 74 provides for a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at its head to aid and advise the President. Article 75 deals with the appointment, tenure, responsibility, oath, qualifications and size of the Council of Ministers.

The constitutional provisions establish the Council of Ministers as a collective institution. The Cabinet is its smaller and most important decision-making body.

1. Meaning and constitutional position

The Council of Ministers is the body of ministers headed by the Prime Minister. It is responsible for formulating government policy, administering Union affairs and advising the President.

The President is the formal constitutional head, but the Council of Ministers exercises political executive authority through the parliamentary system. The Council's decisions are implemented through ministries, departments and the administrative machinery of the Union Government.

Article 74: Aid and advice

Article 74(1) provides that there shall be a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at the head to aid and advise the President, who shall, in the exercise of functions, act in accordance with such advice.

The President may require the Council of Ministers to reconsider its advice, either generally or otherwise. After reconsideration, the President is bound to act in accordance with the advice tendered.

Article 74(2): Advice cannot be questioned in court

Article 74(2) provides that the question whether any advice was tendered by ministers to the President, and if so what that advice was, shall not be inquired into by any court.

This provision protects the confidentiality of ministerial advice. It does not place all government action beyond judicial review. The legality and constitutionality of executive action may still be examined by courts on appropriate grounds.

2. Appointment of ministers

Article 75(1)

The Prime Minister is appointed by the President. Other ministers are appointed by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister.

The Prime Minister therefore plays the central role in determining the composition of the Council of Ministers. The President formally appoints ministers in accordance with the constitutional arrangement.

Appointment of the Prime Minister

When a political party has a clear majority in the Lok Sabha, its leader is ordinarily appointed Prime Minister. In a hung Parliament, the President appoints the person most likely to command the confidence of the Lok Sabha.

Appointment of other ministers

The Prime Minister recommends the persons who are to be appointed as ministers. The President appoints them under Article 75. Their portfolios are allocated as part of the executive organisation of the government.

3. Composition of the Council of Ministers

The Council of Ministers consists of the Prime Minister and other ministers. The Constitution does not prescribe a fixed number of ministers, but it places a maximum limit through Article 75(1A).

91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003

The 91st Amendment inserted Article 75(1A). It provides that the total number of ministers, including the Prime Minister, in the Council of Ministers shall not exceed 15 per cent of the total strength of the Lok Sabha.

The amendment also inserted a restriction concerning the appointment of defectors as ministers. A member disqualified under the Tenth Schedule is also disqualified from being appointed as a minister for the period prescribed by Article 75(1B), subject to the constitutional provisions.

The 15 per cent ceiling is calculated with reference to the total strength of the Lok Sabha, not the number of members present or the strength of the ruling party.

4. Categories of ministers

The Constitution does not expressly classify ministers into Cabinet Ministers, Ministers of State and Deputy Ministers. These categories developed through parliamentary practice and executive organisation.

A. Cabinet Ministers

Cabinet Ministers are senior ministers who head important ministries or departments. They participate in Cabinet meetings and play a central role in major policy decisions.

B. Ministers of State

Ministers of State are generally junior to Cabinet Ministers. They may be given independent charge of a ministry or department, or may assist a Cabinet Minister in the administration of a particular ministry.

C. Deputy Ministers

Deputy Ministers are junior ministers who assist senior ministers in administrative and parliamentary work. This category has been used in different periods of Indian government.

Category General role
Cabinet Minister Heads a major ministry and participates in Cabinet decision-making.

These classifications are administrative and political conventions. They do not create separate constitutional offices.

5. The Cabinet and the Council of Ministers

The Cabinet is the smaller, senior decision-making body within the wider Council of Ministers. It consists primarily of Cabinet Ministers and is central to the formulation of government policy.

The Constitution refers specifically to the Cabinet in certain provisions, including Article 352. The expression "Cabinet" has a distinct constitutional significance in that context.

Why is the Cabinet important?

The Cabinet provides a practical mechanism for collective decision-making. Since the Council of Ministers may contain a relatively large number of ministers, the Cabinet enables senior ministers to deliberate on major issues and coordinate government policy.

6. Collective responsibility

Article 75(3)

Article 75(3) provides that the Council of Ministers shall be collectively responsible to the House of the People (Lok Sabha).

Collective responsibility is one of the central principles of parliamentary government. It ensures that the Council of Ministers functions as a unified political body and remains accountable to the Lok Sabha.

Meaning of collective responsibility

  • All ministers are collectively responsible for government policies and decisions.
  • Ministers must publicly support Cabinet decisions.
  • A minister who cannot support a major government decision is ordinarily expected to resign.
  • If the government loses the confidence of the Lok Sabha, the Council of Ministers must ordinarily resign collectively.
  • The Council of Ministers remains politically accountable to the Lok Sabha as a whole.

Relationship with the no-confidence motion

The Lok Sabha may express its lack of confidence in the government through a no-confidence motion. If such a motion is carried, the Council of Ministers is ordinarily expected to resign.

The principle is not based on the confidence of the Rajya Sabha. Although ministers may participate in the proceedings of either House, collective responsibility under Article 75(3) is specifically to the Lok Sabha.

7. Individual responsibility

Article 75(2)

Article 75(2) states that ministers hold office during the pleasure of the President.

In practice, the President ordinarily acts on the advice of the Prime Minister in matters concerning the continuation of individual ministers. The Prime Minister may recommend that a minister be removed or may ask the minister to resign.

8. Ministerial membership of Parliament

Article 75(5)

A person who is not a member of either House of Parliament may be appointed as a minister. However, if the person does not become a member of either House within six consecutive months, the person ceases to be a minister at the end of that period.

The provision allows flexibility in ministerial appointments while ensuring that ministers ultimately acquire parliamentary membership.

Can a minister belong to the Rajya Sabha?

Yes. A minister may be a member of either the Lok Sabha or the Rajya Sabha. Article 88 gives every minister the right to speak and otherwise take part in proceedings of either House, joint sittings and committees of Parliament of which the minister may be named a member.

However, Article 88 does not give a minister who is not a member of a particular House the right to vote in that House merely because the minister participates in its proceedings.

9. Size of the Council of Ministers

Article 75(1A), inserted by the 91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003, provides that the total number of ministers, including the Prime Minister, shall not exceed 15 per cent of the total strength of the Lok Sabha.

This restriction was introduced to discourage excessively large ministries and promote greater discipline in the size of the political executive.

Important point

The Constitution also provides a minimum representation requirement for certain smaller States under Article 164(1A), which governs the State Council of Ministers. That State-level provision should not be confused with the Union ceiling under Article 75(1A).

10. Disqualification under the anti-defection law

The 91st Constitutional Amendment also inserted Article 75(1B). It restricts the appointment of certain members disqualified under the Tenth Schedule as ministers.

A member disqualified under the Tenth Schedule is disqualified from being appointed as a minister for the period beginning from the date of disqualification and ending when the term of office as a member would expire, or when the person is elected to a House, as applicable under the constitutional provision.

This provision aims to prevent persons disqualified for defection from immediately entering the executive through ministerial appointment.

11. Cabinet Committees

Cabinet Committees are smaller committees constituted to assist the Cabinet in examining specialised subjects and coordinating government decisions.

They are not independently established constitutional bodies. They operate under the framework of government business rules and executive practice.

Functions

  • Examine complex policy questions.
  • Coordinate the work of different ministries.
  • Reduce the workload of the full Cabinet.
  • Provide specialised consideration of administrative and economic issues.
  • Make recommendations or take decisions within the authority assigned to them.

The Prime Minister generally chairs important Cabinet Committees, although their composition and chairmanship depend on the relevant government arrangements.

12. Resignation and dissolution of the Council of Ministers

The Council of Ministers may cease to hold office in different circumstances. These include resignation of the Prime Minister, loss of confidence in the Lok Sabha, dissolution of the Lok Sabha followed by the formation of a new government, or other constitutional circumstances.

Resignation of the Prime Minister

Since the Prime Minister heads the Council of Ministers, resignation of the Prime Minister ordinarily results in the resignation of the entire Council.

Loss of confidence

If the government loses a confidence vote or a no-confidence motion is carried, the Council is ordinarily expected to resign or otherwise act in accordance with constitutional conventions.

General election

Following a general election, the outgoing government ordinarily continues in a caretaker capacity until a new Council of Ministers is appointed and assumes office.

14. Important Supreme Court judgments

Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974)

The Supreme Court explained the constitutional role of the President and Governor as formal heads who ordinarily act on ministerial advice, subject to constitutional exceptions.

U.N.R. Rao v. Indira Gandhi (1971)

The Supreme Court held that the Council of Ministers does not cease to exist merely because the Lok Sabha has been dissolved. The constitutional requirement of a Council of Ministers under Article 74 continues.

S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)

The judgment reinforced the importance of establishing legislative majority on the floor of the House and is important for understanding the principle of parliamentary confidence.

CORE UNDERSTANDING

The Council of Ministers makes parliamentary executive government collectively accountable.

The Council of Ministers is headed by the Prime Minister, advises the President and is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. The Cabinet provides the principal forum for major policy decisions, while the wider Council provides the constitutional and political structure through which the Union Government functions.
